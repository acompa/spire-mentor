Reward and return

In supervised learning someone hands you the target. In reinforcement learning you write one number per step and the learner maximises their sum. This chapter is about that number: what it must contain, what it may leave out, when you can add hints to it without changing the answer, and what Spire Mentor's agent is actually paid for.

1. Where the target comes from

When you fit a classifier, each example carries a label and the loss says how far the prediction is from it. When you fit a Q-network, the target is \(r_t + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s_{t+1}, a')\): part of it is your own network's guess, and part of it, \(r_t\), comes from outside. Chapter 1 set up the decision process. This chapter is about that outside part, the reward, and the sum built from it, the return.

Two things make the reward different from a label. First, it grades outcomes, not actions. Nobody tells the agent which card to play; the environment only says, after the fact, how much the step was worth. Second, a person wrote it. The game of Slay the Spire 2 produces facts: you reached floor 31, you beat the act-2 boss, you have 12 HP left, you won. None of those is a reward until a designer writes a rule that turns some of them into numbers. Every later chapter optimises whatever that rule says, including its mistakes.

We use one example throughout. Spire Mentor's agent, Instinct, plays an Ironclad run at ascension 10: a climb of 46 floors across three acts, about 650 to 700 decisions long, ending in a win or a death. Its designer wrote this rule before training started:

EventReward
Win the run+1.0
Beat a boss, except the one whose defeat ends the run+0.05 each
Reach a floor not reached before+0.002 each
Die, or hit a turn or decision cap0
HP, gold, cards, relics, upgrades, elites foughtnothing

By the end of the chapter you should be able to compute any run's return in your head, say which parts of this rule are harmless hints and which change what the agent is asked to do, and say what it means that HP earns nothing.

2. The reward hypothesis and the return

Sutton and Barto put the founding assumption of the field in one sentence, which they call the reward hypothesis: all of what we mean by goals can be thought of as maximising the expected value of the cumulative sum of a scalar signal [1, §3.2]. It is a modelling choice, not a theorem. It says a goal can always be written as a number per step, added up.

Four different things in this repository are loosely called "reward", and this chapter keeps them apart:

The return from step \(t\) is the sum of the rewards from that step on, each weighted by a power of the discount \(\gamma\):

Definition 3.1 · Return
\begin{equation} G_t = \sum_{k=0}^{T-t} \gamma^{k}\, r_{t+k} \label{eq:return} \end{equation}

\(r_t\) is the reward that follows action \(a_t\), \(T\) is the last step of the episode, and \(0 \le \gamma \le 1\) is the discount. Sutton and Barto write the same reward as \(R_{t+1}\) [1, §3.3]; we index it by the action it follows.

Rewards earned before step \(t\) are not in \(G_t\). The return is a reward-to-go: what is still to come from here. Splitting off the first term gives the recursion that every value method in Chapter 4 is built on:

\begin{equation} G_t = r_t + \gamma\, G_{t+1}, \qquad G_{T+1} = 0. \label{eq:recursion} \end{equation}

The return of one run is a random variable: the deck you are offered, the enemies you meet and, during training, the actions you sample all vary. What training maximises is its expectation under the policy, the objective Chapter 1 named:

\begin{equation} J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}\!\left[ G_0 \right]. \label{eq:objective} \end{equation}

\(\tau\) is a whole trajectory from the first decision to the end, drawn by playing the policy \(\pi_\theta\) in the game. Note where the expectation sits. A reviewer once caught a Bellman equation with the expectation missing; here it matters as much. Instinct is not asked to make one run score high. It is asked to make the average over runs score high.

Tracing one run by hand

Take a run that walks onto floor 1, then floor 2 and onwards, beats the act-1 boss on floor 16, and dies on floor 18. With \(\gamma = 1\):

  • 18 new floors at 0.002 each: 0.036.
  • one boss at 0.05: 0.05.
  • no win: 0.

The return is \(G_0 = 0.086\). It does not matter whether the run died at full HP or at 1 HP, after two elites or none, with a strong deck or a weak one. Because the rule pays only for changes in three counters, the whole sum collapses to the counters' final values. Write \(F(s)\) for the highest floor reached so far, \(B(s)\) for the bosses paid so far and \(W\) for 1 on a win and 0 otherwise. The rule is

\begin{equation} r_t = R_F\,\big[F(s_{t+1}) - F(s_t)\big] + R_B\,\big[B(s_{t+1}) - B(s_t)\big] + R_W\, W\,\mathbb{1}[t = T], \label{eq:rule} \end{equation}

with \(R_F = 0.002\), \(R_B = 0.05\) and \(R_W = 1\). Summing Equation \eqref{eq:rule} over a run with \(\gamma = 1\), the brackets cancel in pairs, and since every run starts on floor 0 with no bosses beaten:

\begin{equation} G_0 = R_F\, F_T + R_B\, B_T + R_W\, W. \label{eq:return-sm} \end{equation}

\(F_T\) and \(B_T\) are the counters at the end. Taking the expectation of Equation \eqref{eq:return-sm} splits the objective into three parts you can read off a results table:

\begin{equation} J(\theta) = R_W \Pr_{\pi_\theta}(\text{win}) + R_B\, \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}[B_T] + R_F\, \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}[F_T]. \label{eq:J-decomp} \end{equation}

So "maximise the return" here means "maximise the win rate, plus small credit for how far you got". How small is a question for the Measured section.

Pause and predict: what is the largest return a losing run can have?

A loss has \(W = 0\), so it scores \(R_F F_T + R_B B_T\). The most a loss can collect is to die in the very last fight, the act-3 second boss on floor 46. By then it has beaten three bosses that pay (act 1, act 2, act-3 first boss) and reached 46 floors: \(0.002 \times 46 + 0.05 \times 3 = 0.242\). The run that wins that same fight scores 1.242. On the rare maps that run two floors longer the figures are 0.246 and 1.246; the Measured section shows both.

3. Sparse and dense reward

A reward is sparse when almost every step pays zero and the signal arrives only at rare events; it is dense when most steps carry information. A win-only rule for Slay the Spire is as sparse as it gets: one nonzero reward in about 700 steps, and only in runs that win.

Sparsity hurts in two ways, and it is worth separating them.

No signal at all. If the current policy never reaches the reward, every return is zero and there is nothing to learn from. Chapter 5 derives the policy gradient; for now it is enough to know that it weights each action's log-probability by how much better than average the return turned out, roughly \((G_0 - b)\,\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\) for some baseline \(b\). If every run returns 0, every weight is 0 and the gradient is exactly zero. Minsky called the long wandering this causes the "plateau problem" [1, §17.4]. A Q-learner has the same trouble in a different form: its targets are all \(0 + \gamma \max_{a'} Q\), so nothing anchors the values.

A weak signal far away. Even when wins happen, the one reward at step 700 has to be credited back to step 3, where a card choice made the win possible. Every step in between adds noise. Chapter 4 shows how a critic and the λ-return trade bias for variance to make this work. Dense rewards shorten the distance between a choice and some feedback about it.

Dense rewards cost something too. Each extra term is a claim by the designer about what progress looks like, and the agent will optimise the claim, not the intent behind it. Sutton and Barto put the risk plainly: rewarding a chess agent for taking pieces may teach it to take pieces and lose the game [1, §3.2]. The next section answers when you can add dense terms with no such risk.

4. Shaping, and the potential-based result

Reward shaping means adding a term \(\tilde r_t\) to the reward the task already has, to give the learner earlier hints. The name comes from Skinner's animal training, where an experimenter rewards closer and closer approximations of a behaviour [1, §14.3, §17.4]. The question is what the shaped problem's best policy has to do with the original problem's best policy.

In general nothing. Ng, Harada and Russell showed in 1999 that there is one family of shaping terms that is always safe, and essentially only one [2]. Pick any function of the state, a potential \(\Phi(s)\), and pay the discounted change in it:

Definition 3.2 · Potential-based shaping
\begin{equation} \tilde r_t = \gamma\, \Phi(s_{t+1}) - \Phi(s_t). \label{eq:pbrs} \end{equation}

\(\Phi\) can be any real-valued function of the state: distance to a goal, a heuristic score, a guess at the value. The learner sees \(r_t + \tilde r_t\) instead of \(r_t\). This is the form quoted from Ng et al. in [3].

Here is why it is safe, derived for the case this chapter needs. Sum the shaping term along one episode from step 0, discounting step \(t\) by \(\gamma^t\) as the return does:

\begin{equation} \sum_{t=0}^{T} \gamma^{t} \tilde r_t = \sum_{t=0}^{T} \Big( \gamma^{t+1} \Phi(s_{t+1}) - \gamma^{t} \Phi(s_t) \Big) = \gamma^{T+1}\, \Phi(s_{T+1}) - \Phi(s_0). \label{eq:telescope} \end{equation}

Every intermediate potential appears once with a plus sign and once with a minus sign, so the sum telescopes to the potential of the final state, discounted, minus the potential of the start. \(s_{T+1}\) is the terminal state the last action leads to. Figure 3.2 draws this.

Add Equation \eqref{eq:telescope} to the unshaped return. The shaped return from the start is

\begin{equation} G'_0 = G_0 + \gamma^{T+1}\Phi(s_{T+1}) - \Phi(s_0). \label{eq:shaped-return} \end{equation}

If the potential of every terminal state is the same constant, conventionally 0, then \(G'_0 = G_0 - \Phi(s_0)\). The start state does not depend on the policy, so every policy's expected return moves by the same amount, and the ranking of policies, and so the best one, is unchanged. The same argument from any state \(s\) instead of \(s_0\) gives the value functions of the shaped problem:

\begin{equation} V'^{\pi}(s) = V^{\pi}(s) - \Phi(s), \qquad Q'^{\pi}(s, a) = Q^{\pi}(s, a) - \Phi(s). \label{eq:qshift} \end{equation}

For a Q-learner this is the cleanest way to see it. Every action in state \(s\) has its value moved by the same \(\Phi(s)\), so \(\arg\max_a Q'(s,a) = \arg\max_a Q(s,a)\). Wiewiora proved the learning-dynamics version in 2003: a Q-learner that receives potential-based shaping makes exactly the same updates as one without shaping whose Q-table starts at \(Q_0(s,a) + \Phi(s)\) [3]. Shaping of this kind is a prior on the value function written into the reward. That is also why it helps only through learning speed: it changes where the learner starts, not where it ends.

Two conditions did the work, and both are worth stating as a test you can apply to any reward term:

  1. Form. The term must be \(\gamma\Phi(s_{t+1}) - \Phi(s_t)\) for a single function \(\Phi\) of the state. A term that depends on the action, or on anything outside the state, fails.
  2. Ending. With \(\gamma = 1\), every terminal state must have the same potential. With \(\gamma \lt 1\) and an infinite horizon, \(\gamma^{T}\Phi\) vanishes on its own; in an episodic problem the terminal potential must still be fixed.

The form condition also rules out reward for cycling. Going round a loop \(s \to s' \to s\) collects \(\Phi(s') - \Phi(s) + \Phi(s) - \Phi(s') = 0\) when \(\gamma = 1\), so no loop can be farmed for shaping reward [3].

5. Discounting and the effective horizon

Equation \eqref{eq:return} weights a reward \(k\) steps ahead by \(\gamma^k\). There are three ways to read \(\gamma\), and in practice the third matters most.

As a preference. A reward now is worth more than the same reward later, as with interest rates. Sutton and Barto introduce it this way: with \(\gamma = 0\) the agent is myopic, and as \(\gamma \to 1\) it becomes farsighted [1, §3.3].

As a chance of stopping. Suppose that after each step the episode is cut off with probability \(1 - \gamma\), independently. The step \(k\) reward is collected only if the episode survives \(k\) cuts, which happens with probability \(\gamma^k\). The expected undiscounted sum is then

\begin{equation} \mathbb{E}\Big[ \sum_{k \ge 0} \mathbb{1}[\text{alive after } k \text{ steps}]\, r_{t+k} \Big] = \sum_{k \ge 0} \gamma^{k}\, r_{t+k}. \label{eq:survival} \end{equation}

So discounting is the same as playing an undiscounted game with a hidden death state reached at rate \(1-\gamma\) per step.

As a horizon. Summing the weights tells you how far ahead the objective effectively looks:

\begin{equation} \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \gamma^{k} = \frac{1}{1-\gamma}. \label{eq:horizon} \end{equation}

This is the effective horizon: \(\gamma = 0.99\) looks about 100 steps ahead and \(\gamma = 0.999\) about 1,000. Rewards much farther than that barely register. Seen from the first decision of a run that wins at step \(T\), the win is worth

\begin{equation} \gamma^{T} R_W \approx e^{-T(1-\gamma)} R_W, \label{eq:discount-win} \end{equation}

where the approximation uses \(\log \gamma \approx -(1-\gamma)\) for \(\gamma\) near 1. With \(T = 678\), the median length of the network's won runs (Section 9), and \(\gamma = 0.99\), that is \(0.99^{678} \approx 0.0011\). An agent with that discount, at the start of a run, would value a win less than the floor reward for the next floor.

Why discount at all, then, in an episodic task that always ends? Mostly for the third reason disguised as the first: a shorter horizon means less noise in the return. A return that sums 700 random rewards has more variance than one that effectively sums 100. Value methods also converge faster when the future is shrunk. The price is bias: the agent now optimises a different objective from the one you care about. Chapter 4 shows that GAE's \(\lambda\) offers the same trade without changing the objective, which is why a system can set \(\gamma = 1\) and still control variance.

6. When the optimiser finds another way

A reward function is a proxy for what the designer wants. An optimiser pushes on the proxy until it finds the cheapest way to raise it, and the cheapest way is sometimes not the intended one. Amodei and coauthors call this reward hacking and list the ways it arises [4, §4]. Three of them apply to game agents:

  • Partially observed goals. The reward measures something the designer can see instead of the goal itself. Their example is a cleaning robot paid for not seeing messes, which learns to close its eyes.
  • Goodhart's law. A quantity that tracks the goal under normal play stops tracking it once it is optimised directly: when a metric is used as a target, it ceases to be a good metric [4, §4].
  • Complicated systems. The more ways the agent can act, the more likely some sequence exploits a gap in how the reward is computed.

Two things follow for reward design. First, every term you add is a new surface to exploit; a sparse reward that pays only for the true goal has the fewest. Second, if you add terms, prefer ones you can prove harmless (potential-based ones) and make sure the counters cannot be pumped. A floor counter that paid for every floor change, rather than for new highs, could be farmed by any mechanic that moves you back and forth. A counter on the maximum is a potential and pays nothing for a loop.

There is a subtler version that does not need a bug. Suppose a term pays for something that usually comes with winning, such as keeping HP high. If the strongest play sometimes spends HP to win more often, the term pushes the agent away from that play. The agent is not cheating. It is doing what it was paid to do, and the designer paid for the wrong thing.

7. The textbook against the engineered version

The clean statement is: reward is \(r(s_t, a_t, s_{t+1})\), one number per step; return is Equation \eqref{eq:return}; maximise \(J\). Real systems depart from it in four ways, and Instinct shows all four.

Rewards attach to stored decisions, not to every engine step. The game engine takes many steps the agent never sees: forced moves with one legal action, animations, a fight's end. Chapter 1 called these forced steps. Reward that accrues during them is held in a pending sum and credited to the most recent decision the agent did make. The total is unchanged; only the bookkeeping moves. The implementation checks that the per-decision rewards sum exactly to the run's total.

The reward is pre-registered. In research practice the reward is tuned by trial and error until the agent does what the designer wanted [1, §17.4]. That makes the final result partly a product of the designer's search. Instinct's designer fixed the rule in a pre-registration before training, together with the caps and the evaluation seeds, so it could not be adjusted after seeing results. Chapter 8 returns to why.

\(\gamma = 1\), with variance controlled elsewhere. The textbook uses \(\gamma \lt 1\) for both reasons in Section 5. Instinct keeps the objective undiscounted, so a win is worth exactly 1.0 from every decision, and handles variance with the λ of GAE, which Chapter 4 derives.

Numerical precision bounds how small a reward can be. The network trains in 16-bit bfloat for speed. Near a value of 1.0, adjacent bfloat16 numbers are 0.0039 apart, wider than the 0.002 floor reward, so a value head in bf16 could not tell "reached one more floor" from rounding. The plan keeps the value projection in 32-bit floats for exactly this reason. Small reward terms are a design constraint on the numerics, not just on the agent.

Testing the floor and boss terms against the potential-based condition

Now apply the two-part test from Section 4. Take the floor term, \(R_F[F(s_{t+1}) - F(s_t)]\), and try the potential \(\Phi_F(s) = R_F\,F(s)\) with \(\gamma = 1\):

\begin{equation} \gamma\,\Phi_F(s_{t+1}) - \Phi_F(s_t) = R_F\,F(s_{t+1}) - R_F\,F(s_t) = R_F\big[F(s_{t+1}) - F(s_t)\big]. \label{eq:floor-form} \end{equation}

The form condition holds. The floor term is exactly the change in a potential, because it pays the change in a high-water mark. The boss term passes the same way with \(\Phi_B(s) = R_B\,B(s)\). This is why neither can be farmed by looping.

The ending condition is where they fail. At the terminal state the counters keep their final values, so \(\Phi_F(s_{T+1}) = R_F F_T\), which is 0.036 for a death on floor 18 and 0.092 for a win on floor 46. The terminal potential depends on how the run went. Split the floor term into a part that passes and a part that does not. Define \(\hat\Phi_F\) equal to \(\Phi_F\) everywhere except the terminal state, where it is 0. Then for every step

\begin{equation} R_F\big[F(s_{t+1}) - F(s_t)\big] = \underbrace{\hat\Phi_F(s_{t+1}) - \hat\Phi_F(s_t)}_{\text{potential-based, safe}} \; + \; \underbrace{\mathbb{1}[t = T]\; R_F\,F_T}_{\text{terminal progress bonus}}. \label{eq:floor-split} \end{equation}

On a non-final step the indicator is 0 and \(\hat\Phi_F = \Phi_F\), so the two sides agree. On the final step the left side is \(R_F[F_T - F(s_T)]\), and the right side is \(0 - R_F F(s_T) + R_F F_T\), the same thing. By Equation \eqref{eq:telescope} the first part sums to \(-\hat\Phi_F(s_0) = 0\) over every run. All of the floor term's effect on the return comes from the second part, a bonus paid at the end in proportion to how far the run got. The same split applies to the boss term.

So the verdict: the floor and boss terms have potential-based form but violate the terminal condition. They are not policy-invariant shaping. Their net effect is a terminal reward for progress, \(R_F F_T + R_B B_T\), added to the win. That is just Equation \eqref{eq:return-sm} read differently. It is also a deliberate design: the plan calls them reward, not shaping, and the designer wanted a death on floor 40 to score above a death on floor 5.

Does the progress bonus change which policy is best? It can, and Equation \eqref{eq:J-decomp} says how much it takes. Write \(p\) for the win probability and \(m_L\) for the mean progress bonus of the runs a policy loses. Every win collects the same progress (0.242 on a standard map), so

\begin{equation} J = p\,(R_W + 0.242) + (1 - p)\, m_L. \label{eq:J-pm} \end{equation}

A policy that gives up \(\Delta p\) of win rate is preferred only if its losses collect enough extra progress to pay for it: \((1-p)\,\Delta m_L \gt \Delta p\,(R_W + 0.242 - m_L)\). With the network's measured \(p\) and \(m_L\) (below), giving up one percentage point of wins would need every loss to gain about \(0.056\) in progress, roughly one more boss or 28 more floors per lost run. The bonus can tip near-ties. It cannot buy a meaningful loss of win rate.

The contrast: version 1's shaping

An earlier version of the project's PPO agent used a different reward, and the contrast shows both halves of Section 4 in one design. Its return was built from a potential \(\Phi = \text{base} + \text{shape}\). The base paid graded progress and the win, half and half: \(0.5\,F/46 + 0.5\,W\) at ascension 10. The shape term was potential-based shaping on HP fraction, upgraded cards and relics:

9. Measured

Everything below comes from playing the first 300 seeds of the tour's panel A (seeds 170000000 to 170000299) twice: once with the published Ironclad network choosing greedily, once with uniformly random choices. Runs went through the training environment, so every reward is the one training saw. Each run passed a three-way check: the sum of its ledger, the environment's own total and Equation \eqref{eq:return-sm} applied to its floor, bosses and win all agreed to \(10^{-7}\).

Under the win-only rule, a random policy produces 300 returns of exactly zero. Under Instinct's rule the returns differ from run to run, because some random runs climb a few floors further than others, so a gradient exists. It is a weak one: a standard deviation of 0.0026 against a win worth 1.0. In practice Instinct did not start from random play. Chapter 7 covers the imitation stage that produced a policy which already wins, which is what makes a mostly sparse reward workable.

Where losses end

The largest return among the losing runs we played was 0.246. Won runs scored 1.242 (238 runs) or 1.246 (5 runs). The 1.246 runs reached floor 48. All five held the Golden Compass relic, which the project's version-1 reward code notes makes the act-2 map two rows longer, so a win on that path collects 0.004 more. The losing run that scored 0.246 also reached floor 48 and died to the final boss. The progress bonus therefore pays slightly more for a win on a long map. At this size it cannot matter to training, but it is a small example of Section 6: the rule pays for floors, and floors are not quite the same thing as progress.

What the reward does not see

Among the network's won runs, the HP left at the end ranged widely: the middle 80% finished with between 28% and 85% of their maximum HP, and the lowest with 10%. Every one of them scored the same return. The rule cannot tell a win on 5 HP from a win at full health, and so neither can the objective. If the network protects HP, it does so because HP helps it win later fights, not because HP is paid. Chapter 4 makes that concrete: the critic's value estimate, not the reward, is where HP gets a price.

The discount, on real run lengths

The network's won runs ended on row 678 at the median. By Equation \eqref{eq:discount-win}, \(\gamma = 0.99\) would make the win worth 0.0011 from the first decision, and \(\gamma = 0.999\) would make it 0.51. An effective horizon of 100 decisions is a fraction of one act. With \(\gamma = 1\) the win is worth 1.0 from every decision, and the hard job of carrying it back through 700 steps moves to the critic and λ, which is the subject of Chapter 4.

10. Common confusions

"The reward is the label." In supervised learning the label says what the right output was. The reward says how good the outcome was, after the fact, and only as a sum over many steps. A run that won still contains bad decisions; a run that died contains good ones. The policy is a classifier, but it is trained on a weighted log-likelihood whose weights come from returns (Chapter 5), not on the actions as targets.

"The return of decision \(t\) is the run's return." \(G_t\) is reward-to-go. Rewards that arrived before step \(t\) are not part of it, and with \(\gamma = 1\), \(G_t = G_0 - \sum_{k \lt t} r_k\). The floors already climbed when you make a choice on floor 20 are not credited to that choice.

"A small dense term is just shaping, so it cannot change the optimum." Only potential-based terms with a fixed terminal potential are safe. Instinct's floor term has the right form and still changes the objective, because its potential at the end depends on the run. "Small" is not the test; Equation \eqref{eq:shaped-return} is.

"γ is a property of the game." It is a choice in the objective. A Q-learning habit is to treat \(\gamma = 0.99\) as a default. In a 700-step episode that default turns a win into a rounding error at the start of the run. Instinct sets \(\gamma = 1\) and controls variance with λ instead.

"If HP earns nothing, the agent ignores HP." It ignores HP as a goal, not as a means. A Q-learner already knows this pattern: \(Q(s,a)\) can be high for an action with zero immediate reward because of what it leads to. HP is part of the state, the value of states with more HP is higher when HP helps win, and the policy learns to protect it to exactly the extent it helps.
