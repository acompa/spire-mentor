Self-test: policies, from scores to probabilities to one action

Questions on Chapter 2, Policies. Every answer is in the chapter.

Write each answer from memory before you open the answer below it. Come back in a few days and answer all of them again.

Recall questions

  1. A DQN agent and Spire Mentor's policy-gradient agent both explore. For each one, where does the randomness come from, and what decides how much of it there is?

    Answer

    The DQN plays \(\arg\max_a Q(s,a)\), so its policy is a by-product of the value estimate. Its randomness comes from outside the network. With probability \(\varepsilon\) it picks an action uniformly at random, and the engineer sets \(\varepsilon\) as a schedule. The original DQN annealed it from 1 to 0.1 over the first million frames. The policy-gradient agent explores by sampling from its own distribution \(\pi_\theta(a \mid s)\), and training sets how spread that distribution is, state by state. (Sections 1 and 8)

  2. Greedy Q-learning gives a deterministic policy. Why would you want a stochastic one? Give the chapter's reasons, and say which one matters most in practice.

    Answer

    Sutton and Barto give two reasons. When the agent cannot see the whole state, the best policy can itself be random, as with bluffing in poker. And with a smooth parameterization, a small change in \(\theta\) makes a small change in behaviour, which an argmax over noisy value estimates does not do. A third reason matters most in practice. During training, randomness is how the agent finds out what its untried actions are worth. (Section 2)

  3. A card game offers three options at one decision and eleven at the next. How does the softmax handle that, and how does the code fit a changing set into a fixed-size GPU tensor? Then explain why Spire Mentor merges identical options, such as three Strikes, before it scores them.

    Answer

    The denominator of the softmax sums only over \(\mathcal{A}(s)\), so the normalising constant is computed again for each state's legal set. In a batch, every row is padded to a fixed width and each empty slot gets the logit \(-\infty\). Because \(\exp(-\infty) = 0\), those slots get probability zero and add nothing to the denominator. Without merging, \(k\) identical copies with logit \(z\) would contribute \(k\,e^{z}\), which is the same as adding \(\log k\) to the move's logit. The chance of playing a Strike would then grow with the number of Strikes in hand, which has nothing to do with whether a Strike is a good play. (Section 3)

  4. Add 100 to every logit at one state. What happens to the policy, and why? What does that tell you about the card reward, where the network gives Unrelenting a logit of 39.5?

    Answer

    The policy does not change. Every numerator and the denominator gain the same factor \(e^{c}\), and the factors cancel. Only differences between logits matter, because the ratio of two probabilities is \(\exp\big(z_\theta(s,a) - z_\theta(s,b)\big)\). So a logit has no absolute level and is not a value estimate. The 39.5 says only that the policy prefers Unrelenting to the next option, Pommel Strike at 12.8, by a factor of about \(e^{27}\). The separate value head, which Chapter 4 introduces, is what predicts return. (Sections 3 and 12)

  5. Write the derivative of \(\log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\) with respect to the logit \(z_\theta(s,b)\). What does each term do in a gradient step, how does it relate to training a classifier, and what does it say about when learning stops?

    Answer

    The derivative is \(\mathbb{1}[a = b] - \pi_\theta(b \mid s)\). The first term comes from the action's own logit, and the second comes from differentiating the log-sum-exp. To raise \(\log \pi(a \mid s)\), the step pushes the logit of \(a\) up by \(1 - \pi(a \mid s)\) and pushes every other logit down by that action's current probability. A classifier's cross-entropy gradient with respect to its logits is the probabilities minus the one-hot label, and this is that gradient with the sign flipped. Once \(\pi(a \mid s)\) is near 1, the push on \(a\) is near zero, so learning on that action stops. (Section 3)

  6. Why does Spire Mentor score each candidate action with one shared function instead of a DQN-style output layer with one unit per action? What goes into the policy head, and where does each input come from?

    Answer

    A fixed output layer would need a unit for every action that could ever occur, and it would have to learn separately that a Strike at hand position 2 and a Strike at position 4 are the same move. One shared function \(f_\theta\big(u_\theta(s), \psi_\theta(s,a)\big)\) runs on every candidate, so the number of candidates can be anything. In Spire Mentor, a transformer trunk with no positional encoding returns one 256-number vector per token. The head reads the option token's vector \(h_a\), the state token's vector \(h_{\text{state}}\) from RUNCTX and a learned kind embedding \(e_{\text{kind}(a)}\), sums their projections inside a SiLU hidden layer and returns one logit. The term \(W_{\text{state}} h_{\text{state}}\) is the same for every option, so it acts as a state-dependent bias that lets one option score high in one state and low in another. (Sections 4 and 10)

  7. Why can raw entropy not compare a map decision with a shop decision, and what does Spire Mentor do about it? Which way does the entropy gradient push the logit of an action that is more likely than average?

    Answer

    Entropy lies between 0 and \(\log |\mathcal{A}(s)|\), so its ceiling depends on how many options are on offer. A uniform choice between two map paths has 0.69 nats, and a uniform choice among eighteen shop items has 2.89 nats. Dividing by \(\log |\mathcal{A}(s)|\) gives the normalised entropy \(\bar{\mathcal H}(s)\), which lies in \([0,1]\) for every decision, and Spire Mentor's entropy bonus uses that form per decision type. The gradient \(-\pi_\theta(b \mid s)\big(\log \pi_\theta(b \mid s) + \mathcal{H}\big)\) pushes the logit of a more-likely-than-average action down and a less-likely one up. Because of the factor \(\pi_\theta(b \mid s)\), an action with almost no probability barely moves. (Sections 5 and 9)

  8. The Gumbel-max trick connects sampling to greedy selection. What does it say, and what does its noise scale tell you about which leads flip? Then give the one-decision argument for greedy evaluation and its two gaps.

    Answer

    If you add independent standard Gumbel noise to each logit and take the argmax, the result is a draw from \(\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s)\). So a sampling agent is a greedy agent that sees its scores through noise. The noise has standard deviation \(\pi/\sqrt{6} \approx 1.3\), so a lead of 1 often flips and a lead of 10 almost never does. At one decision, the expected value of sampling is a probability-weighted average of \(Q^\pi(s,a)\), and an average cannot exceed its largest term. So if the top choice is the best action, sampling can only lose at a single decision. The first gap is that the policy's top choice may not be the best action. The second is that over hundreds of decisions the greedy agent reaches states that the sampling agent rarely visits, and training saw only the sampling agent. (Section 6)

  9. The score-function estimator lets an agent estimate the gradient of its expected payoff from its own play. Why does the learner have to sample, rather than play greedily, to use it? Give the three reasons.

    Answer

    The identity \(\nabla_\theta \pi = \pi\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi\) turns the gradient into an expectation over actions drawn from \(\pi_\theta\), so the agent averages each payoff times \(\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\). First, the expectation is over the current policy, and data from another rule, such as greedy selection, estimates a different expectation. Second, greedy data contains only one action per state, so it can never show that an untried action pays more. In off-policy terms, a greedy behaviour policy has no support on the alternatives. Third, the argmax is a step function of the logits with zero derivative almost everywhere, so gradient descent has nothing to follow, while the softmax is smooth. (Section 7)

  10. How does a softmax policy explore differently from ε-greedy? Then describe how a learned spread can fail, and the standard fix.

    Answer

    ε-greedy explores every non-greedy action equally, so at the Seapunk decision it gives a Strike and an idle end of turn the same exploration probability. A softmax gives each action randomness in proportion to \(e^{z}\), learns how much randomness to use at each state, and changes continuously. Under ε-greedy, a tiny change in two \(Q\) estimates can swap which action gets probability \(1-\varepsilon\). The failure mode is early collapse. The log-probability gradient keeps pushing the winning action toward 1, the agent stops trying anything else, and it never collects the evidence that could reverse a mediocre choice. The fix adds \(\beta\) times the expected entropy to the objective, the entropy bonus that Williams and Peng proposed and A3C made standard. (Section 8)

  11. Spire Mentor trains under bfloat16 autocast but computes the policy's final projection and its log-softmax in float32. Overflow is not the reason. What is? What precision reason does the training plan itself give?

    Answer

    Overflow cannot happen because the softmax subtracts the largest logit before it exponentiates. Rounding is the risk. Logits in this network reach magnitudes of tens, and between 32 and 64 bfloat16 values are 0.25 apart, so a logit near 40 can move by up to 0.125. Two such roundings can shift a probability ratio by a factor of up to \(e^{0.25} \approx 1.28\). The plan's own precision reason concerns the value output. It notes that bfloat16 spacing near 1 is 0.0039, larger than the smallest reward, 0.002. The record gives no separate reason for the float32 log-softmax, so the rounding argument is the chapter's inference. (Sections 9 and 10)

  12. On the first 120 seeds of panel A, greedy play won 82.5% and sampling at temperature 1 won 79.2%. What do those numbers show, which seeds carry the comparison, and which policy does the greedy number describe?

    Answer

    Sampling at temperature 1 won 95 seeds against greedy's 99, a cost of 3.3 points from about 68 departures per run, so the departures cost little. Only the discordant seeds compare the two rules. At temperature 1, greedy alone won 12 and sampling alone won 8. Seeds that both rules won or both lost say nothing about which rule is better. With 120 seeds, the 95% Wilson intervals are about ±7 points each, and the data cannot separate differences of 2 or 3 points. The greedy number belongs to the argmax policy, which is deterministic and generated none of the training data. The published weights are also an exponential moving average, so greedy evaluation measures the argmax of a smoothed policy. (Sections 9, 11 and 12)

Calculations

  1. At the Seapunk decision, potion 0 has logit 8.82 and Defend has 7.33. The other three classes have logits of −3.80 or lower, so ignore them. Compute the two probabilities, the ratio between them, the entropy in nats and the normalised entropy over the five legal classes.

    Answer
    1. Subtract the largest logit: \(8.82 - 8.82 = 0\) and \(7.33 - 8.82 = -1.49\).
    2. Exponentiate: \(e^{0} = 1\) and \(e^{-1.49} \approx 0.225\). The sum is 1.225.
    3. Divide by the sum: \[\pi(\text{potion 0}) = 1/1.225 = 0.816\] and \[\pi(\text{Defend}) = 0.225/1.225 = 0.184.\]
    4. The ratio is \(e^{1.49} \approx 4.4\), so the potion is about 4.4 times as likely as Defend.
    5. Entropy: \(-0.816 \ln 0.816 = 0.166\) and \(-0.184 \ln 0.184 = 0.311\), so \(\mathcal H = 0.477\) nats. The three ignored classes add less than 0.0001.
    6. Normalised entropy: \[\bar{\mathcal H} = 0.477 / \ln 5 = 0.477 / 1.609 = 0.30.\]

    (Sections 3 and 5)

  2. Now divide both logits by a temperature \(T = 2\). What is Defend's probability, and how does the result agree with the rule that tempering raises every probability ratio to the power \(1/T\)?

    Answer
    1. Dividing by \(T = 2\) halves the gap: \(1.49 / 2 = 0.745\).
    2. The new ratio is \(e^{0.745} \approx 2.1\). The rule gives the same number: \(4.4^{1/2} \approx 2.1\).
    3. \[\pi(\text{Defend}) \approx 1/(1 + 2.1) \approx 0.32,\] about a third, which matches Figure 2.2.
    4. The Strike class rises to about \(10^{-3}\) at this temperature, so ignoring it still barely changes the answer.

    At \(T = 1\), a sampler leaves the potion with probability 0.184. At \(T = 2\), it leaves with about 0.32. (Section 6)
