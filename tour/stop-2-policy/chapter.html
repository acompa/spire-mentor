Policies: from scores to probabilities to one action

A policy gives every legal action a probability. A Q-learner gets its actions from an argmax over estimated values, while a policy-gradient agent learns the probabilities directly. This chapter shows how a network produces those probabilities when the set of legal actions changes at every decision, and then measures what it costs Spire Mentor's agent to sample from them instead of taking its top choice.

1. A policy-gradient agent learns its action distribution directly

A policy is the rule an agent uses to choose actions. For each state, it gives every legal action a probability. Spire Mentor's agent builds its policy in three steps. A network scores each legal option, a softmax turns the scores into probabilities, and a selection rule picks one option. In training, the rule draws an option at random with those probabilities. The shipped agent always takes the most probable one.

If you have trained a deep Q-network, you already know another way to act. The network estimates \(Q(s,a)\) for each action and the agent plays \(\arg\max_a Q(s,a)\), so the policy is a by-product of the value estimate. Exploration comes from outside the network. With probability \(\varepsilon\), the agent ignores its estimates and picks an action uniformly at random. The original DQN annealed \(\varepsilon\) from 1 to 0.1 over the first million frames and held it there after that [7].

Policy-gradient methods learn the action distribution itself, moving probability toward the actions that led to good outcomes. The agent needs no value estimate to act, although Chapter 4 adds one to help training. It explores by sampling from its own distribution, and training also adjusts how spread out that distribution is.

Three things change when you move from Q-learning to a policy.

  • The output is a distribution. Section 3 shows how scores become probabilities, and why the scores are not values.
  • The set of legal actions varies. A DQN for Atari has one output unit per valid joystick action. The game fixes that number somewhere between 4 and 18, and the set is the same in every frame [7]. A card game offers three cards now, eleven plays next turn and two paths on the map after that, so the network has to score whatever is on offer. Section 4 shows how.
  • The selection rule matters. Training samples from the distribution, and evaluation usually takes the most probable action. These are two different policies, and Section 11 measures the gap between them.

The worked example throughout is Spire Mentor's end-to-end agent, which its author calls Instinct. It plays Slay the Spire 2 as the Ironclad, from the first decision of a run to the last. Chapter 1 defined what one decision is in this game, and this chapter looks at what the network outputs at each one.

2. A policy maps each state to a distribution over legal actions

A policy is written \(\pi(a \mid s)\), the probability of action \(a\) in state \(s\). When the rule has learnable parameters \(\theta\), such as the weights of a neural network, it is written \(\pi_\theta(a \mid s)\).

Definition 2.1 · Policy

A policy assigns to every state \(s\) and every legal action \(a \in \mathcal{A}(s)\) a probability, and these probabilities sum to one over the legal actions.

\begin{equation} \pi_\theta(a \mid s) \ge 0, \qquad \sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}(s)} \pi_\theta(a \mid s) = 1 \quad \text{for every } s. \label{eq:policy} \end{equation}

Here \(\mathcal{A}(s)\) is the set of actions the game allows in state \(s\). The equation says only that a policy is a conditional distribution over legal actions. In supervised-learning terms, it is a classifier whose label set depends on the input.

A deterministic policy puts all its probability on one action in every state, the special case \(\pi(a \mid s) = 1\) for a single \(a\). A stochastic policy spreads probability across several actions. Greedy Q-learning gives a deterministic policy. ε-greedy Q-learning gives a stochastic one whose randomness does not depend on how close the values are.

An agent that sees only an observation \(o_t\) can act on \(o_t\) or on its history, but not on the hidden state \(s_t\). Chapter 1 drew that distinction for Slay the Spire, where the order of the draw pile is hidden. This chapter keeps the notation of the RL literature, writing \(\pi_\theta(a \mid s)\) and reading \(s\) as what the agent sees. Nothing here depends on the Markov property.

Sutton and Barto give a reason that the best policy itself can be stochastic [1, §13.1]. When the agent cannot see the whole state, the best policy can be random. Their example is bluffing in poker, where opponents exploit a player whose bets are predictable. They also point to an advantage for learning [1, §13.2]. With a smooth parameterization, a small change in \(\theta\) makes a small change in behaviour, and an argmax over noisy value estimates does not have that property. In practice, a third reason matters most. During training, randomness is how the agent finds out what its untried actions are worth, and Section 7 makes that precise.

3. A softmax turns scores into probabilities over the legal set

One decision, from scores to probabilities

Take a real decision. On floor 1 of seed 170,000,000, the Ironclad faces a Seapunk with 48 hit points that intends to attack for 13. The hand holds three Strikes, a Defend and the curse Ascender's Bane, and the potion belt holds two potions. After identical options merge, as Chapter 1 described, the game offers five classes. The agent can play a Strike at the Seapunk, play Defend, drink potion 0, drink potion 1 or end the turn.

The network gives each class one real number, its logit, written \(z_\theta(s,a)\). The code calls these numbers logits, and Sutton and Barto call them action preferences \(h(s,a,\theta)\) [1, §13.1]. The table gives the logits for this decision.

To get probabilities, exponentiate each logit and divide by the sum. Subtracting the largest logit first keeps the exponentials from overflowing, and the next subsection shows that it does not change the result.

Definition 2.2 · Softmax policy

Given one logit per legal action, the softmax policy is

\begin{equation} \pi_\theta(a \mid s) = \frac{\exp z_\theta(s,a)}{\sum_{b \in \mathcal{A}(s)} \exp z_\theta(s,b)}, \qquad a \in \mathcal{A}(s). \label{eq:softmax} \end{equation}

The sum in the denominator runs over the legal actions of this state only, and that is how a softmax handles a variable action set. The normalising constant is computed again for each \(\mathcal{A}(s)\). Sutton and Barto write the same equation as their (13.2) and call it "soft-max in action preferences" [1, §13.1].

Only the differences between logits matter

Add the same constant \(c\) to every logit. Each numerator gains a factor \(e^{c}\), and so does the denominator. The factors cancel, so the policy does not change:

\begin{equation} \frac{\exp\big(z_\theta(s,a)+c\big)}{\sum_{b} \exp\big(z_\theta(s,b)+c\big)} = \frac{e^{c}\exp z_\theta(s,a)}{e^{c}\sum_{b}\exp z_\theta(s,b)} = \pi_\theta(a \mid s). \label{eq:shift} \end{equation}

With \(c = -\max_b z_\theta(s,b)\), Equation \eqref{eq:shift} gives the overflow-safe form that the table used. The same cancellation turns the gap between two logits into the ratio of their probabilities:

\begin{equation} \frac{\pi_\theta(a \mid s)}{\pi_\theta(b \mid s)} = \exp\big(z_\theta(s,a) - z_\theta(s,b)\big). \label{eq:ratio} \end{equation}

In the Seapunk decision, the potion's logit is 1.49 above Defend's, so the potion is \(e^{1.49} \approx 4.4\) times as likely. A gap of 2.3 would be a factor of 10. Defend's logit is 11.1 above the Strike's, a factor of about 68,000. So a logit is a log-odds score relative to the other options on offer, not an estimate of return. Section 12 comes back to this.

The log of Equation \eqref{eq:softmax} is the log-softmax, and it is the form that training uses:

\begin{equation} \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s) = z_\theta(s,a) - \log \sum_{b \in \mathcal{A}(s)} \exp z_\theta(s,b). \label{eq:logsoftmax} \end{equation}

The second term, the log-sum-exp, is a smooth maximum of the logits. So a log-probability is the action's own score minus a smooth version of the best score.

A mask lets a fixed-size tensor hold a variable set

A GPU processes a batch of decisions as a rectangular tensor. If the widest decision in the batch has 18 options, every row gets 18 slots, and a decision with 5 options has 13 empty ones. The network sets the logit of each empty slot to \(-\infty\) before the softmax. Because \(\exp(-\infty) = 0\), those slots get probability zero and add nothing to the denominator:

\begin{equation} \tilde z_\theta(s,a) = \begin{cases} z_\theta(s,a) & a \in \mathcal{A}(s) \\ -\infty & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad \operatorname{softmax}(\tilde z)_a = \pi_\theta(a \mid s). \label{eq:mask} \end{equation}

Here \(\tilde z\) is the padded, masked vector of logits. The mask turns the sum over \(\mathcal{A}(s)\) in Equation \eqref{eq:softmax} into a tensor operation.

Identical options merge into one class

The hand above holds three Strikes, and the Ironclad can play any of them. Suppose that the network scored each copy as its own action. Identical inputs give identical logits, say \(z\), so the three copies together would get this probability:

\begin{equation} \pi(\text{some Strike} \mid s) = \frac{k\, e^{z}}{k\, e^{z} + \sum_{b \notin \text{Strikes}} e^{z_b}}, \qquad k = 3. \label{eq:dup} \end{equation}

Here \(k\) is the number of identical copies. The chance of playing a Strike would grow with the number of Strikes in hand, which has nothing to do with whether a Strike is a good play. In effect, \(k\) copies add \(\log k\) to the logit of the move. Spire Mentor avoids this by merging identical options into one class before it scores them, so the softmax runs over distinct choices. That is why the table has a single Strike row, with 3 copies merged.

The gradient of a log-probability

Training needs the derivative of \(\log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\) with respect to each logit. We differentiate Equation \eqref{eq:logsoftmax} with respect to \(z_\theta(s,b)\). The first term gives 1 if \(b = a\) and 0 otherwise, and the second term gives \(\exp z_\theta(s,b) / \sum_{c}\exp z_\theta(s,c) = \pi_\theta(b \mid s)\). So

\begin{equation} \frac{\partial \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)}{\partial z_\theta(s,b)} = \mathbb{1}[a = b] - \pi_\theta(b \mid s). \label{eq:gradlogpi} \end{equation}

Here \(\mathbb{1}[a=b]\) is 1 when \(b\) is the action taken and 0 otherwise. To raise \(\log \pi(a \mid s)\), the gradient pushes the logit of \(a\) up by \(1 - \pi(a \mid s)\) and pushes every other logit down by that action's current probability. The gradient of a classifier's cross-entropy loss with respect to its logits is the predicted probabilities minus the one-hot label, and Equation \eqref{eq:gradlogpi} is that gradient with the sign flipped. Raising \(\log \pi(a \mid s)\) is the same step as lowering the cross-entropy loss for label \(a\). The equation also shows when learning stops. Once \(\pi(a \mid s)\) is near 1, the push on \(a\) is near zero. Sutton and Barto derive the same expression for the gradient bandit, in the form \(\partial \pi(x)/\partial H(a) = \pi(x)\,(\mathbb{1}[a=x] - \pi(a))\) [1, §2.8].

4. One network scores every candidate action

A network can produce Equation \eqref{eq:softmax} in two ways.

The first is the DQN way. The network maps the state to a vector with one entry per action index, and output unit 3 always means "fire". That works when the action set is small and fixed. It breaks down when an action is "play this card on that enemy", because the same card can sit anywhere in the hand, the hand changes size, and there are many possible card and target pairs. A fixed output layer would need a unit for every action that could ever occur. It would also have to learn separately that a Strike at hand position 2 and a Strike at position 4 are the same move.

The second way scores each candidate. Each legal action gets its own input vector, and one shared function reads the state and one candidate and returns a number for that candidate:

\begin{equation} z_\theta(s,a) = f_\theta\big(u_\theta(s),\, \psi_\theta(s,a)\big), \qquad a \in \mathcal{A}(s). \label{eq:scorer} \end{equation}

Here \(u_\theta(s)\) summarises the state, and \(\psi_\theta(s,a)\) describes candidate \(a\) in the context of \(s\). \(f_\theta\) is a small network that returns a scalar. Because the same \(f_\theta\) runs on every candidate, the number of candidates can be anything. Sutton and Barto's simplest instance is linear, \(h(s,a,\theta) = \theta^{\top} x(s,a)\), with a hand-built feature vector \(x(s,a)\) for each state and action [1, §13.1, eq. 13.3]. Deep RL agents for text games used the same pattern to score free-text actions [9], and pointer networks use it to choose among a variable number of input elements [10].

In supervised-learning terms, Equation \eqref{eq:scorer} is a ranking model. A recommender scores the candidate items for one user and takes a softmax over them. Here the game state plays the user and the legal moves are the items.

Spire Mentor's network has this shape, with an attention trunk that computes both \(u\) and \(\psi\). Figures 2.1a, 2.1b and 2.1c build it up on the Seapunk decision one stage at a time, starting with the trunk.

The trunk's output for an option token is that option's vector \(\psi\), and its output for the state token is the state vector \(u\). The second stage adds a head that scores each option from those two vectors.

The head in Figure 2.1b is the combat head. The network has two heads with the same architecture and separate weights, a combat head and an out-of-combat head. Both read the same trunk, and the type of the decision selects which one scores it:

\begin{equation} z_\theta(s,a) = \begin{cases} f^{\text{combat}}_\theta\big(h_a, h_{\text{state}}, e_{\text{kind}(a)}\big) & s \text{ is inside a fight} \\ f^{\text{ooc}}_\theta\big(h_a, h_{\text{state}}, e_{\text{kind}(a)}\big) & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases} \label{eq:heads} \end{equation}

Here \(h_a\) is the trunk output for the token of option \(a\), and \(h_{\text{state}}\) is the trunk output for the state token. \(e_{\text{kind}(a)}\) is a learned embedding of the kind of action that \(a\) is, such as playing a card, drinking a potion or moving on the map. Each head is a network with one hidden layer:

\begin{equation} f_\theta(h_a, h_{\text{state}}, e) = w^{\top} \operatorname{SiLU}\!\big(W_{\text{opt}}\, h_a + W_{\text{state}}\, h_{\text{state}} + W_{\text{kind}}\, e + b\big) + b_{\text{out}}. \label{eq:head} \end{equation}

The matrices \(W\) map vectors of 256 numbers to a hidden layer of 256 units, and SiLU is the activation \(x \cdot \sigma(x)\). The vector \(w\) and the scalar \(b_{\text{out}}\) project the hidden layer down to one number. The term \(W_{\text{state}} h_{\text{state}}\) is the same for every option. It acts as a state-dependent bias inside the nonlinearity, which lets the same option score high in one state and low in another.

The third stage applies Equation \eqref{eq:softmax} to the five logits.

The network computes every score before any option is chosen, and it does not do the choosing. A separate selection rule does, and Section 6 compares the two standard rules.

5. Entropy measures how spread a policy is

The entropy of a policy at a state is a single number for how spread out the policy is. Low entropy means that the agent is sure of one option, and high entropy means that it is split between several. The measure comes from information theory [11].

Definition 2.3 · Entropy of a policy at a state
\begin{equation} \mathcal{H}[\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s)] = -\sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}(s)} \pi_\theta(a \mid s) \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s) = \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s)}\big[-\log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\big]. \label{eq:entropy} \end{equation}

The sum runs over the legal actions. The logarithm is natural, so entropy is measured in nats, and a term with \(\pi = 0\) contributes zero by the limit \(p \log p \to 0\). The second form says what entropy means. It is the expected surprise, \(-\log \pi\), of the action that the policy draws, so a policy that is certain is never surprised.

In the Seapunk decision, the potion contributes \(-0.816 \ln 0.816 = 0.166\) nats and Defend contributes \(-0.184 \ln 0.184 = 0.311\) nats, for a total of \(\mathcal H = 0.477\) nats. The three unlikely classes add less than 0.0001.

Entropy has fixed limits. It is zero exactly when one action has probability 1, and it is largest when all \(n = |\mathcal{A}(s)|\) actions are equally likely. Then every term is \(-\tfrac{1}{n}\log\tfrac{1}{n}\), and the sum is \(\log n\):

\begin{equation} 0 \;\le\; \mathcal{H}[\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s)] \;\le\; \log |\mathcal{A}(s)|. \label{eq:bounds} \end{equation}

The upper bound depends on how many actions are on offer. A uniform choice between two map paths has entropy \(\log 2 = 0.69\) nats, and a uniform choice among eighteen shop items has \(\log 18 = 2.89\) nats. So raw entropy cannot compare a map decision with a shop decision. Dividing by the bound gives the normalised entropy, which lies in \([0,1]\) for every decision:

\begin{equation} \bar{\mathcal{H}}(s) = \frac{\mathcal{H}[\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s)]}{\log |\mathcal{A}(s)|}, \qquad |\mathcal{A}(s)| \ge 2. \label{eq:normH} \end{equation}

The Seapunk decision has \(\bar{\mathcal H} = 0.477 / \log 5 = 0.30\). Spire Mentor's entropy bonus uses this normalised form, per decision type, and Section 9 describes the bonus.

For a softmax policy, entropy has a closed form in the logits. We substitute Equation \eqref{eq:logsoftmax} into Equation \eqref{eq:entropy}:

\begin{equation} \mathcal{H}[\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s)] = \log \sum_{b \in \mathcal{A}(s)} \exp z_\theta(s,b) \;-\; \sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}(s)} \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\, z_\theta(s,a). \label{eq:Hlogits} \end{equation}

Entropy is the gap between the smooth maximum of the logits and their probability-weighted average. When one logit is far above the rest, both terms approach that logit and the gap closes to zero.

Chapter 5 adds a multiple of entropy to the training objective, so you need to know which way entropy pushes each logit. We differentiate Equation \eqref{eq:entropy} with the help of Equation \eqref{eq:gradlogpi}:

\begin{equation} \frac{\partial\, \mathcal{H}[\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s)]}{\partial z_\theta(s,b)} = -\,\pi_\theta(b \mid s)\,\Big(\log \pi_\theta(b \mid s) + \mathcal{H}[\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s)]\Big). \label{eq:gradH} \end{equation}

The bracket compares the log-probability of action \(b\) with the average log-probability, \(-\mathcal{H}\). If \(b\) is more likely than average, the bracket is positive and the gradient pushes its logit down. If it is less likely than average, the gradient pushes its logit up. Because of the factor \(\pi(b \mid s)\), an action with almost no probability barely moves. At the Seapunk decision, a step that raises entropy lowers the potion's logit by 0.22 per unit step, raises Defend's by 0.22 and leaves the three unlikely classes almost unchanged.

6. Temperature moves a policy between sampling and greedy selection

To act, the agent needs a rule that turns the distribution into one action. There are two standard rules, and a setting called the temperature moves the policy between them.

Definition 2.4 · Sampled and greedy selection

Sampled selection draws \(a_t \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s_t)\). For a categorical distribution, the rule picks action \(a\) with probability \(\pi_\theta(a \mid s_t)\) [8]. Greedy selection takes the most probable action,

\begin{equation} a_t^{\text{greedy}} = \arg\max_{a \in \mathcal{A}(s_t)} \pi_\theta(a \mid s_t) = \arg\max_{a \in \mathcal{A}(s_t)} z_\theta(s_t, a). \label{eq:greedy} \end{equation}

The two argmaxes are equal because the softmax increases with each logit. Greedy selection is itself a policy, the deterministic one that puts probability 1 on \(a^{\text{greedy}}\). So one network defines two agents, one stochastic and one deterministic, and they can win at different rates.

At each decision, greedy selection throws away the probability of every other action, \(1 - \max_a \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\). At the Seapunk decision that probability is 0.184, the chance that a sampling agent plays Defend instead of drinking the potion.

Temperature scales every logit gap

Divide every logit by a positive number \(T\), the temperature, before the softmax. In this chapter, \(T\) means the temperature, not the horizon from the tour's notation table. The softmax becomes:

\begin{equation} \pi_{\theta,T}(a \mid s) = \frac{\exp\big(z_\theta(s,a)/T\big)}{\sum_{b \in \mathcal{A}(s)} \exp\big(z_\theta(s,b)/T\big)}. \label{eq:temp} \end{equation}

At \(T = 1\), Equation \eqref{eq:temp} is the trained policy. Dividing by \(T\) multiplies every logit gap by \(1/T\), so by Equation \eqref{eq:ratio} every probability ratio is raised to the power \(1/T\). As \(T \to 0\), the gap to the top action becomes infinite and the policy becomes greedy. As \(T \to \infty\), all gaps shrink to zero and the policy becomes uniform over \(\mathcal{A}(s)\):

\begin{equation} \lim_{T \to 0^+} \pi_{\theta,T}(a \mid s) = \mathbb{1}\big[a = a^{\text{greedy}}\big], \qquad \lim_{T \to \infty} \pi_{\theta,T}(a \mid s) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}(s)|}. \label{eq:limits} \end{equation}

The first limit assumes a single top logit. If several logits tie for the top, the probability splits evenly among them. Between the limits, entropy rises with temperature. For the derivation, we write \(\kappa = 1/T\). From Equation \eqref{eq:Hlogits} with logits \(\kappa z\), we can show that \(d\mathcal{H}/d\kappa = -\kappa \operatorname{Var}_{\pi_{\theta,T}}[z]\), and the chain rule then gives

\begin{equation} \frac{d}{dT}\,\mathcal{H}[\pi_{\theta,T}(\cdot \mid s)] = \frac{1}{T^{3}}\, \operatorname{Var}_{a \sim \pi_{\theta,T}(\cdot \mid s)}\big[z_\theta(s,a)\big] \;\ge\; 0. \label{eq:dHdT} \end{equation}

The variance is that of the logits under the tempered policy. At any positive temperature, every legal action keeps some probability, so the variance is zero only when all legal logits are equal, and then the policy is uniform. Entropy therefore never decreases as temperature rises. Figure 2.2 shows what temperature does to the Seapunk decision.

Sampling is greedy selection on noisy logits

The Gumbel-max trick connects the two rules [12]. Draw one independent noise value \(g_a\) for each legal action from the standard Gumbel distribution, whose distribution function is \(F(g) = \exp(-e^{-g})\). Then take the argmax of the noisy logits:

\begin{equation} \arg\max_{a \in \mathcal{A}(s)} \big(z_\theta(s,a) + g_a\big) \;\sim\; \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s), \qquad g_a \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \operatorname{Gumbel}(0,1). \label{eq:gumbel} \end{equation}

We prove it as follows. Action \(a\) wins if \(z_a + g_a > z_b + g_b\) for every \(b \ne a\). We condition on \(g_a = g\), and each such event then has probability \(F(z_a - z_b + g) = \exp(-e^{-g} e^{z_b - z_a})\). We multiply over \(b \ne a\) and integrate over \(g\) against the Gumbel density \(e^{-g}\exp(-e^{-g})\), which gives \(\int e^{-g}\exp\!\big(-e^{-g} \sum_b e^{z_b - z_a}\big)\,dg\). That integral equals \(1 / \sum_b e^{z_b - z_a} = \pi(a \mid s)\).

So a sampling agent is a greedy agent that sees its scores through noise. The noise has standard deviation \(\pi/\sqrt{6} \approx 1.3\), where \(\pi\) is the circle constant. A lead of 1 often flips, and a lead of 10 almost never does.

At one decision, sampling cannot beat the best action

Suppose that you knew the true action values at a state, \(Q^\pi(s,a)\), which Chapter 4 defines. The expected value of acting by sampling is a probability-weighted average of those values, and an average never exceeds its largest term:

\begin{equation} \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s)}\big[Q^\pi(s,a)\big] = \sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}(s)} \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\, Q^\pi(s,a) \;\le\; \max_{a \in \mathcal{A}(s)} Q^\pi(s,a). \label{eq:onestep} \end{equation}

If the top choice of the policy is also the best action, sampling can only lose at a single decision. That inequality is the argument for greedy evaluation, and the argument has two gaps. The top choice may not be the best action. And over hundreds of decisions, the greedy agent reaches states that the sampling agent rarely visits, while training saw only the sampling agent. Section 11 measures the net effect.

7. Training must sample to estimate its gradient

Consider one decision on its own, a bandit. The agent picks \(a\) from \(\mathcal{A}(s)\), receives a random payoff with mean \(q(a)\), and the episode ends. The objective is the expected payoff, \(J(\theta) = \sum_a \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\, q(a)\). Training needs the gradient of \(J\), but the agent does not know \(q\). It sees payoffs only for the actions it tries.

We apply the log-derivative identity, \(\nabla_\theta \pi = \pi\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi\):

\begin{equation} \nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}(s)} q(a)\, \nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(a \mid s) = \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s)}\Big[q(a)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\Big]. \label{eq:score} \end{equation}

The left side needs \(q\) for every action. The right side is an expectation over actions drawn from the current policy, so the agent can estimate it from its own play. It draws actions from \(\pi_\theta\), observes their payoffs, and averages each payoff times \(\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\). This is the score-function or likelihood-ratio estimator, which Williams introduced to reinforcement learning as REINFORCE [2]. Chapter 5 extends it from one decision to a whole trajectory, which gives the policy gradient theorem [3].

Take one sample \(a\) with payoff \(R\), and subtract a baseline \(\bar R\) from the payoff. With Equation \eqref{eq:gradlogpi}, a gradient step on the logits is

\begin{equation} z(s,b) \leftarrow z(s,b) + \alpha\,(R - \bar R)\,\big(\mathbb{1}[b = a] - \pi_\theta(b \mid s)\big) \quad \text{for all } b \in \mathcal{A}(s). \label{eq:bandit} \end{equation}

Here \(\alpha\) is a step size and \(\bar R\) is a running average of payoffs. This is the gradient bandit algorithm, Sutton and Barto's Equation (2.12), with \(z\) in place of their preferences \(H\) [1, §2.8]. When the payoff beats the average, the logit of the sampled action goes up and the other logits go down in proportion to their probabilities. The baseline does not change the expected step, and Chapter 5 explains why it helps anyway.

Equation \eqref{eq:score} is also a statement about data, and it gives three reasons that the learner has to sample.

  • The expectation is over the current policy. An unbiased estimate needs actions drawn from \(\pi_\theta\) itself, and data from another rule, such as greedy selection, estimates a different expectation. The tour writes the rule that chose the actions as \(\mu\), the behaviour policy, and here \(\mu\) is a policy, not a mean. Chapter 6 shows how importance weights correct for the mismatch, and why the correction adds variance.
  • Greedy data has no information about the other actions. If the agent always plays \(a^{\text{greedy}}\), every sample in Equation \eqref{eq:score} is that one action. The estimator can raise or lower the logit of that action, but it can never discover that an untried action pays more. In the terms of off-policy evaluation, a greedy behaviour policy has no support on the alternatives.
  • An argmax has no useful gradient. The greedy action is a step function of the logits, with zero derivative almost everywhere, so gradient descent has nothing to follow. The softmax is a smooth replacement for the argmax, and its gradient is useful everywhere.

The Ironclad network plays about 660 decisions per run. Over 120 runs at temperature 1, the sampled class differed from the greedy class at 10.6% of decisions. At more than half of all decisions the top class has a probability above 0.99, so most draws agree with greedy selection, and a minority of split decisions produce nearly all the departures. Over a run, those add up to tens of off-greedy actions. Section 11 shows how early the runs diverge.

8. A softmax policy learns where to explore, and ε-greedy does not

Value-based agents explore with ε-greedy selection [1, §2.2]. Written as a policy over the legal actions, ε-greedy is

\begin{equation} \pi_{\varepsilon}(a \mid s) = (1 - \varepsilon)\, \mathbb{1}\Big[a = \arg\max_{b \in \mathcal{A}(s)} Q(s,b)\Big] + \frac{\varepsilon}{|\mathcal{A}(s)|}. \label{eq:egreedy} \end{equation}

Here \(Q\) is the agent's current value estimate and \(\varepsilon\) is a fixed or annealed exploration rate. This \(\varepsilon\) is the ε-greedy rate, not the PPO clip range of Chapter 5. A softmax policy differs from ε-greedy in three ways.

  • A softmax spends its randomness on plausible actions. ε-greedy explores every non-greedy action equally. At the Seapunk decision it gives a Strike and an idle end of turn the same exploration probability, although the Strike is plausible and ending the turn wastes it. A softmax policy gives each action randomness in proportion to \(e^{z}\), so it tries plausible alternatives far more often than poor ones.
  • A softmax learns how much randomness to use at each state. In ε-greedy, \(\varepsilon\) is a schedule that the engineer chooses, and it is the same whether two actions are close or one is clearly right. In a softmax policy, training learns the spread state by state. The spread stays wide where the evidence is mixed and narrows where the evidence is clear, because Equation \eqref{eq:gradlogpi} drives probabilities toward 0 or 1 wherever one action keeps winning.
  • A softmax changes continuously. In ε-greedy, an arbitrarily small change in two \(Q\) estimates can swap which action gets probability \(1-\varepsilon\). Sutton and Barto give this discontinuity as the main reason that policy-gradient methods have stronger convergence guarantees [1, §13.2].

There is also a softmax over values, the Boltzmann rule \(\pi(a \mid s) \propto \exp\big(Q(s,a)/T\big)\). It shares the first advantage but not the second. The \(Q\) estimates converge to fixed true values whose gaps the game sets, so the value gaps and the chosen \(T\) set the spread of the policy, and learning does not [1, §13.1].

The learned spread has a failure mode. Equation \eqref{eq:gradlogpi} keeps pushing the probability of the winning action toward 1. Once that probability is near 1, the agent almost never tries anything else, so it never collects the evidence that could reverse the choice. A policy can collapse early onto a mediocre deterministic choice this way. The standard remedy adds the entropy of the policy to the objective with a small weight \(\beta\), so that the gradient in Equation \eqref{eq:gradH} resists the collapse:

\begin{equation} J_\beta(\theta) = J(\theta) + \beta\; \mathbb{E}_{s}\Big[\mathcal{H}\big[\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s)\big]\Big]. \label{eq:entbonus} \end{equation}

The expectation is over the states that the policy visits. Williams and Peng proposed this entropy bonus in 1991 [4], and A3C made it standard in deep RL. The A3C authors reported that the bonus "improved exploration by discouraging premature convergence to suboptimal deterministic policies" [5]. Chapter 5 places the bonus inside the PPO loss.

Taken to its limit, the idea becomes maximum-entropy RL. Soft actor-critic maximises reward plus \(\alpha\) times entropy at every step [6], so entropy is part of the objective rather than a regulariser. Its policy-improvement step moves the policy toward a Boltzmann distribution over a soft action value, \(\pi_{\text{new}}(\cdot \mid s) \propto \exp\big(Q^{\pi_{\text{old}}}(s,\cdot)\big)\), with the temperature folded into the reward scale. This connects the Q-learning view of Section 1 to the policy view. In maximum-entropy RL, a softmax over action values is the optimal policy for an objective that pays for staying uncertain, rather than an exploration heuristic added to a greedy learner.

9. Production code departs from the textbook form in six ways

The textbook form is Equations \eqref{eq:softmax} and \eqref{eq:score}. A network produces logits, a softmax turns them into probabilities, training samples actions, and evaluation takes the greedy action. Spire Mentor departs from that form in six ways, each for a reason.

  • Padding and masking. Each batch is padded to a fixed width, with \(-\infty\) logits in the empty slots as in Equation \eqref{eq:mask}. Spire Mentor pads to one of a few widths, such as 8, 16, 32 or 64 options, so the compiled GPU graph sees few distinct shapes.
  • Numerical precision. The training plan runs the network under bfloat16 autocast for speed, but keeps the weights, the optimizer, the log-softmax and the value projection in float32. The policy's final projection to logits also runs in float32. Logits in this network reach magnitudes of tens, and the card reward in Section 10 has a top logit of 39.5 and a gap of 27 to the next class. Overflow is not the risk, because the softmax subtracts the largest logit before it exponentiates. Rounding is. Between 32 and 64, bfloat16 values are 0.25 apart, so a logit near 40 can move by up to 0.125, and two such roundings can shift a probability ratio by a factor of up to \(e^{0.25} \approx 1.28\). The Rust inference port computes in float32 and reports probabilities in float64.
  • Ties. Equation \eqref{eq:greedy} needs a rule for equal logits. Spire Mentor takes the first maximum, as PyTorch's argmax does, so the greedy agent is fully deterministic for a given state.
  • The behaviour policy is not always the student. In parts of training, teacher networks choose some actions instead of \(\pi_\theta\). The learner records the log-probability under whichever rule acted, the behaviour policy \(\mu\), and Chapter 6 shows how the update corrects for \(\mu \ne \pi_\theta\).
  • Entropy per decision type. A single entropy weight would treat a two-way choice at a rest site the same as an eighteen-way choice in a shop. Spire Mentor uses normalised entropy, Equation \eqref{eq:normH}, with separate weights for fights and for decisions between fights, and a schedule lowers both weights over training.
  • The evaluated policy is an average of policies. The published network's weights are an exponential moving average of the training weights over roughly the last 131 million decisions. A greedy evaluation of this network measures the argmax of a smoothed policy, and that smoothed policy generated none of the training data.

10. How Spire Mentor builds its policy, and why

In Spire Mentor · the policy network

Input. Every decision becomes a set of tokens. Token 0 is the state token, which the code calls RUNCTX, and summary tokens follow for the draw pile, the discard pile, the exhaust pile, the deck, the relics and the act's boss. A fight decision adds tokens for the enemies, the cards and the fight context, plus one token per legal action. When several actions merge into one class, the policy sees only the token of the first member. A decision between fights adds tokens for the deck, the relics, the potions and the offer, plus one option token per class. The Seapunk decision has 28 tokens, and the card reward after it has 19.

Trunk. The trunk is a transformer with no positional encoding, so the order of the tokens carries no meaning. The published Ironclad network has 4 blocks of width 256 with 4 attention heads, and 6.1 million parameters including the value module. The code's default is 6 blocks.

Heads. Two policy heads have the form of Equation \eqref{eq:head}, each with a hidden layer of 256 units. One scores fights, and the other scores every other decision.

Output. The network returns one logit per merged class, with \(-\infty\) in the padded slots, and then applies a log-softmax in float32. In the code, \(z_\theta(s,a)\) is logits and \(\log\pi_\theta(a \mid s)\) is logp, and the index cls_pos locates the option token of each class.

The card reward after the Seapunk fight offers Unrelenting, Pommel Strike, True Grit or a skip, and the out-of-combat head scores them 39.5, 12.8, −15.9 and −7.3. The gap of 27 between the top two leaves \(2 \times 10^{-12}\) of probability off Unrelenting, and the entropy of the decision rounds to zero.

Training. The learner draws actions from the behaviour policy's probabilities with torch.multinomial. The entropy bonus of Equation \eqref{eq:entbonus} uses normalised entropy per decision type. Its weight is zero while the teacher term is strong, and then it decays from 0.01 to 0.002 in fights and from 0.0125 to 0.006 between fights. If the raw entropy of a decision type stays below 0.1 nats for 20 iterations, that type's gate opens and its weight is multiplied by 1.5. This boost can happen at most twice per type.

Evaluation. Every reported win rate uses greedy selection, which the plan calls "greedy argmax over merged options".

The design record explains some choices and only describes others

The record of this design is the training plan, docs/plans/2026-11-02-ironclad-e2e-ppo-v0111.md, and the campaign log of the run. The commits that change the network are sync commits whose messages list one-line subjects and give no design reasons. The list below says where the record is silent. A statement marked "Inference" is this chapter's reading, not the author's.

  • Candidate scoring and greedy evaluation follow a published recipe. The plan says that the design "replicates AlphaDefect's recipe class: entity transformer, PPO, greedy play". It gives no separate reason for scoring each candidate instead of using a fixed output layer, or for greedy evaluation. Inference: a fixed output layer fits a changing set of options badly, as Section 4 showed, and greedy play gives the deterministic, paired results on fixed seeds that the plan requires.
  • One trunk feeds both heads so that all decisions train together. In the plan's words, "One trunk feeds both the combat and the out-of-combat (OOC) heads, so run and combat decisions train together". The plan does not say why the heads are separate. Inference: fights and decisions between fights differ enough to need separate weights.
  • The plan chose the merge key with care but does not say why it merges. It rejected a coarser key that "would merge enchanted with plain Strikes". Inference: the reason to merge is Equation \eqref{eq:dup}, since duplicate copies inflate the probability of a move.
  • Normalised entropy keeps the bonus in a fixed range. The plan normalises entropy by the log of the class count, into \([0,1]\), and one of its unit tests requires entropy in that range. The record gives no reason for the zero weight while the teacher term is strong, for the decay endpoints or for the collapse thresholds. Inference: the teacher term already keeps the policy spread early in training, and normalisation makes one weight comparable across decisions with different option counts.
  • The plan's precision reason concerns the value output. The plan notes that bfloat16 spacing near 1 is 0.0039, which is larger than the smallest reward, 0.002. The record gives no separate reason for the float32 log-softmax or for float64 in the Rust port. Inference: bfloat16 would round logits of magnitude tens to steps of 0.25 and distort the probability ratios, as Section 9 showed.
  • A throughput benchmark chose 4 blocks. The plan's default was 6 blocks of width 256, the size of AlphaDefect's network, and its rule kept 6 blocks only if training updates on 3 GPUs reached 12,000 decisions per second. The 6-block network reached 7,916, so the run used 4 blocks. End to end, the two sizes ran at nearly the same rate, about 3,200 and 3,050 decisions per second.

A policy can stay uncertain for two reasons. The options may be close, as when the agent chooses a potion to discard, and then a spread policy costs little. Or the network may not have learned enough about a rare decision to settle it. The loss cannot tell the two cases apart.

A sampling run leaves the greedy path within a few dozen decisions

A sampling run copies the greedy run until its first departure. At each decision on the greedy path, a sampler at temperature 1 stays on the path with probability \(\max_a \pi_\theta(a \mid s_t)\). So its chance of matching greedy for the first \(k\) decisions is the product of those probabilities, and its expected number of departures is roughly the sum of their complements:

\begin{equation} \Pr\big[\text{no departure in decisions } 0,\dots,k-1\big] = \prod_{t=0}^{k-1} \max_{a} \pi_\theta(a \mid s_t), \qquad \mathbb{E}[\#\text{departures}] \approx \sum_{t} \Big(1 - \max_a \pi_\theta(a \mid s_t)\Big). \label{eq:survival} \end{equation}

Here \(s_t\) are the states of the greedy run, and the sum runs over all of its decisions. The product is exact, because the sampler stays in the greedy run's states until it first departs. The sum is an approximation, because after a departure the sampler visits states that the greedy run never reached.

After the first few dozen decisions, a sampled run and a greedy run of the same seed meet different fights, rewards and shops. So the paired comparison below compares two policies on the same seeds, not the same trajectories.

12. Common confusions

"A logit is a value estimate." A Q-value predicts return, and its absolute level means something. A logit has no absolute level. Add 100 to every logit at a state and the policy does not change, by Equation \eqref{eq:shift}. The card reward in Section 10 gives its favourite card a logit of 39.5, but the card is not worth 39.5 of anything. The logit says that the policy prefers that card to the next option by a factor of about \(e^{27}\). The separate value head, which Chapter 4 introduces, is what predicts return.

"A top-class probability of 0.999 means the network is 99.9% sure it is right." A policy probability is not a calibrated probability of being correct. Training drives the probability of an action that keeps winning toward 1, even when a slightly better action exists that the policy stopped trying. Near-certainty in Figure 2.3 means that the policy has stopped exploring at that decision, not that its choice is optimal.

"Evaluating greedily measures the policy we trained." Greedy evaluation measures a different, deterministic policy built from the same network. The trained policy is the stochastic one, and the training data came from sampling it or from teachers. Section 11 shows the two policies diverging within the first few dozen decisions of a run. Greedy selection is a reasonable way to deploy and to compare checkpoints, but the number it produces belongs to the argmax policy.

"Sampling from the policy is just ε-greedy with a learned ε." The two spend their randomness differently. ε-greedy departs from its top choice at a rate set by \(\varepsilon\) and the number of legal actions, whatever the evidence at that state, and it spreads the departures evenly over all other actions. A softmax policy almost never departs at settled decisions and often departs at split ones. Inference: it departs mostly toward the runner-up. That would explain why a sampler that departs at about one decision in ten still wins nearly as often. The runs did not record the rank of each departure.

"More entropy is always better for exploration." Entropy helps during training at states where the right answer is not yet known. Where the options are equivalent, high entropy is correct and costs nothing. Where one option is clearly best, high entropy is noise. That is why the entropy bonus is small and scheduled to shrink.
