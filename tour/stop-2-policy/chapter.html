Policies: scores, softmax, and what greedy play throws away

A Q-learner acts by taking an argmax over estimated values. A policy-gradient agent instead keeps a probability for every legal action and learns those probabilities directly. This chapter builds that object from scratch, shows how a network produces it when the set of legal actions changes from one moment to the next, and measures what happens when Spire Mentor's agent samples from it rather than taking its top choice.

1. From argmax to a distribution

If you have trained a deep Q-network, you already have a way to act. The network estimates \(Q(s,a)\) for each action, and the agent plays \(\arg\max_a Q(s,a)\). The policy is a by-product of the value estimate. During training you add exploration from outside: with probability \(\varepsilon\) the agent ignores its estimates and picks a uniformly random action. The original DQN annealed \(\varepsilon\) from 1 to 0.1 over the first million frames and then held it there [7].

Policy-gradient methods turn this around. The object being learned is the action distribution itself. The network outputs a score for every legal action, a softmax turns the scores into probabilities, and training nudges those probabilities toward actions that led to good outcomes. No value estimate is needed to choose an action, though Chapter 4 adds one to help training. Exploration is no longer bolted on: the agent explores because it samples from its own distribution, and the distribution's spread is itself something training adjusts.

Three things change when you move to this setting, and this chapter takes them in order.

  • The output is a distribution, not a number per action. We need to say exactly how scores become probabilities, what the scores mean, and what they do not mean (they are not values).
  • The set of legal actions varies. A DQN for Atari has one output unit per valid joystick action, between 4 and 18 depending on the game, and the same set every frame [7]. A card game offers three cards now, eleven plays next turn and a map with two paths after that. The network must score whatever is on offer.
  • How you pick from the distribution matters. Training samples from it. Evaluation usually takes the most probable action. These are different policies, and the gap between them is measurable.

The worked example throughout is Spire Mentor's end-to-end agent, which its author calls Instinct. It plays Slay the Spire 2 as the Ironclad from the first decision of a run to the last. Chapter 1 defined what one decision is in this game; here we look at what the network says at each one.

2. What a policy is

A policy is a rule that maps each state to a probability distribution over the actions legal in that state. We write it \(\pi(a \mid s)\). When the rule has learnable parameters \(\theta\) (here, the weights of a neural network) we write \(\pi_\theta(a \mid s)\).

Definition 2.1 · Policy

A policy assigns to every state \(s\) and every legal action \(a \in \mathcal{A}(s)\) a probability, and these probabilities sum to one over the legal actions.

\begin{equation} \pi_\theta(a \mid s) \ge 0, \qquad \sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}(s)} \pi_\theta(a \mid s) = 1 \quad \text{for every } s. \label{eq:policy} \end{equation}

Here \(\mathcal{A}(s)\) is the set of actions the game allows in state \(s\), and \(\theta\) is the parameter vector. The equation says nothing more than "a policy is a conditional distribution over legal actions". In a supervised-learning frame, it is a classifier whose label set depends on the input.

A deterministic policy puts all its probability on one action in every state. It is the special case \(\pi(a \mid s) = 1\) for a single \(a\). A stochastic policy spreads probability across several actions. Greedy Q-learning yields a deterministic policy; ε-greedy Q-learning yields a stochastic one whose randomness does not depend on how close the values are.

Strictly, an agent that sees only an observation \(o_t\) acts on \(o_t\) (or on its history), not on the hidden state \(s_t\). Chapter 1 drew that distinction for Slay the Spire, where the draw pile's order is hidden. To keep the notation of the RL literature we write \(\pi_\theta(a \mid s)\) and read \(s\) as "what the agent sees". Nothing in this chapter depends on the Markov property.

Why would we ever want a stochastic policy at the end of training? Sutton and Barto give two reasons [1, §13.1]. First, when the agent cannot see the whole state, the best policy can be genuinely random. Their example is bluffing in poker: a player whose bets are predictable is exploited. Second, a smooth parameterization of probabilities gives smooth learning. A small change in \(\theta\) makes a small change in behaviour, which is not true of an argmax over noisy value estimates. The third reason is the one that matters most in practice: during training, randomness is how the agent finds out what its untried actions are worth. Section 7 makes that precise.

3. Softmax over a variable action set

From scores to probabilities, traced on one decision

Take a real decision. On floor 1 of seed 170,000,000, the Ironclad faces a Seapunk with 48 hit points, which intends to attack for 13. The hand holds three Strikes, a Defend and the curse Ascender's Bane, and the potion belt holds two potions. After merging identical options (Chapter 1), the game offers five classes: play a Strike at the Seapunk, play Defend, drink potion 0, drink potion 1, or end the turn.

The network gives each class one real number, its logit. Write it \(z_\theta(s,a)\). The code calls these numbers logits; Sutton and Barto call them action preferences \(h(s,a,\theta)\) [1, §13.1]. For this decision the logits are:

To get probabilities, exponentiate each logit and divide by the sum. Subtracting the largest logit first changes nothing, as we show next, but keeps the exponentials from overflowing.

Definition 2.2 · Softmax policy

Given one logit per legal action, the softmax policy is

\begin{equation} \pi_\theta(a \mid s) = \frac{\exp z_\theta(s,a)}{\sum_{b \in \mathcal{A}(s)} \exp z_\theta(s,b)}, \qquad a \in \mathcal{A}(s). \label{eq:softmax} \end{equation}

The sum in the denominator runs over the legal actions of this state only. That is the whole trick for a variable action set: the normalising constant is recomputed for whatever \(\mathcal{A}(s)\) happens to be. Sutton and Barto write the same equation as their (13.2) and call it "soft-max in action preferences" [1, §13.1].

Only differences between logits matter

Add the same constant \(c\) to every logit. Each numerator gains a factor \(e^{c}\), and so does the denominator, so the policy does not change:

\begin{equation} \frac{\exp\big(z_\theta(s,a)+c\big)}{\sum_{b} \exp\big(z_\theta(s,b)+c\big)} = \frac{e^{c}\exp z_\theta(s,a)}{e^{c}\sum_{b}\exp z_\theta(s,b)} = \pi_\theta(a \mid s). \label{eq:shift} \end{equation}

Setting \(c = -\max_b z_\theta(s,b)\) gives the overflow-safe form the table used. The same cancellation gives the ratio of two actions' probabilities directly from their logit gap:

\begin{equation} \frac{\pi_\theta(a \mid s)}{\pi_\theta(b \mid s)} = \exp\big(z_\theta(s,a) - z_\theta(s,b)\big). \label{eq:ratio} \end{equation}

In the Seapunk decision, the potion's logit exceeds Defend's by 1.49, so the potion is \(e^{1.49} \approx 4.4\) times as likely. A gap of 2.3 is a factor of 10; a gap of 11.1, the distance from Defend down to Strike, is a factor of about 68,000. So a logit is a log-odds score relative to the other options on offer, not an estimate of return (Section 12).

Taking logs of Equation \eqref{eq:softmax} gives the log-softmax, the form training actually uses:

\begin{equation} \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s) = z_\theta(s,a) - \log \sum_{b \in \mathcal{A}(s)} \exp z_\theta(s,b). \label{eq:logsoftmax} \end{equation}

The second term, the log-sum-exp, is a smooth maximum of the logits. A log-probability is therefore "this action's score minus a soft version of the best score".

Masking: how a fixed-size tensor holds a variable set

A batch of decisions on a GPU is a rectangular tensor. If the widest decision in the batch has 18 options, every row gets 18 slots, and a decision with 5 options has 13 empty ones. The network sets the logit of each empty slot to \(-\infty\) before the softmax. Since \(\exp(-\infty) = 0\), those slots get probability zero and contribute nothing to the denominator:

\begin{equation} \tilde z_\theta(s,a) = \begin{cases} z_\theta(s,a) & a \in \mathcal{A}(s) \\ -\infty & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad \operatorname{softmax}(\tilde z)_a = \pi_\theta(a \mid s). \label{eq:mask} \end{equation}

Here \(\tilde z\) is the padded, masked vector of logits. Masking is how the "sum over \(\mathcal{A}(s)\)" of Equation \eqref{eq:softmax} becomes a tensor operation.

Why identical options are merged

The hand above holds three Strikes, and any of them can be played. Suppose the network scored each copy as its own action. Identical inputs give identical logits, say \(z\), so the three copies together would get

\begin{equation} \pi(\text{some Strike} \mid s) = \frac{k\, e^{z}}{k\, e^{z} + \sum_{b \notin \text{Strikes}} e^{z_b}}, \qquad k = 3. \label{eq:dup} \end{equation}

Here \(k\) is the number of identical copies. The policy's appetite for "play a Strike" would grow with how many Strikes happen to be in hand, which has nothing to do with whether a Strike is a good play. Equivalently, duplicates add \(\log k\) to the merged option's effective logit. Spire Mentor merges identical options into one class before scoring, so the softmax runs over distinct choices. That is why the table lists one Strike row with "3 copies merged".

The gradient of a log-probability

Training needs the derivative of \(\log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\) with respect to each logit. Differentiate Equation \eqref{eq:logsoftmax} with respect to \(z_\theta(s,b)\). The first term gives 1 if \(b = a\) and 0 otherwise. The second gives \(\exp z_\theta(s,b) / \sum_{c}\exp z_\theta(s,c) = \pi_\theta(b \mid s)\). So

\begin{equation} \frac{\partial \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)}{\partial z_\theta(s,b)} = \mathbb{1}[a = b] - \pi_\theta(b \mid s). \label{eq:gradlogpi} \end{equation}

Here \(\mathbb{1}[a=b]\) is 1 when \(b\) is the action taken and 0 otherwise. Increasing \(\log \pi(a \mid s)\) pushes \(a\)'s own logit up by \(1 - \pi(a \mid s)\) and every other logit down by its current probability. This is a classifier's cross-entropy gradient, "one-hot label minus predicted probabilities", with the sign flipped. It also says when learning stops: once \(\pi(a \mid s)\) is near 1, the push on \(a\) is near zero. Sutton and Barto derive the same expression for the gradient bandit, in the form \(\partial \pi(x)/\partial H(a) = \pi(x)\,(\mathbb{1}[a=x] - \pi(a))\) [1, §2.8].

4. A network that scores candidate actions

There are two ways to build a network whose output is Equation \eqref{eq:softmax}.

The first is the one you know from DQN. The network maps the state to a vector with one entry per action index: output unit 3 always means "fire". This works when the action set is small and fixed. It fails when actions are things like "play this card on that enemy", because the same card can sit at any hand position, the hand changes size, and the number of possible (card, target) pairs is large. A fixed output layer would need a unit for every action that could ever occur, and would have to learn separately that "Strike at hand position 2" and "Strike at position 4" are the same move.

The second way scores each candidate. Give each legal action its own input representation, run a shared function over each (state, candidate) pair, and read off one number per candidate:

\begin{equation} z_\theta(s,a) = f_\theta\big(\phi_\theta(s),\, \psi_\theta(s,a)\big), \qquad a \in \mathcal{A}(s). \label{eq:scorer} \end{equation}

Here \(\phi_\theta(s)\) is a vector summarising the state, \(\psi_\theta(s,a)\) is a vector describing candidate \(a\) in the context of \(s\), and \(f_\theta\) is a small network that returns a scalar. The same \(f_\theta\) is applied to every candidate, so the number of candidates can be anything. Sutton and Barto's simplest instance is linear, \(h(s,a,\theta) = \theta^{\top} x(s,a)\) with a hand-built feature vector \(x(s,a)\) for each state-action pair [1, §13.1, eq. 13.3]. Deep RL agents for text games used the same pattern to score free-text actions [9], and pointer networks use it to choose among a variable number of input elements [10].

In supervised terms, Equation \eqref{eq:scorer} is a ranking model: a recommender that scores one user's candidate items and applies a softmax over them, with the game state as the query and the legal moves as the items.

Spire Mentor's network has exactly this shape, with an attention trunk computing both \(\phi\) and \(\psi\). Figure 2.1 traces it on the Seapunk decision.

Two details of the figure matter for what follows. First, the head that scores a fight decision is not the head that scores a card reward. The network has a combat head and an out-of-combat head: two copies of the same small architecture with separate weights, both reading the same trunk. The decision's type selects which one produces the logits:

\begin{equation} z_\theta(s,a) = \begin{cases} f^{\text{combat}}_\theta\big(h_a, h_{\text{state}}, e_{\text{kind}(a)}\big) & s \text{ is inside a fight} \\ f^{\text{ooc}}_\theta\big(h_a, h_{\text{state}}, e_{\text{kind}(a)}\big) & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases} \label{eq:heads} \end{equation}

Here \(h_a\) is the trunk's output for option \(a\)'s token, \(h_{\text{state}}\) is its output for the state token, and \(e_{\text{kind}(a)}\) is a learned embedding of what sort of action \(a\) is (play a card, drink a potion, move on the map). Each head is a one-hidden-layer network:

\begin{equation} f_\theta(h_a, h_{\text{state}}, e) = w^{\top} \operatorname{SiLU}\!\big(W_{\text{opt}}\, h_a + W_{\text{state}}\, h_{\text{state}} + W_{\text{kind}}\, e + b\big) + b_{\text{out}}. \label{eq:head} \end{equation}

The matrices \(W\) map 256-number vectors to a 256-unit hidden layer, SiLU is the activation \(x \cdot \sigma(x)\), and \(w\) and \(b_{\text{out}}\) project the hidden layer to one number. Because \(W_{\text{state}} h_{\text{state}}\) is the same for every option, it acts like a state-dependent bias inside the nonlinearity: the same option can score high in one state and low in another. Second, the scores are produced before anything is known about which option will be chosen. The network does not "pick"; it scores, and a separate rule picks.

5. Entropy

We need one number that says how spread out a policy is at a state. Is the agent sure, or torn between options? The standard measure comes from information theory [11].

Definition 2.3 · Entropy of a policy at a state
\begin{equation} \mathcal{H}[\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s)] = -\sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}(s)} \pi_\theta(a \mid s) \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s) = \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s)}\big[-\log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\big]. \label{eq:entropy} \end{equation}

The sum runs over the legal actions, and the logarithm is natural, so entropy is measured in nats. Terms with \(\pi = 0\) contribute zero, by the limit \(p \log p \to 0\). The second form says what entropy is: the expected surprise, \(-\log \pi\), of the action the policy will draw. A policy that is certain is never surprised.

For the Seapunk decision the potion contributes \(-0.816 \ln 0.816 = 0.166\) and Defend \(-0.184 \ln 0.184 = 0.311\), for \(\mathcal H = 0.477\) nats, and the three tiny classes add less than 0.0001.

Entropy has hard limits. It is zero exactly when one action has probability 1. It is largest when all \(n = |\mathcal{A}(s)|\) actions are equally likely, where every term is \(-\tfrac{1}{n}\log\tfrac{1}{n}\):

\begin{equation} 0 \;\le\; \mathcal{H}[\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s)] \;\le\; \log |\mathcal{A}(s)|. \label{eq:bounds} \end{equation}

The upper bound depends on how many actions are on offer. A uniform choice between two map paths has entropy \(\log 2 = 0.69\) nats; a uniform choice among eighteen shop items has \(\log 18 = 2.89\). So raw entropy cannot compare a map decision with a shop decision. Divide by the bound to get the normalised entropy, which lies in \([0,1]\) for every decision:

\begin{equation} \bar{\mathcal{H}}(s) = \frac{\mathcal{H}[\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s)]}{\log |\mathcal{A}(s)|}, \qquad |\mathcal{A}(s)| \ge 2. \label{eq:normH} \end{equation}

The Seapunk decision has \(\bar{\mathcal H} = 0.477 / \log 5 = 0.30\). Spire Mentor's training uses this normalised form, per decision type, for its entropy bonus (Section 9).

For a softmax policy, entropy has a closed form in the logits. Substitute Equation \eqref{eq:logsoftmax} into \eqref{eq:entropy}:

\begin{equation} \mathcal{H}[\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s)] = \log \sum_{b \in \mathcal{A}(s)} \exp z_\theta(s,b) \;-\; \sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}(s)} \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\, z_\theta(s,a). \label{eq:Hlogits} \end{equation}

Entropy is the gap between the soft maximum of the logits and their probability-weighted average. When one logit towers over the rest, both terms approach that logit and the gap closes to zero.

Finally, the gradient. Chapter 5 adds a multiple of entropy to the training objective, so we need to know which way it pushes the logits. Differentiating Equation \eqref{eq:entropy} with the help of Equation \eqref{eq:gradlogpi} gives

\begin{equation} \frac{\partial\, \mathcal{H}[\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s)]}{\partial z_\theta(s,b)} = -\,\pi_\theta(b \mid s)\,\Big(\log \pi_\theta(b \mid s) + \mathcal{H}[\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s)]\Big). \label{eq:gradH} \end{equation}

The bracket compares action \(b\)'s log-probability with its average, \(-\mathcal{H}\). Actions more likely than average (bracket positive) have their logits pushed down; less likely ones are pushed up. The factor \(\pi(b \mid s)\) means an action the policy has all but abandoned barely moves. At the Seapunk decision, raising entropy would lower the potion's logit by 0.22 per unit step and raise Defend's by the same amount, and leave the three tiny classes almost untouched.

6. Temperature, sampling and greedy selection

A policy is a distribution. To act, you still need a rule that turns it into one action. There are two standard rules, and one dial that slides between them.

Definition 2.4 · Sampled and greedy selection

Sampled selection draws \(a_t \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s_t)\): for a categorical distribution, pick action \(a\) with probability \(\pi_\theta(a \mid s_t)\) [8]. Greedy selection takes the most probable action,

\begin{equation} a_t^{\text{greedy}} = \arg\max_{a \in \mathcal{A}(s_t)} \pi_\theta(a \mid s_t) = \arg\max_{a \in \mathcal{A}(s_t)} z_\theta(s_t, a). \label{eq:greedy} \end{equation}

The two argmaxes are equal because the softmax is increasing in each logit. Greedy selection is itself a policy: the deterministic one that puts probability 1 on \(a^{\text{greedy}}\). So one network defines two agents, a stochastic one and a deterministic one, which can win at different rates.

What greedy selection throws away at one decision is the probability mass on every other action, \(1 - \max_a \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\). At the Seapunk decision that is 0.184, the chance that a sampling agent would have played Defend instead of drinking the potion.

The temperature dial

Divide every logit by a positive number \(T\), the temperature, before the softmax:

\begin{equation} \pi_{\theta,T}(a \mid s) = \frac{\exp\big(z_\theta(s,a)/T\big)}{\sum_{b \in \mathcal{A}(s)} \exp\big(z_\theta(s,b)/T\big)}. \label{eq:temp} \end{equation}

At \(T = 1\) this is the trained policy. Dividing by \(T\) multiplies every logit gap by \(1/T\), and by Equation \eqref{eq:ratio} every probability ratio is raised to the power \(1/T\). As \(T \to 0\) the gap to the top action becomes infinite, and the policy becomes greedy. As \(T \to \infty\) all gaps shrink to zero, and the policy becomes uniform over \(\mathcal{A}(s)\):

\begin{equation} \lim_{T \to 0^+} \pi_{\theta,T}(a \mid s) = \mathbb{1}\big[a = a^{\text{greedy}}\big], \qquad \lim_{T \to \infty} \pi_{\theta,T}(a \mid s) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}(s)|}. \label{eq:limits} \end{equation}

The first limit assumes a unique top logit; with ties, the mass splits evenly among the tied actions. Between the limits, entropy rises steadily with temperature. Write \(\beta = 1/T\). From Equation \eqref{eq:Hlogits} with logits \(\beta z\), one can show \(d\mathcal{H}/d\beta = -\beta \operatorname{Var}_{\pi_{\theta,T}}[z]\), and the chain rule gives

\begin{equation} \frac{d}{dT}\,\mathcal{H}[\pi_{\theta,T}(\cdot \mid s)] = \frac{1}{T^{3}}\, \operatorname{Var}_{a \sim \pi_{\theta,T}(\cdot \mid s)}\big[z_\theta(s,a)\big] \;\ge\; 0. \label{eq:dHdT} \end{equation}

The variance is that of the logits under the tempered policy. It is zero only when the policy has collapsed onto actions that share one logit, so entropy never decreases as temperature rises. Figure 2.2 shows the dial on the Seapunk decision.

Sampling is greedy play on noisy logits

A neat identity connects the two rules. Draw one independent noise value \(g_a\) for each legal action from the standard Gumbel distribution, whose distribution function is \(F(g) = \exp(-e^{-g})\). Then take the argmax of the noisy logits:

\begin{equation} \arg\max_{a \in \mathcal{A}(s)} \big(z_\theta(s,a) + g_a\big) \;\sim\; \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s), \qquad g_a \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \operatorname{Gumbel}(0,1). \label{eq:gumbel} \end{equation}

The proof is short. Action \(a\) wins if \(z_a + g_a > z_b + g_b\) for every \(b \ne a\). Conditioning on \(g_a = g\), each such event has probability \(F(z_a - z_b + g) = \exp(-e^{-g} e^{z_b - z_a})\). Multiplying over \(b \ne a\), then integrating over \(g\) against the Gumbel density \(e^{-g}\exp(-e^{-g})\), gives \(\int e^{-g}\exp\!\big(-e^{-g} \sum_b e^{z_b - z_a}\big)\,dg\), which equals \(1 / \sum_b e^{z_b - z_a} = \pi(a \mid s)\). This is the Gumbel-max trick [12]. It says a sampled agent is a greedy agent that sees its scores through noise with standard deviation \(\pi/\sqrt{6} \approx 1.3\). A lead of 10 almost never flips; a lead of 1 often does.

The one-step cost of sampling

Suppose we knew the true action values at a state, \(Q^\pi(s,a)\) (Chapter 4 defines them). Then the expected value of acting by sampling is a probability-weighted average of those values, and an average never exceeds the largest term:

\begin{equation} \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s)}\big[Q^\pi(s,a)\big] = \sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}(s)} \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\, Q^\pi(s,a) \;\le\; \max_{a \in \mathcal{A}(s)} Q^\pi(s,a). \label{eq:onestep} \end{equation}

So at a single decision, if the policy's top choice is also the value-maximising choice, sampling can only lose. That is the case for greedy evaluation. It has two gaps: the top choice may not be the best action, and over hundreds of decisions the greedy agent reaches states that the sampled agent, the one training saw, rarely visits. Section 11 measures the net effect.

7. Why training must sample

Consider one decision in isolation, a bandit: the agent picks \(a\) from \(\mathcal{A}(s)\), receives a random payoff with mean \(q(a)\), and the episode ends. The objective is the expected payoff, \(J(\theta) = \sum_a \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\, q(a)\). We want its gradient, but we do not know \(q\). We only get payoffs for actions we actually try.

Apply the log-derivative identity, \(\nabla_\theta \pi = \pi\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi\):

\begin{equation} \nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}(s)} q(a)\, \nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(a \mid s) = \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s)}\Big[q(a)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\Big]. \label{eq:score} \end{equation}

The left side needs \(q\) for every action. The right side is an expectation over actions drawn from the current policy, so we can estimate it by drawing actions from \(\pi_\theta\), observing their payoffs, and averaging \(\text{payoff} \times \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\). This is the score-function or likelihood-ratio estimator, introduced to reinforcement learning as REINFORCE by Williams [2]. Chapter 5 extends it from one decision to a whole trajectory, which is the policy gradient theorem [3].

With Equation \eqref{eq:gradlogpi}, one sample \(a\) with payoff \(R\), and a baseline \(\bar R\) subtracted from the payoff, a gradient step on the logits is

\begin{equation} z(s,b) \leftarrow z(s,b) + \alpha\,(R - \bar R)\,\big(\mathbb{1}[b = a] - \pi_\theta(b \mid s)\big) \quad \text{for all } b \in \mathcal{A}(s). \label{eq:bandit} \end{equation}

Here \(\alpha\) is a step size and \(\bar R\) is a running average of payoffs. This is Sutton and Barto's gradient bandit algorithm, their Equation (2.12), with \(z\) in place of their preferences \(H\) [1, §2.8]. If the payoff beats the average, the sampled action's logit goes up and the others go down in proportion to their probability. Chapter 4 explains why the baseline helps; for now, note only that it does not change the expected step.

Now read Equation \eqref{eq:score} as a statement about data. Three consequences follow, and each is a reason the learner samples.

  • The expectation is over the current policy. Unbiased estimates need actions drawn from \(\pi_\theta\) itself. Data from a different rule, such as greedy play, estimates a different expectation. The tour writes the rule that actually chose the actions as \(\mu\), the behaviour policy; here \(\mu\) is a policy, not a mean. Chapter 6 shows how importance weights correct for a mismatch, and why the correction has a variance cost.
  • Greedy data has no information about the other actions. If the agent always plays \(a^{\text{greedy}}\), every sample in Equation \eqref{eq:score} is that one action. The estimator can raise or lower its logit, but it can never discover that an untried action pays more. In the language of off-policy evaluation, a greedy behaviour policy has no support on the alternatives.
  • An argmax has no useful gradient. The greedy action is a step function of the logits. Its derivative is zero almost everywhere, so there is nothing to follow. The softmax is the smooth surrogate that makes \(\nabla_\theta\) meaningful.
Pause and predict: the Ironclad net plays about 660 decisions per run. If you sample from it at temperature 1 instead of taking the argmax, at what share of decisions will the sampled class differ from the greedy one?

Measured over 120 runs: 10.6%. Most decisions have a top class above 0.99, so most draws agree with greedy; a minority of split decisions produce nearly all the departures. Over a run that is tens of off-greedy actions, and Section 11 shows how early the runs diverge.

8. Exploration: ε-greedy against a softmax policy

Value-based agents explore with ε-greedy selection [1, §2.2]. Written as a policy over the legal actions, it is

\begin{equation} \pi_{\varepsilon}(a \mid s) = (1 - \varepsilon)\, \mathbb{1}\Big[a = \arg\max_{b \in \mathcal{A}(s)} Q(s,b)\Big] + \frac{\varepsilon}{|\mathcal{A}(s)|}. \label{eq:egreedy} \end{equation}

Here \(Q\) is the agent's current value estimate and \(\varepsilon\) is a fixed or annealed exploration rate. Compare it with a softmax policy on three counts.

  • Where the randomness goes. ε-greedy explores every non-greedy action equally. Playing a Strike at the Seapunk and ending the turn without acting would get the same exploration probability, though one is plausible and the other wasteful. A softmax policy spends its randomness on actions in proportion to \(e^{z}\), so plausible alternatives get tried far more than absurd ones.
  • Where the amount of randomness comes from. In ε-greedy, \(\varepsilon\) is a schedule the engineer chooses. It is the same in a state where two actions are near-equal and in a state where one action is obviously right. In a softmax policy, the spread at each state is learned. It stays wide where the evidence is ambiguous and narrows where it is clear. Equation \eqref{eq:gradlogpi} drives probabilities toward 0 or 1 wherever one action keeps winning.
  • Continuity. In ε-greedy, an arbitrarily small change in two \(Q\) estimates can swap which action gets probability \(1-\varepsilon\). Sutton and Barto cite this discontinuity as the main reason policy-gradient methods have stronger convergence guarantees [1, §13.2].

There is also a softmax over values, the Boltzmann rule \(\pi(a \mid s) \propto \exp\big(Q(s,a)/T\big)\). It shares the first advantage but not the second. The \(Q\) estimates converge to fixed true values whose gaps are set by the game, so the policy's spread is set by those gaps and the chosen \(T\), not by learning [1, §13.1].

The learned spread has a failure mode. Equation \eqref{eq:gradlogpi} keeps pushing the winning action's probability toward 1, and once it is near 1 the agent almost never tries anything else, so it never collects the evidence that could reverse the decision. Policies can collapse early onto a mediocre deterministic choice. The standard remedy adds the policy's entropy to the objective with a small weight \(\beta\), so the gradient of Equation \eqref{eq:gradH} resists the collapse:

\begin{equation} J_\beta(\theta) = J(\theta) + \beta\; \mathbb{E}_{s}\Big[\mathcal{H}\big[\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s)\big]\Big]. \label{eq:entbonus} \end{equation}

The expectation is over states the policy visits. Williams and Peng proposed this entropy bonus in 1991 [4], and A3C made it standard in deep RL, reporting that it "improved exploration by discouraging premature convergence to suboptimal deterministic policies" [5]. Chapter 5 places it inside the PPO loss.

Push the idea to its limit and you get maximum-entropy RL. Soft actor-critic maximises reward plus \(\alpha\) times entropy at every step, not as a regulariser but as the objective [6]. Its policy-improvement step moves the policy toward a Boltzmann distribution over a "soft" action value, \(\pi_{\text{new}}(\cdot \mid s) \propto \exp\big(Q^{\pi_{\text{old}}}(s,\cdot)\big)\), with the temperature folded into the reward scale. The bridge from Q-learning runs through here. In maximum-entropy RL, a softmax over action values is not an exploration heuristic added to a greedy learner; it is the optimal policy for an objective that pays for staying uncertain.

9. Textbook against engineered

The clean statement is Equations \eqref{eq:softmax} and \eqref{eq:score}: logits, a softmax, sampled actions for training, greedy actions for evaluation. A production system departs from it in several ways, and each departure has a reason.

  • Padding and masking. Variable sets are padded to a fixed width, with \(-\infty\) logits in the empty slots (Equation \eqref{eq:mask}). Spire Mentor also pads to a small set of fixed widths so that the compiled GPU graph sees few distinct shapes.
  • Numerical precision. Large parts of the network run in bfloat16 for speed, but the final projection to logits and the log-softmax run in float32. Logits in this network reach magnitudes of tens (the card reward in Section 10 has a 27-unit gap between its top two classes), and \(\exp\) of such numbers loses all precision in a 16-bit format. The Rust inference port goes further: it computes the softmax in float64.
  • Ties. Equation \eqref{eq:greedy} needs a rule for equal logits. Spire Mentor takes the first maximum, as PyTorch's argmax does, so the greedy agent is fully deterministic given the state.
  • The behaviour policy is not always the student. During parts of training, some actions are chosen by teacher networks rather than by \(\pi_\theta\). The learner records the log-probability of whatever rule acted, the behaviour policy \(\mu\). Chapter 6 shows how the update corrects for \(\mu \ne \pi_\theta\). Here \(\mu\) is the behaviour policy; it has nothing to do with a mean.
  • Entropy per decision type. A single entropy weight would treat a two-way rest-site choice and an eighteen-way shop the same. Spire Mentor uses normalised entropy (Equation \eqref{eq:normH}), separate weights for fights and for decisions between fights, and a schedule that lowers them over training.
  • The evaluated policy is an average of policies. The published network's weights are an exponential moving average of the training weights over roughly the last 131 million decisions. Evaluating it greedily evaluates neither the policy that generated any training data nor its argmax, but the argmax of a smoothed version.

10. In Spire Mentor

In Spire Mentor · the policy network

Input. Every decision becomes a set of tokens. Token 0 is the state token, which the code calls RUNCTX. Then come summary tokens for the draw pile, discard pile, exhaust pile, deck and relics, and the act's boss. A fight decision adds tokens for the enemies, the cards and the fight context, plus one token per legal action; when several actions merge into one class, only the first member's token is visible to the policy. A decision between fights adds the deck, relics, potions and what is on offer, plus one option token per class. The Seapunk decision is 28 tokens; the card reward that follows it is 19.

Trunk. A transformer with no positional encoding, so the tokens form a set and their order means nothing. The design specified 6 blocks of width 256 unless a throughput benchmark chose 4; the published Ironclad network has 4 blocks of width 256 with 4 attention heads, and 6.1 million parameters including the value module.

Heads. Two policy heads of the form of Equation \eqref{eq:head}, one for fights and one for everything else, each with a 256-unit hidden layer. Separate weights let the network learn different scales and features for "which card now" and "which reward to take" while sharing everything it knows about the deck and the run.

Output. One logit per merged class, \(-\infty\) in padded slots, then a float32 log-softmax. In code, \(z_\theta(s,a)\) is logits, \(\log\pi_\theta(a \mid s)\) is logp, and the option token is found through cls_pos.

The card reward that follows the Seapunk fight offers Unrelenting, Pommel Strike, True Grit or skipping. The out-of-combat head scores them 39.5, 12.8, −15.9 and −7.3. The 27-unit gap between the top two leaves \(2 \times 10^{-12}\) of probability off Unrelenting, and the entropy of this decision rounds to zero.

Training. Actions are drawn from the behaviour policy's probabilities with torch.multinomial. The entropy bonus of Equation \eqref{eq:entbonus} uses normalised entropy per decision type. Its weight is zero while the teacher term is strong, then decays from 0.01 to 0.002 in fights and from 0.0125 to 0.006 between fights. If any decision type's raw entropy stays below 0.1 nats for 20 iterations, the weight is raised by half, at most twice.

Evaluation. Every reported win rate uses greedy selection, "greedy argmax over merged options" in the plan's words.

How fast does a sampled run leave the greedy path?

A sampled run copies the greedy run until its first departure. At each decision along the greedy path, a temperature-1 sampler stays on that path with probability \(\max_a \pi_\theta(a \mid s_t)\). Its chance of matching greedy for the first \(k\) decisions is the product of those probabilities, and its expected number of departures is roughly their complements summed:

\begin{equation} \Pr\big[\text{no departure in decisions } 0,\dots,k-1\big] = \prod_{t=0}^{k-1} \max_{a} \pi_\theta(a \mid s_t), \qquad \mathbb{E}[\#\text{departures}] \approx \sum_{t=0}^{T} \Big(1 - \max_a \pi_\theta(a \mid s_t)\Big). \label{eq:survival} \end{equation}

Here \(s_t\) are the states of the greedy run. The product is exact, because until the first departure the sampler is in exactly the greedy run's states. The sum is an approximation, because after a departure the sampler visits states the greedy run never reached.

So after the first few dozen decisions, sampled and greedy play of one seed are different runs that meet different fights, rewards and shops. The paired comparison below compares two policies on the same seeds, not the same trajectories.

12. Common confusions

"A logit is a value estimate." It is not. A Q-value predicts return, and its absolute level means something. A logit has no absolute level: add 100 to every logit at a state and the policy is unchanged (Equation \eqref{eq:shift}). The card reward in Section 10 gives its favourite card a logit of 39.5. That does not mean the card is worth 39.5 of anything; it means the card is preferred to the next option by a factor of about \(e^{27}\). Chapter 4 introduces the separate value head that does predict return.

"A top-class probability of 0.999 means the network is 99.9% sure it is right." A policy probability is not a calibrated probability of correctness. Training drives the probability of any action that keeps winning toward 1, whether or not a slightly better action exists that the policy stopped trying. Near-certainty in Figure 2.3 says the policy has stopped exploring there. It does not say the choice is optimal.

"Evaluating greedily measures the policy we trained." It measures a different, deterministic policy built from the same network. The trained policy is the stochastic one, and the training data came from sampling it (or from teachers). Section 11 shows that the two diverge within the first few dozen decisions of a run. Greedy is a reasonable way to deploy and to compare checkpoints, but the number it produces belongs to the argmax policy.

"Sampling from the policy is just ε-greedy with a learned ε." The two spend their randomness differently. ε-greedy deviates from its top choice at the same rate everywhere and spreads the deviation evenly over all other actions. A softmax policy deviates almost never at settled decisions and often at split ones, and it deviates mostly toward the runner-up. That is why a sampler that departs from greedy at roughly one decision in ten can still win nearly as often.

"More entropy is always better for exploration." Entropy is useful during training at states where the right answer is not yet known. At states where options are equivalent, high entropy is correct and costs nothing. At states where one option is clearly best, high entropy is just noise. The entropy bonus is kept small, scheduled to shrink, and normalised per decision type for this reason.
