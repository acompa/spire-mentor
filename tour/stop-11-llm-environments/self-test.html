RL environments for language-model agents: self-test

Write each answer from memory before you open it. Come back in a few days and answer the questions again.

Questions on Chapter 11, RL environments for language-model agents.

Recall

  1. The chapter gives three reasons an environment for a language-model agent is harder to build than a game simulator. What are they? Then compare where the difficulty sits in Q-learning on Atari with where it sits here.

    Answer

    First, the tasks are data that someone collected, and nobody knows how hard a task is until a model tries it. Second, the sandbox runs arbitrary code, so the model can do far more than the designer considered. Third, the reward comes from a verifier, a program that checks the outcome, and a verifier can be wrong. On Atari the emulator is fixed and correct, and all the difficulty is in the learner. Here you often reuse chapter 10's learner unchanged, and most of the engineering, along with most of the ways a run goes wrong, sits in the environment. (Section 1)

  2. Why is an episode in this environment a partially observed decision process? Give one way the render function can stop the policy from learning something, and one way it can teach the policy something it should not learn.

    Answer

    The sandbox state is the state, but the policy sees only the transcript \(h_t\): the task, its own turns, and the rendered result of each call:

    \[ o_{t+1} = \mathrm{render}(s_t, a_t, s_{t+1}) \]

    The render function cuts megabytes of raw output to text that fits the context, so the transcript is a lossy view of the sandbox. If it drops the stack-trace line that names the failing test, the policy cannot learn to read that line. If it shows a file the task meant to hide, the policy can learn to copy from it. (Section 3)

  3. What is the difference between a terminated episode and a truncated one? How does a bootstrapping learner treat each, and what must the designer decide about the verifier's score on a budget cut?

    Answer

    An episode is terminated when it reaches a terminal state of the task, for example when the model calls submit. It is truncated when it stops for a reason outside the task, such as a turn, token or time limit. A value target treats a truncated episode as if the future continued, and zeroes the bootstrap term only on termination:

    \[ \hat{V}(s_t) = r_t + \gamma\,\big(1 - \mathrm{terminated}_t\big)\,V_\phi(s_{t+1}) \]

    Most language-model training drops the critic, so the distinction moves to the verifier, which scores a budget cut like any other ending. The designer must give that ending an explicit score no better than a wrong answer, so a model that never submits cannot beat one that submits a wrong answer. (Sections 3 and 10)

  4. Under class-conditional errors, derive the expected verdict for one trajectory and then the gradient of \(J_{\mathrm{ver}}\). What happens to training when \(\kappa\) is positive, zero and negative?

    Answer

    Class-conditional means the error rates depend only on whether \(y\) is 0 or 1. A trajectory with \(y = 1\) passes with probability \(1 - e_{\mathrm{fail}}\), and one with \(y = 0\) passes with probability \(e_{\mathrm{pass}}\). Collecting the \(y\) terms gives

    \[ \mathbb{E}[v \mid \tau] = y(1 - e_{\mathrm{fail}}) + (1 - y)\,e_{\mathrm{pass}} = e_{\mathrm{pass}} + \kappa\, y \]

    Averaging over \(\pi_\theta\) gives the first equation below. The intercept does not depend on \(\theta\), so it drops out of the gradient:

    \[ J_{\mathrm{ver}} = e_{\mathrm{pass}} + \kappa J_{\mathrm{true}}, \qquad \nabla_\theta J_{\mathrm{ver}} = \kappa\,\nabla_\theta J_{\mathrm{true}} \]

    If \(\kappa > 0\), the direction is exactly right and only the length shrinks. If \(\kappa = 0\), the verdict says nothing about the truth and the gradient is zero. If \(\kappa \lt 0\), training climbs the wrong way. (Section 4)

  5. Wang, Liu and Li's surrogate reward \(\hat{r}(v)\) is unbiased for the true reward. Why does it change nothing important for a policy-gradient learner with a baseline? What does it fail to fix?

    Answer

    The surrogate is an affine function of \(v\):

    \[ \hat{r}(v) = \frac{v - e_{\mathrm{pass}}}{\kappa} \]

    Subtracting the constant \(e_{\mathrm{pass}}\) from every reward is absorbed by the baseline. Dividing by \(\kappa\) is the same as multiplying the learning rate by \(1/\kappa\). The direction was already right under class-conditional errors, so there is nothing for the surrogate to correct. It does nothing about the variance of each verdict, and nothing about hacks, whose errors depend on the trajectory. (Sections 4 and 10)

  6. Write the gradient of \(J_{\mathrm{ver}}\) when the error rates depend on the trajectory, and say what each of the two error terms pushes the policy toward. Why do they disappear when the rates are constant?

    Answer \[ \nabla_\theta J_{\mathrm{ver}} = \nabla_\theta J_{\mathrm{true}} + \nabla_\theta\, \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}\!\big[(1 - y)\, e_{\mathrm{pass}}(\tau)\big] - \nabla_\theta\, \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}\!\big[y\, e_{\mathrm{fail}}(\tau)\big] \]

    The middle term is the hacking gradient. It points toward failed trajectories that the verifier passes more often, and nothing in the verdict tells it apart from progress. The last term pushes away from correct answers the verifier tends to reject, such as a correct answer in a format the parser does not accept. With constant rates the two error terms become multiples of the true gradient, and they fold back into it:

    \[ \nabla J_{\mathrm{true}} - e_{\mathrm{pass}}\nabla J_{\mathrm{true}} - e_{\mathrm{fail}}\nabla J_{\mathrm{true}} = \kappa\,\nabla J_{\mathrm{true}} \]

    (Section 5)

  7. In the small model with one honest action and one hack, which tasks does the hack take over at convergence, and why are those the tasks you can least afford to lose?

    Answer

    The learner compares the expected verdicts of the two actions:

    \[ \underbrace{p_x(1 - e_{\mathrm{fail}}) + (1 - p_x)\,e_{\mathrm{pass}}}_{\text{honest attempt}} \quad\text{against}\quad \underbrace{e_{\mathrm{hack}}}_{\text{hack}} \]

    Solving for \(p_x\), the hack wins on every task where

    \[ p_x \lt \frac{e_{\mathrm{hack}} - e_{\mathrm{pass}}}{\kappa} \]

    so the hardest tasks go first. With an otherwise perfect verifier, a hack that passes 30% of the time leaves alone a task the policy solves 80% of the time, and takes every task it solves less than 30% of the time. Those hard tasks are the ones that should teach the policy something new. This is also a second reason section 6 gives for keeping a spread of difficulty in the task set. (Sections 5 and 6)

  8. Name the checks practitioners run against hacks and say what each one catches. Why is a chain-of-thought monitor safer as an audit than as a reward term?

    Answer

    An oracle solution must pass, which catches broken tasks and false failures. A do-nothing agent must fail, which catches free reward. A held-out verifier that the policy never trains against catches a training reward that rises while the held-out score does not, as in Huang and coauthors' hacked model verifier. Reading the top-reward trajectories finds hacks where they concentrate. Removing the opportunity, with hidden or read-only tests and grading in a fresh sandbox, stops some hacks from being possible. Baker and coauthors put a chain-of-thought monitor into the reward of a smaller agent. The agent hacked less but still often, and it learned to hide its intent, so the monitor's recall fell to near zero. (Section 5)

  9. With group baselines, why does a task the policy almost always fails contribute almost no gradient? And how does flaky test behaviour show up in section 4's and section 5's terms?

    Answer

    If all \(n\) attempts at a task pass or all fail, every advantage in the group is zero. The chance that the group is mixed is \(1 - q^{n} - (1 - q)^{n}\). With \(n = 8\) that is 0.99 at \(q = 0.5\) but 0.08 at \(q = 0.01\), so the sandbox time spent on such a task is mostly wasted. Flakiness shared equally by every task acts as a class-conditional error and lowers \(\kappa\). If some tasks are flakier than others, each task gets its own \(\kappa(x)\) and the gradient favours tasks with reliable tests. If the flakiness hits some solutions more than others, it is an \(e_{\mathrm{fail}}(\tau)\) term in the hacking-gradient equation. (Section 6)

  10. Which three assumptions of the Gym interface does a language-model environment break? According to each project's own documentation, as read for the chapter, how do OpenEnv, Verifiers, SkyRL-Gym and NeMo Gym differ in where they draw boundaries?

    Answer

    Gym assumes the environment runs in the learner's process, steps quickly, and computes its reward in code. A language-model step can run a whole test suite, the sandbox is a container that should be isolated from the trainer, and the reward is a separate program. As their documentation describes them, Verifiers, SkyRL-Gym and NeMo Gym separate the task set from the code that runs it, while OpenEnv keeps dataset loading inside the environment with an optional Task API. Verifiers and NeMo Gym give the verifier its own named place, and Verifiers offers grading in a fresh sandbox, which is check 5 built in. OpenEnv and NeMo Gym put the environment behind a network interface. SkyRL-Gym stays closest to Gymnasium's in-process step. The chapter warns that these projects change quickly, so check their current documentation. (Section 7)

  11. In verifier terms, what are Spire Mentor's error rates, and with respect to what? What could still make a verdict wrong, and which runbook rule plays the role of a held-out verifier? Then, in the worked design that exists only on paper, why must an unparsable turn never fall back to a default action?

    Answer

    The reward is computed in code from the run's outcome, so both error rates are zero with respect to the simulator. A verdict can still be wrong with respect to the real game wherever the Rust simulator differs from it. The runbook audits the top runs against the C# game when two evaluation reads differ by more than 5 percentage points, which is check 4, reading the top-reward trajectories, and works as the held-out verifier. In the paper design for a language-model agent, a default that happened to be strong would become a free policy. An unparsable turn instead gets an error message and counts against the budget, so a do-nothing agent that emits garbage scores no better than one that loses. Nothing in the repository implements this design. (Section 8)

  12. The chapter's toy problem, which it labels a toy and not a language model, ran two experiments. What did each set up, what did each show, and which common confusion does Experiment B answer?

    Answer

    In Experiment A every failed attempt passed with the same probability \(e_{\mathrm{pass}}\), the class-conditional case. All settings climbed toward the always-attempt rate of 0.50, and a larger \(e_{\mathrm{pass}}\) only slowed the climb, roughly as \(1/\kappa\). In Experiment B only the hack passed falsely, the trajectory-dependent case. At \(e_{\mathrm{hack}} = 0.6\) the verifier pass rate climbed from 0.38 to 0.67, while true success peaked at 0.46 after 60 updates and ended at 0.34. Random errors cost time, and errors the policy can choose to trigger change where training ends. That answers the confusion that a higher training reward means a better policy. Track a held-out measure, which in the toy would have caught the problem at update 60. (Sections 9 and 10)

Calculations

  1. Take the verifier from Figure 11.7, with \(e_{\mathrm{pass}} = 0.2\) and \(e_{\mathrm{fail}} = 0.1\). (a) Compute \(\kappa\). (b) For a policy with \(J_{\mathrm{true}} = 0.5\), compute \(J_{\mathrm{ver}}\). (c) By what factor is the gradient you follow shorter than the true one? (d) Compute the surrogate reward \(\hat{r}\) for a pass and for a fail, and check that its expectation is 1 on a trajectory that truly solved the task.

    Answer

    (a) \[ \kappa = 1 - 0.2 - 0.1 = 0.7 \]

    (b) \[ J_{\mathrm{ver}} = e_{\mathrm{pass}} + \kappa J_{\mathrm{true}} = 0.2 + 0.7 \times 0.5 = 0.55 \]

    (c)

    \[ \nabla J_{\mathrm{ver}} = 0.7\,\nabla J_{\mathrm{true}} \]

    The direction is the same and the length is 0.7 of the true gradient.

    (d) \[ \hat{r}(1) = \frac{1 - 0.2}{0.7} \approx 1.143, \qquad \hat{r}(0) = \frac{0 - 0.2}{0.7} \approx -0.286 \]

    A trajectory with \(y = 1\) passes with probability \(1 - e_{\mathrm{fail}} = 0.9\):

    \[ 0.9 \times 1.143 + 0.1 \times (-0.286) = 1.029 - 0.029 = 1.0 \]

    (Section 4)

  2. Huang and coauthors' rule-based math verifiers had \(e_{\mathrm{pass}}\) near zero and \(e_{\mathrm{fail}}\) up to about 0.14. (a) Compute \(\kappa\) for the worst of them. (b) At a small learning rate, how many times more updates does training need than with a perfect verifier? (c) How many times more trajectories does one update need for the same confidence in the gradient's direction? (d) In the chapter's toy problem, not a language model, \(e_{\mathrm{pass}} = 0.7\). What ratio of updates does \(1/\kappa\) predict, and what did the toy measure?

    Answer

    (a) \[ \kappa = 1 - 0 - 0.14 = 0.86 \]

    (b) Updates grow as \(1/\kappa\):

    \[ 1/0.86 \approx 1.16 \]

    (c) The required trajectory count grows as \(1/\kappa^2\):

    \[ 1/0.86^2 \approx 1/0.74 \approx 1.35 \]

    Halving \(\kappa\) would quadruple this count.

    (d) \[ \kappa = 1 - 0.7 = 0.3, \qquad 1/\kappa \approx 3.3 \]

    The toy reached a true success rate of 0.45 in a median of 355 updates against 110 with a perfect verifier, a ratio of 3.2. That is a result about the toy, not about a language model. (Sections 4 and 9)
