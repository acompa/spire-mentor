1. From Q-learning to the policy itself

In deep Q-learning we learn \(Q(s,a)\) by regression and act by taking the argmax. The policy is a by-product: it exists only through the value estimates. That works well when the action set is small and fixed, and when a greedy policy is good enough.

Spire Mentor's agent, Instinct, does something different. Its network outputs a probability for every legal option in the current decision: which card to play, which target, which reward to take, which path on the map. Chapter 2 built that network as a softmax over a set of legal actions \(\mathcal{A}(s)\) that changes from state to state. This chapter answers the question that follows: how do we change the weights so that the runs the network plays get better?

The answer has two halves. The first is the policy gradient: the gradient of the expected return with respect to the policy's parameters, which turns out to be a weighted log-likelihood gradient on the network's own actions. You already know that object from supervised learning. The second half is about step size. The data that estimates the gradient was collected by the policy before the update. Move the weights too far and the data describes a policy that no longer exists. Trust-region methods, TRPO, and PPO are three answers to "how far is too far". By the end of the chapter you will be able to read the four numbers a PPO learner logs every iteration (approximate KL, clip fraction, entropy, explained variance) and say what each one means for the update that produced it.

We measure all of this on the project's own learner, trained from random weights on this Mac, in two configurations: one with the clip on, and one where nothing limits the step.

2. The policy gradient

The objective

Chapter 1 defined the objective. A trajectory \(\tau = (s_0, a_0, r_0, \dots, s_T)\) is one episode, here one run of the game. Its return from step \(t\) is \(G_t = \sum_{k=0}^{T-t} \gamma^k r_{t+k}\), and the objective is the expected return of the whole run:

\begin{equation} J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}\left[ G_0 \right] = \sum_{\tau} p_\theta(\tau)\, G_0(\tau). \label{eq:J} \end{equation}

\(\theta\) are the network's weights and \(p_\theta(\tau)\) is the probability that the policy \(\pi_\theta\) produces trajectory \(\tau\). The sum runs over every possible run. We want \(\nabla_\theta J\), and the difficulty is that \(\theta\) appears only inside the distribution we take the expectation over, not inside \(G_0\).

The probability of a trajectory factors into what the environment does and what the policy does:

\begin{equation} p_\theta(\tau) = p(s_0) \prod_{t=0}^{T-1} \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\, P(s_{t+1}, r_t \mid s_t, a_t). \label{eq:ptau} \end{equation}

\(p(s_0)\) is the distribution of starting states: the seed of the run. \(P\) is the game's dynamics: card draws, enemy intents, damage. Only the \(\pi_\theta\) factors depend on \(\theta\).

The derivation

You have used the log-derivative identity before: \(\nabla_\theta p_\theta = p_\theta \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta\). Apply it to Equation \eqref{eq:J}, moving the gradient inside the sum (a finite sum here, so this is allowed):

\begin{equation} \begin{aligned} \nabla_\theta J(\theta) &= \sum_\tau \nabla_\theta p_\theta(\tau)\, G_0(\tau) = \sum_\tau p_\theta(\tau)\, \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau)\, G_0(\tau) \\ &= \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}\big[ \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau)\; G_0(\tau) \big]. \end{aligned} \label{eq:score} \end{equation}

The gradient of an expectation has become an expectation of something we can compute on a sampled run. That is the whole trick: we can estimate Equation \eqref{eq:score} by playing runs and averaging.

The second layer is the log of the product in Equation \eqref{eq:ptau}. The log turns the product into a sum, and every term that does not contain \(\theta\) has zero gradient:

\begin{equation} \begin{aligned} \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau) ={}& \underbrace{\nabla_\theta \log p(s_0)}_{0} + \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) \\ &+ \underbrace{\sum_{t} \nabla_\theta \log P(s_{t+1}, r_t \mid s_t, a_t)}_{0}. \end{aligned} \label{eq:logp} \end{equation}

The dynamics drop out. We never need a model of the game, nor its gradient. That is why the same estimator works for a card game, a robot, and a language model.

Substituting Equation \eqref{eq:logp} into \eqref{eq:score}:

\begin{equation} \nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}\Big[ \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\; G_0 \Big]. \label{eq:pg-full} \end{equation}

Every action in the run is pushed up in proportion to the return of the whole run. This is correct in expectation, but wasteful: an action at step \(t\) cannot have caused rewards that arrived before it.

Causality: reward to go

Write \(G_0\) as the sum of its rewards and look at one pair: the score of action \(a_t\) times a reward \(r_k\) with \(k \lt t\). Condition on everything up to \(s_t\). Then \(r_k\) is fixed and the only randomness left is \(a_t \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s_t)\). The expected score is zero:

\begin{equation} \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s)}\big[ \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s) \big] &= \sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}(s)} \pi_\theta(a \mid s) \frac{\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(a \mid s)}{\pi_\theta(a \mid s)} \\ &= \nabla_\theta \sum_{a} \pi_\theta(a \mid s) = \nabla_\theta 1 = 0. \end{aligned} \label{eq:eglp} \end{equation}

This little lemma does a lot of work in this chapter. It says the score function has mean zero under the policy that defines it. So every past reward contributes nothing in expectation, and we drop it. What remains for action \(a_t\) are the rewards from step \(t\) on, \(\sum_{k \ge t} \gamma^k r_k = \gamma^t G_t\):

Definition 5.1 · The policy gradient (trajectory form)
\begin{equation} \nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}\Big[ \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \gamma^t\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\; G_t \Big]. \label{eq:pg} \end{equation}

Each action's log-probability gradient is weighted by the return that followed it.

The factor \(\gamma^t\) is exact for the discounted objective \eqref{eq:J}, and most implementations drop it. In Spire Mentor \(\gamma = 1\) (Chapter 3), so the question does not arise. Spinning Up derives the same reward-to-go form for the undiscounted case [11].

Sutton and Barto state the same result differently, as the policy gradient theorem [1, §13.2], following Sutton, McAllester, Singh and Mansour [3]:

\begin{equation} \begin{aligned} \nabla_\theta J(\theta) &\propto \sum_s d^{\pi}(s) \sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}(s)} Q^{\pi}(s,a)\, \nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(a \mid s) \\ &= \mathbb{E}_{s \sim d^\pi,\, a \sim \pi_\theta}\big[ Q^{\pi}(s,a)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s) \big]. \end{aligned} \label{eq:pgt} \end{equation}

Here \(d^\pi(s)\) is the share of time the policy spends in state \(s\) (the on-policy state distribution), and \(Q^\pi(s,a)\) is the expected return after taking \(a\) in \(s\) (Chapter 4). The two forms say the same thing. Equation \eqref{eq:pg} samples whole runs and uses the realised \(G_t\). Equation \eqref{eq:pgt} groups the same terms by state and replaces \(G_t\) with its expectation given \((s_t, a_t)\), which is \(Q^\pi(s_t,a_t)\). The proportionality sign hides a constant (the mean episode length in the episodic case) that a learning rate absorbs. Sutton and Barto index the reward after \(A_t\) as \(R_{t+1}\); this tour writes \(r_t\).

Two properties matter for the rest of the chapter. The state distribution \(d^\pi\) depends on \(\theta\), yet the gradient contains no derivative of it. That is the theorem's real content. And the expectation is over the current policy: states and actions must be drawn from \(\pi_\theta\) itself. That is what makes the method on-policy.

The bridge to supervised learning

Take one decision. In a fight, the Ironclad's hand offers Strike, Defend and Bash, and the network gives them probabilities 0.3, 0.2 and 0.5 (illustrative numbers). It samples Strike. For a softmax over logits \(z_\theta(s,a)\), the gradient of the log-probability with respect to the logits is

\begin{equation} \frac{\partial \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)}{\partial z_\theta(s, a')} = \mathbb{1}[a' = a] - \pi_\theta(a' \mid s). \label{eq:softmax} \end{equation}

For the sampled Strike this is \((+0.7, -0.2, -0.5)\) on (Strike, Defend, Bash). It is exactly the negative gradient of cross-entropy loss with Strike as the label. A classifier trained on this row would move Strike up and the others down. The policy gradient does the same, multiplied by \(G_t\). So one policy-gradient step is weighted maximum likelihood on the network's own actions: the sampled action plays the label, and the return plays the example weight. If you write the loss for an autodiff framework, it is

\begin{equation} L^{\mathrm{PG}}(\theta) = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \log \pi_\theta(a_i \mid s_i)\; \hat{A}_i, \label{eq:lpg} \end{equation}

a negative weighted log-likelihood over the \(N\) decisions in a batch, with a weight \(\hat{A}_i\) that the next sections refine from \(G_t\) to an advantage. This is PPO paper's Equation 2 with the sign flipped for minimisation [7].

The analogy breaks in one place, and the rest of the chapter is about that place. In supervised learning the labels do not depend on the model. Here they do: the network chose the actions, so the dataset is a function of \(\theta\). Change \(\theta\) and the right dataset changes.

3. REINFORCE

Williams named the simplest estimator of Equation \eqref{eq:pg} REINFORCE in 1992 [2]. Play one run with the current policy. For every step, compute \(G_t\). Take a gradient step on \(\sum_t \gamma^t G_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\). Repeat. Sutton and Barto give it as their Equation 13.8 [1, §13.3]:

\begin{equation} \theta \leftarrow \theta + \alpha\, \gamma^t\, G_t\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t), \qquad t = 0, 1, \dots, T-1. \label{eq:reinforce} \end{equation}

\(\alpha\) is the learning rate. The estimator is unbiased: averaged over runs, it equals \(\nabla_\theta J\). It is also very noisy. Consider what \(G_t\) looks like in Spire Mentor. The reward is \(+0.002\) per new floor, \(+0.05\) per boss beaten and \(+1\) for a win (Chapter 3). Every run reaches floor 1, so every return is positive. REINFORCE therefore pushes up every action it sampled, good or bad, and relies on the good runs being pushed up slightly more. The direction is right on average, but each single estimate points mostly at "do more of whatever you just did".

4. Baselines and variance

The lemma in Equation \eqref{eq:eglp} gives a free fix. For any function \(b(s)\) that depends on the state but not on the action,

\begin{equation} \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s)}\big[ b(s)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s) \big] = b(s)\cdot 0 = 0, \label{eq:baseline-zero} \end{equation}

so subtracting \(b(s_t)\) from \(G_t\) leaves the gradient unbiased [1, §13.4]:

\begin{equation} \nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}\Big[ \sum_{t} \gamma^t\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\, \big( G_t - b(s_t) \big) \Big]. \label{eq:pg-baseline} \end{equation}

The baseline \(b\) changes the variance, not the mean. If you know CUPED from experiment analysis, this is the same move: subtract a control variate whose expectation you know is zero. The leave-one-out baseline you have used for language models, \(\hat{A}_i = G_i - \frac{1}{K-1}\sum_{j \ne i} G_j\) over \(K\) samples from the same prompt, is one choice of \(b\). It uses the other samples' returns as an estimate of the prompt's value, and it leaves the estimator unbiased because sample \(i\)'s action does not influence the other samples' returns.

How much variance a baseline removes

Take one decision with three options and probabilities \(\pi = (0.5, 0.3, 0.2)\). Suppose their expected returns are \(G = (0.30, 0.25, 0.20)\): a typical Spire Mentor situation, where every option leads to a positive return and the differences between options are small next to the shared offset. The one-sample estimator with respect to the logits is \(\hat{g} = (G_a - b)\,(e_a - \pi)\) for the sampled \(a\), using Equation \eqref{eq:softmax}. Its mean does not depend on \(b\). Its variance does:

\begin{equation} \operatorname{tr}\operatorname{Var}[\hat g] = \sum_{a} \pi(a)\, (G_a - b)^2\, \lVert e_a - \pi \rVert^2 \;-\; \big\lVert \mathbb{E}[\hat g] \big\rVert^2 . \label{eq:var-b} \end{equation}

Setting the derivative with respect to \(b\) to zero gives the variance-minimising baseline, a weighted mean of the returns:

\begin{equation} b^{*} = \frac{\sum_a \pi(a)\, \lVert e_a - \pi \rVert^2\, G_a}{\sum_a \pi(a)\, \lVert e_a - \pi\rVert^2}. \label{eq:bstar} \end{equation}

It weights each return by how large that action's score is. In practice everyone uses \(b(s) = V(s) = \sum_a \pi(a)G_a\), the state value, because it is almost as good and a critic can learn it by regression.

Notice the scale in Figure 5.1. Without a baseline, the standard deviation of one sample is about 0.20 against a mean of norm 0.022: the signal is a tenth of the noise. With \(b = V\) the noise is about 0.025, so one sample already carries roughly as much signal as noise. A 63-fold variance reduction is worth 63 times more data.

From returns to advantages

With \(b(s) = V^\pi(s)\) the weight on each action becomes \(G_t - V^\pi(s_t)\), a sample of the advantage \(A^\pi(s,a) = Q^\pi(s,a) - V^\pi(s)\): how much better this action was than the policy's average in that state. Chapter 4 derives the advantage, the critic \(V_\phi\) that estimates \(V^\pi\), and the generalised advantage estimator (GAE) [13] that trades bias for variance with the parameter \(\lambda\) (here never a risk weight). Here we only need the result: each decision \(t\) arrives at the update with an estimate \(\hat{A}_t\), and the policy gradient we use is

\begin{equation} \hat{g} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{t} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\, \hat{A}_t . \label{eq:pg-adv} \end{equation}

This is the actor-critic form: the actor is the policy, the critic supplies the weights. The critic is a regression problem, and we will read its quality through the explained variance in Section 9.

Pause and predict: Spire Mentor normalises \(\hat A_t\) to mean 0 and variance 1 before the update. Does that change the expected gradient?

Subtracting a constant mean is a baseline: by Equation \eqref{eq:baseline-zero} it leaves the expectation unchanged, as long as the constant does not depend on the action (a batch mean depends weakly on every action in the batch, so this holds only approximately). Dividing by a standard deviation rescales the gradient, which an optimiser like Adam largely undoes anyway. So normalisation changes the effective learning rate and the variance, not the direction. Spire Mentor normalises per decision type with running moments, so that a card-reward choice and a combat turn get comparable weights.

5. Why large steps fail

Equation \eqref{eq:pg-adv} is a gradient at the current \(\theta\). It tells us which direction improves \(J\) locally. It says nothing about how far we can go. In supervised learning a too-large step costs you a worse loss value, which you see and fix on the next batch. In policy optimisation a bad step does more damage, for three reasons.

The data moves with the policy. After a bad step, the next batch is collected by the bad policy. If it stopped playing Bash, it will not see what Bash does, and nothing in the next gradient pushes it back. The error feeds its own training data. This is the compounding problem Chapter 7 analyses for imitation, in another form.

One batch, many steps. Collecting data is expensive: in Spire Mentor it is most of the wall time. We would like to take many gradient steps on each batch. But Equation \eqref{eq:pg-adv} holds only at the \(\theta\) that collected the batch. After the first step the samples come from a slightly different policy, and the estimator is biased. Repeated steps on the plain loss \eqref{eq:lpg} drive the probability of every positive-advantage action towards 1. The PPO paper reports that optimising \(L^{\mathrm{PG}}\) for several epochs "often leads to destructively large policy updates" [7, §2.1].

Distance in parameters is not distance in behaviour. A step of fixed length in \(\theta\) can barely change one state's distribution and completely change another's. A softmax with logits \((5, 0, 0)\) moves very little when you add 1 to the first logit; one with logits \((0, 0, 0)\) moves a lot. What we want to control is how much the policy's behaviour changes, measured in a space of distributions.

What a new policy is worth, measured with the old one's advantages

Kakade and Langford [5] gave an exact identity for how much better a new policy \(\pi'\) is than the old \(\pi\):

\begin{equation} \begin{aligned} J(\pi') &= J(\pi) + \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi'}\Big[ \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \gamma^t A^{\pi}(s_t, a_t) \Big] \\ &= J(\pi) + \sum_s \rho_{\pi'}(s) \sum_a \pi'(a \mid s)\, A^\pi(s,a). \end{aligned} \label{eq:pdl} \end{equation}

\(A^\pi\) is the old policy's advantage, and \(\rho_{\pi'}(s) = \sum_t \gamma^t P(s_t = s \mid \pi')\) is the discounted visit frequency of state \(s\) under the new policy. This is TRPO paper's Equations 1 and 2 [6]. Read the right-hand side as: the new policy gains whatever advantage (judged by the old policy's critic) its own choices collect, in the states it visits. We have the old policy's advantages. We do not have the new policy's state visits, since we have not played it yet. The tractable approximation replaces \(\rho_{\pi'}\) with \(\rho_{\pi}\):

\begin{equation} \begin{aligned} L_\pi(\pi') &= J(\pi) + \sum_s \rho_{\pi}(s) \sum_a \pi'(a \mid s)\, A^\pi(s,a) \\ &= J(\pi) + \mathbb{E}_{s \sim \rho_\pi,\, a \sim \pi}\Big[ \frac{\pi'(a\mid s)}{\pi(a\mid s)}\, A^\pi(s,a) \Big]. \end{aligned} \label{eq:surrogate} \end{equation}

The second form rewrites the sum over \(a\) as an expectation under the old policy, with an importance weight. This surrogate objective can be estimated from the batch we have. \(L_\pi\) matches \(J\) and its gradient at \(\pi' = \pi\) (TRPO's Equation 4), so for small moves it is a faithful stand-in. For large moves it ignores that the new policy visits different states, which is exactly the failure described above.

Define the probability ratio between the policy being optimised and the one that collected the data:

\begin{equation} \rho_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)}{\mu(a_t \mid s_t)}, \qquad \hat{L}^{\mathrm{CPI}}(\theta) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_t \rho_t(\theta)\, \hat{A}_t . \label{eq:cpi} \end{equation}

\(\mu\) is the behaviour policy. In textbook PPO \(\mu = \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), the policy at the start of the update, so \(\rho_t(\theta_{\text{old}}) = 1\). The PPO paper writes this ratio as \(r_t(\theta)\); this tour writes \(\rho_t\) because \(r_t\) is the reward. (If you are used to \(\mu\) as a cost mean, note that here it is always the behaviour policy.) The weight is the same importance weight you know from off-policy evaluation, applied to one action at a time. The superscript CPI is for Kakade and Langford's conservative policy iteration [7, Eq. 6]. Its gradient at \(\theta_{\text{old}}\) is Equation \eqref{eq:pg-adv}, because \(\nabla_\theta \rho_t = \rho_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta\) and \(\rho_t = 1\) there.

So "taking several gradient steps on one batch" means maximising \(\hat L^{\mathrm{CPI}}\). Unconstrained, that maximum puts all probability on positive-advantage actions: the ratio goes to infinity where \(\hat A_t \gt 0\) and to 0 where \(\hat A_t \lt 0\). The rest of the chapter is about how to stop short.

6. Trust regions and TRPO

Schulman and colleagues bounded the error of the surrogate [6, Theorem 1]. Write \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}^{\max}(\pi, \pi') = \max_s D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\pi(\cdot\mid s) \,\|\, \pi'(\cdot\mid s)\big)\) for the worst-case KL divergence between the old and new action distributions over all states, where for distributions \(p\), \(q\) over the same options \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\,\|\,q) = \sum_x p(x) \log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\). Then

\begin{equation} \begin{gathered} J(\pi') \;\ge\; L_\pi(\pi') - C\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}^{\max}(\pi, \pi'), \\ C = \frac{4 \varepsilon \gamma}{(1-\gamma)^2}, \quad \varepsilon = \max_{s,a} \lvert A^\pi(s,a)\rvert . \end{gathered} \label{eq:trpo-bound} \end{equation}

(TRPO's Equation 9. Its \(\epsilon\) is the largest advantage, not the clip range; this tour writes it \(\varepsilon\) to keep the two apart.) The right-hand side is a minorant: it touches \(J\) at \(\pi' = \pi\) and lies below it elsewhere. Maximise it, and \(J\) cannot go down. Iterating gives monotonic improvement, the same minorise-maximise logic as the EM algorithm.

The bound is far too conservative to use as written: with \(\gamma\) near 1 the constant \(C\) is enormous and the steps would be tiny. In Spire Mentor, where \(\gamma = 1\), it is infinite. TRPO keeps the shape of the idea and replaces the penalty by a constraint on the average KL [6, Eq. 12]:

Definition 5.2 · The trust-region problem (TRPO)
\begin{equation} \begin{aligned} &\max_\theta\; \mathbb{E}_t\big[ \rho_t(\theta)\, \hat A_t \big] \\ &\text{subject to} \quad \mathbb{E}_t\Big[ D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big( \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot \mid s_t) \,\|\, \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s_t) \big) \Big] \le \delta . \end{aligned} \label{eq:trpo} \end{equation}

Improve the surrogate as much as possible while the policy, averaged over visited states, moves at most \(\delta\) nats.

The trust region is the set of policies within \(\delta\) of the old one: the region where we trust the surrogate. The constraint is in the space of distributions, which fixes the third problem from Section 5: a step is measured by how much it changes behaviour, not by how far it moves the weights.

How TRPO solves it

Linearise the objective around \(\theta_{\text{old}}\): its gradient is \(g\), Equation \eqref{eq:pg-adv}. Expand the KL to second order: it is zero with zero gradient at \(\theta_{\text{old}}\), so \(\mathbb{E}_t[D_{\mathrm{KL}}] \approx \tfrac12 \Delta\theta^\top F \Delta\theta\), where \(F\) is the Fisher information matrix of the policy, \(F = \mathbb{E}\big[\nabla_\theta \log\pi_\theta\, \nabla_\theta \log\pi_\theta^\top\big]\). Maximising \(g^\top \Delta\theta\) under \(\tfrac12 \Delta\theta^\top F \Delta\theta \le \delta\) has a closed form:

\begin{equation} \Delta\theta = \sqrt{\frac{2\delta}{g^\top F^{-1} g}}\; F^{-1} g . \label{eq:npg} \end{equation}

The direction \(F^{-1}g\) is the natural gradient of Kakade [4]: the steepest-ascent direction when distance is measured in KL rather than in Euclidean parameter distance. The step length comes from solving \(\tfrac12 \beta^2 s^\top F s = \delta\) for \(s = F^{-1}g\) (TRPO's Appendix C). TRPO never forms \(F\). It computes \(F^{-1}g\) with conjugate gradient, using Fisher-vector products, and then runs a line search that shrinks the step until the surrogate improves and the exact KL is below \(\delta\).

TRPO works, and it is awkward. It needs second-order machinery, it does not mix easily with architectures that share parameters between policy and value, and each update is one large step rather than many cheap minibatch steps. That awkwardness is what PPO set out to remove.

7. PPO's clipped objective

Schulman, Wolski, Dhariwal, Radford and Klimov's PPO [7] asks: can we get most of TRPO's protection with first-order steps on minibatches? Their main answer changes the objective, not the optimiser.

Definition 5.3 · The clipped surrogate
\begin{equation} \begin{aligned} L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}(\theta) &= \mathbb{E}_t\big[ L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}_t(\theta) \big], \\ L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}_t(\theta) &= \min\big( \rho_t(\theta)\, \hat A_t,\; \operatorname{clip}(\rho_t(\theta),\, 1-\epsilon,\, 1+\epsilon)\, \hat A_t \big). \end{aligned} \label{eq:clip} \end{equation}

\(\epsilon\) is the clip range, 0.2 in the paper and in Spire Mentor. \(\operatorname{clip}(x, l, u)\) limits \(x\) to \([l, u]\).

The first term inside the min is the CPI surrogate. The second is the same with the ratio held to the band \([1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon]\). The min makes \(L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}\) a pessimistic version of \(L^{\mathrm{CPI}}\): it equals it near \(\rho = 1\), and it ignores changes in the ratio that would make the objective look better than the band allows.

Trace one row

Return to the fight. The old policy gave Strike 0.30 and sampled it, and the advantage came out \(\hat A = +1.5\). The minibatch steps raise Strike's probability.

  • At 0.33, the ratio is 1.1. Inside the band: both terms equal \(1.1 \times 1.5 = 1.65\), and the gradient pushes Strike up, exactly as in Equation \eqref{eq:pg-adv} weighted by \(\rho\).
  • At 0.36, the ratio is 1.2. This is the edge.
  • At 0.40, the ratio is 1.33. The unclipped term is \(2.0\), the clipped term is \(1.2 \times 1.5 = 1.8\), and the min picks 1.8, which is constant in \(\theta\). This row's gradient is now zero. Other rows keep training, and the shared weights may still move Strike's probability, but this row no longer pulls it.

Now suppose the advantage had been \(\hat A = -1.0\). The steps lower Strike. Once its probability falls below \(0.8 \times 0.30 = 0.24\), the clipped term \(-0.8\) is larger than the unclipped one, the min picks the clipped term, and the row's gradient is zero again.

What happens on the other side of 1? If an advantage is positive but some other row's update has lowered Strike to a ratio of 0.7, the unclipped term \(0.7 \times 1.5 = 1.05\) is smaller than the clipped \(0.8 \times 1.5 = 1.2\). The min picks the unclipped term, and the gradient pushes Strike back up. The clip only removes the incentive to move further in the direction the advantage wants. It never stops a correction of a move that went the wrong way.

Written as a gradient, the clip is a mask:

\begin{equation} \nabla_\theta L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}_t = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } \hat A_t \gt 0 \text{ and } \rho_t \gt 1+\epsilon, \\ 0 & \text{if } \hat A_t \lt 0 \text{ and } \rho_t \lt 1-\epsilon, \\ \rho_t\, \hat A_t\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases} \label{eq:clip-grad} \end{equation}

Compared with TRPO this is crude. It does not bound the KL. It bounds, row by row, how much each sampled action's probability is rewarded for moving, and only for the sampled action. A policy can move far on the options it did not take, and many small moves on shared weights can still add up. That is why PPO implementations also watch the KL and stop early when it grows, as we will see Spire Mentor do. The PPO paper also tried a KL penalty with an adaptive coefficient \(\beta\) and found the clip worked better [7, §4].

The dual clip

Look at the right panel of Figure 5.2 for large \(\rho\). If a row has a negative advantage but its action's probability has grown far beyond the old one, the min picks the unclipped term \(\rho \hat A\), which is unbounded below. One such row can dominate a minibatch's gradient. Ye and colleagues' dual clip [9] puts a floor under it, with \(L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}_t\) the per-row term of Equation \eqref{eq:clip}:

\begin{equation} L^{\mathrm{DUAL}}_t = \begin{cases} \max\big( L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}_t,\; c\, \hat A_t \big) & \text{if } \hat A_t \lt 0, \\ L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}_t & \text{if } \hat A_t \ge 0, \end{cases} \label{eq:dual} \end{equation}

with a constant \(c \gt 1\), set to 3 in their paper and in Spire Mentor. For \(\hat A_t \lt 0\) the term never falls below \(3\hat A_t\), so a row whose ratio passed 3 stops contributing.

The whole loss

A PPO learner trains the policy and the critic together, usually through one loss. The paper's Equation 9 [7] maximises

\begin{equation} \begin{aligned} L^{\mathrm{CLIP+VF+S}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t\Big[ &L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}_t(\theta) - c_1 \big( V_\phi(s_t) - V_t^{\text{targ}} \big)^2 \\ &+ c_2\, \mathcal{H}\big[\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s_t)\big] \Big], \end{aligned} \label{eq:ppo-full} \end{equation}

where the middle term is the critic's squared error against its target (Chapter 4), \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) are weights, and \(\mathcal{H}\) is the entropy of the action distribution, the subject of the next section. Then the algorithm is short:

  1. Play the current policy and store each decision: observation, action, \(\log \mu(a_t \mid s_t)\), reward.
  2. Compute values, advantages \(\hat A_t\) and value targets with the critic (Chapter 4).
  3. For \(K\) epochs, shuffle the batch into minibatches and take one optimiser step on \(-L^{\mathrm{CLIP+VF+S}}\) per minibatch.
  4. Discard the batch. Go to 1.

Step 4 is the on-policy rule. The batch is used for a few epochs and then thrown away, never replayed like a DQN buffer.

Pause and predict: in the first minibatch of the first epoch, what is the clip fraction?

Zero. Before any step, \(\pi_\theta = \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), so every ratio is exactly 1 (up to float rounding and, in Spire Mentor, up to the gap between the update-start policy and the acting policy). Rows can only leave the band after some steps. That is why a learner's clip fraction, averaged over all minibatches of an update, is small even when the last minibatches clip often.

8. The entropy bonus

The entropy of the policy in a state is \(\mathcal{H}[\pi(\cdot\mid s)] = -\sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}(s)} \pi(a\mid s) \log \pi(a\mid s)\), in nats. For Strike, Defend, Bash at \((0.3, 0.2, 0.5)\) it is 1.03 nats. It is largest for the uniform distribution, \(\log \lvert \mathcal{A}(s)\rvert\) (1.10 nats for three options), and zero for a certain choice.

A policy gradient method explores only through the randomness of its own policy. Chapter 2 contrasted this with epsilon-greedy, where exploration is added from outside. If the policy becomes certain early, it stops sampling the alternatives, never sees their advantages, and has no gradient to bring them back. Adding \(\beta\, \mathcal{H}\) to the objective rewards keeping some probability spread. A3C made the bonus standard, crediting Williams and Peng (1991) with the idea and reporting that it discourages premature convergence to a deterministic policy [10]. Its gradient for a softmax pulls every logit towards the log-probability-weighted mean, flattening the distribution. The coefficient trades exploration against exploitation and is usually small: Spire Mentor uses 0.01 for combat decisions and 0.0125 for the rest at the start.

Spire Mentor divides each state's entropy by \(\log\lvert\mathcal{A}(s)\rvert\):

\begin{equation} \bar{\mathcal{H}}(s) = \frac{\mathcal{H}[\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s)]}{\log \lvert \mathcal{A}(s) \rvert} \in [0, 1] . \label{eq:hnorm} \end{equation}

Without that, a decision with 30 options (a map, a shop) would earn more bonus than one with 2 at the same level of indecision. The normalised entropy is also what the learner logs, so 1.0 means uniform play and 0 means a deterministic policy.

9. Reading the diagnostics

A PPO learner logs many numbers. Four of them tell you whether the update did what it should.

KL between the old and new policy

The quantity TRPO constrains, \(\mathbb{E}_s\big[D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\text{old}}(\cdot \mid s) \,\|\, \pi_{\text{new}}(\cdot \mid s))\big]\), measures how much one update changed behaviour. It can be computed exactly by running the old and new networks on a sample of states and summing over all options. Most implementations estimate it from the sampled actions alone, because the per-row log-probabilities are already there. With \(\rho = \pi_{\text{new}}(a\mid s)/\pi_{\text{old}}(a\mid s)\) and \(a \sim \pi_{\text{old}}\), two estimators are common:

\begin{equation} k_1 = -\log \rho, \qquad k_3 = (\rho - 1) - \log \rho . \label{eq:kl-est} \end{equation}

Both are unbiased for \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\text{old}} \,\|\, \pi_{\text{new}})\): the expectation of \(-\log\rho\) under \(\pi_{\text{old}}\) is the KL by definition, and \(\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\text{old}}}[\rho - 1] = \sum_a \pi_{\text{new}}(a\mid s) - 1 = 0\), so \(k_3\) adds a zero-mean control variate (the baseline idea again). \(k_1\) can be negative on a single row; \(k_3\) cannot, because \(x - 1 - \log x \ge 0\). Spinning Up logs the mean of \(k_1\) as "approx KL" [11]; Spire Mentor logs the mean of \(k_3\) and also the exact KL on a fixed probe of rows. The names \(k_1\), \(k_3\) follow a blog post by Schulman, which we could not retrieve to check.

How to read it: the PPO paper's Figure 2 shows the clipped objective peaking at a KL of about 0.02 on its first Hopper update [7], and common targets sit around 0.01 to 0.02 nats per update. A KL far below that means the learning rate, not the clip, is limiting the step. A KL near 1 nat or above means the policy changed so much that the batch no longer describes it. The estimators fail first in that regime: \(k_3\) grows like \(\rho\) when ratios are large, so an exploding approximate KL can be an artefact as well as a symptom.

Clip fraction

The clip fraction is the share of rows with \(\lvert \rho_t - 1\rvert \gt \epsilon\), averaged over the update's minibatches. It says how often the clip, rather than the learning rate, decided a row's gradient. Near zero: the clip is idle and the optimiser's step size is the limit. A few percent: the clip trims the largest moves. Large values: the minibatch steps are trying to move much further than the band allows, and the update is mostly zeroed gradients.

Entropy

Watch its direction and speed. A learning policy becomes more decided, so normalised entropy falls slowly from near 1. A fall to near 0 within a few updates is a collapse: the policy has committed before it has explored, and the entropy bonus was not enough to hold it.

Explained variance

The critic is a regression of value targets on states. Its explained variance over the batch is

\begin{equation} \mathrm{EV} = 1 - \frac{\operatorname{Var}\big[ V^{\text{targ}}_t - V_\phi(s_t) \big]}{\operatorname{Var}\big[ V^{\text{targ}}_t \big]} , \label{eq:ev} \end{equation}

the \(R^2\) of the critic against its own targets. 1 is perfect, 0 is no better than predicting the batch mean, and negative is worse than that. Advantages are differences of critic outputs (Chapter 4), so a critic with EV near zero gives advantages that are mostly noise, and the policy gradient inherits the noise. A rising EV early in training is the critic learning; a falling EV late is often the policy changing faster than the critic can follow.

10. The textbook version against the engineered version

Real PPO implementations differ from Equation \eqref{eq:ppo-full} in many small ways, and these choices matter. Engstrom and colleagues showed that much of PPO's advantage over TRPO in benchmark results came from such implementation details (value clipping, reward scaling, learning-rate annealing, orthogonal initialisation), not from the clip itself [12]. The departures that matter most:

  • Advantage normalisation. Advantages are standardised per batch or by running moments. As the predict box in Section 4 showed, this rescales without changing the direction.
  • Gradient-norm clipping. The global gradient norm is clipped (often at 0.5 or 1.0) before each step. This is a second step limit that has nothing to do with the policy's distributions.
  • KL early stopping. Many implementations stop the remaining epochs when the approximate KL passes a threshold, a TRPO-like guard on top of the clip. Spinning Up stops at 1.5 times a target KL [11].
  • Asynchronous data. Large systems collect the next batch while training on the last one, so some rows come from a policy one update older than \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\). Then \(\mu \ne \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), and the ratio must be split: clip relative to a proximal policy (the weights at the update's start), and correct for the behaviour policy with a separate importance weight. Hilton, Cobbe and Schulman call this decoupled PPO [8]. Chapter 6 owns that correction.
  • No value clipping. Many implementations clip the critic's update like the policy's. Spire Mentor's plan removes it explicitly.

11. In Spire Mentor

In Spire Mentor · one iteration of the learner

Rollout workers play runs in the Rust simulator, sampling every decision from the current network. Each stored decision carries the tokens the network saw, the option it took, and \(\log\mu(a_t\mid s_t)\), the log-probability under the network that acted. When an iteration's batch is full (131,072 decisions in the campaign), the learner hands it to the update and keeps playing with the weights it has.

The update first runs a pre-pass with no gradient: it scores every row with the weights as they are at the update's start, recording the critic's value and \(\log\pi_{\text{prox}}(a_t\mid s_t)\). Those are \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) of this chapter. It then computes returns and advantages (Chapter 4), normalises advantages per decision type, and runs 2 epochs. Each epoch splits the batch into minibatches of about 8,192 rows (24 strata of rows of similar length) and takes one AdamW step per minibatch, after clipping the gradient norm at 1.0. After each epoch it measures the exact KL from the update-start policy on a fixed probe of 8,192 rows.

The loss itself is short. Here is the policy part, with the tour's symbols in the comments added for this excerpt:

Mapped to the chapter's symbols: ratio is \(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{old}}\), the ratio the clip acts on; w is \(\pi_{\text{old}}/\mu\), clamped at 2, so that w * ratio is the full importance ratio \(\rho_t = \pi_\theta/\mu\) of Equation \eqref{eq:cpi}. When the actors and the learner are in step, \(\mu = \pi_{\text{old}}\), \(w = 1\), and this is textbook PPO with the dual clip. The entropy term is \(\beta\bar{\mathcal{H}}\), Equation \eqref{eq:hnorm}, and the value term is \(0.5\,(V_\phi - V^{\text{targ}})^2\) with no value clipping. The total loss per minibatch is

\begin{equation} \begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}(\theta) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{t \in \text{mb}} \Big[ &-w_t\, L^{\mathrm{DUAL}}_t(\theta) + 0.5 \big( V_\phi(s_t) - V^{\text{targ}}_t \big)^2 \\ &- \beta_t\, \bar{\mathcal{H}}(s_t) \Big], \end{aligned} \label{eq:sm-loss} \end{equation}

where \(N\) is the number of trained rows in the minibatch and \(\beta_t\) is the entropy coefficient of row \(t\)'s decision type. In the campaign a teacher term is added in the early phases (Chapter 7).

Two choices stand out. First, the KL guard. The plan records that an earlier campaign stopped epochs on a per-minibatch KL over all rows, and that this stop "fired in 60-86% of iterations"; the author replaced it with an exact KL on a fixed probe after each epoch, and an adaptive learning rate that aims the KL at 0.005 to 0.02. The author's learning check is the test that PPO works at all on this game: train from random weights with no teacher, and pass if the mean floor of the last 10,000 finished runs beats the first 10,000 by at least 1.0. On the training machine it ran for 15 minutes on 7 million decisions and the floor rose from about 2 to about 14.6.

Does the policy improve?

Pause and predict: from random weights, with no teacher, what mean floor does the clipped arm reach after about 345,000 decisions? And does the unclipped arm, which takes ten times larger steps, get there faster?

The clipped arm went from 2.04 (first 1,000 runs) to 7.78 (last 1,000), and passed the learning check by a wide margin. The unclipped arm did climb faster at first, reaching floor 6 within 7 iterations, and then fell apart: its last 1,000 runs averaged 2.39, and it failed the check. Large steps can win early, because a random policy is so bad that almost any confident move helps. They cannot keep a policy that the next step can destroy.

How far does each update move the policy?

The clipped arm's median KL was 0.0029 nats per update. For a three-option decision it is the move from \((0.50, 0.30, 0.20)\) to about \((0.53, 0.28, 0.19)\). Its clip fraction tells us why it is small: a median of 1.4% of rows, with a maximum of 8.5%. The learning rate and the gradient-norm clip set the step, and the policy never came near the edge of the trust region that the clip defines.

Measured · the clip at a large step size

The clipped-fast arm ran only 9 iterations, 73,792 decisions. It ran later than the other two, while other jobs on the machine cut its throughput to about 90 decisions per second. So it cannot say whether the clip keeps a fast learner improving for 345,000 decisions. It does answer the narrower question. With ten times the learning rate and twice the epochs, its updates had an exact KL of 0.011 nats at the median and 0.016 at most, against 4.9 and 52 for the same settings without the clip. Its clip fraction had a median of 12%, against 1.4% for the clipped arm at the default learning rate: the clip now did the limiting that the learning rate did before. Its entropy fell only from 0.99 to 0.94. The mean floor of each iteration's finished runs rose from 1.81 to 3.86, ahead of the clipped arm at the same number of decisions (1.55) and behind the unclipped arm's early jump (5.65). At this length its learning-check windows (first and last 1,000 runs, 2.07 and 2.86) overlap heavily, so its failed verdict says nothing yet.

Entropy and the critic

The two panels belong together. In the clipped arm the policy becomes more decided at a pace the critic can follow. Explained variance starts below zero (a fresh critic is worse than the batch mean) and rises past 0.5 once the policy is stable enough for its returns to be predictable. In the unclipped arm the critic is chasing a target that changes completely every iteration. Its advantages are close to noise, and a policy trained on noise with large steps collapses: normalised entropy as low as 0.07, a policy that has almost stopped exploring.

Pause and predict: the learner also logs the approximate KL \(k_3\) of Equation \eqref{eq:kl-est}, from the sampled actions. In the unclipped arm, how does its median compare with the exact KL's median of 4.9 nats?

It is 315,268 nats: five orders of magnitude off. \(k_3 = (\rho - 1) - \log\rho\) grows linearly in \(\rho\), and once a few rows have ratios in the millions (the unclipped arm's clip fraction counts rows with \(\rho \gt 10^6\), and up to 1.4% of rows qualified), they dominate the mean. In the clipped arm the two agree to a factor of two (median \(k_3\) 0.0015 against exact 0.0029). An approximate KL is a good diagnostic when the policy moves a little, which is when you need it. When it explodes, the precise value means nothing; the explosion is the message.

Throughput

From its second iteration to its last, the clipped arm stored 494 decisions per second of wall time, with the unclipped arm running beside it (that arm ran at 310 per second, because its 4 epochs make each update twice as long). For scale, the campaign log records the author's own learning checks: on a laptop with 6 workers, 7.0 million decisions in 45 minutes (about 2,600 per second); on the training box, 7.09 million in 15 minutes (about 7,900 per second). Our runs shared this Mac with other heavy jobs, so their throughput is a lower bound.

13. Common confusions

"The policy loss should go down." In supervised learning the loss is a measure of quality. The PPO loss is not. Its value depends on the advantages of this batch, which are normalised to mean zero and change every iteration. A policy can improve while the logged loss wanders around zero. Judge the update by the return, the KL and the entropy, not by the loss.

"The advantage is a label, so this is behaviour cloning." The sampled action is the label and the advantage is the weight. Negative weights push the label's probability down, which never happens in cloning. And the dataset was produced by the model being trained, so it must be re-collected after each update. Chapter 7 compares the two directly.

"PPO bounds the KL." It bounds the incentive to move each sampled action's probability beyond the band. It says nothing about unsampled actions, and many small moves through shared weights can add up. Our clipped arm stayed far inside any reasonable KL bound because its learning rate was small, not because the clip enforced one. Implementations that want a KL bound add early stopping, as the campaign does.

"A replay buffer would make this more sample-efficient, like DQN." DQN's target, \(r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s', a')\), is valid for any transition, whoever collected it, because the max does not depend on the behaviour policy. The policy gradient is an expectation under \(\pi_\theta\) itself. Old data can be used only with importance weights, whose variance grows quickly as the policies drift apart. PPO uses each batch for a few epochs, within the trust region, and then discards it. Chapter 6 shows how far Spire Mentor stretches that rule.

"Low entropy means the policy has learned." Low entropy means the policy is sure. In our unclipped arm, entropy fell to 0.07 while the floor fell to 1.5. Certainty that comes faster than the critic's explained variance rises is a collapse, not learning.

14. Suggested experiments

All commands run from the fork's root in a git worktree.

  1. Rerun the two arms. This reproduces Figures 5.4 to 5.7 with a different seed. Compare the clipped arm's KL range and the unclipped arm's collapse point.

  2. Isolate the clip. Run the clipped-fast arm alone, and compare its KL and floor with the unclipped arm's. If its KL stays near the clipped arm's, the clip did the work.
