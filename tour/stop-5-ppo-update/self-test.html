Questions on Chapter 5, Policy gradients and PPO.

Recall

  1. The derivation of the policy gradient starts from \(\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau)\), where \(p_\theta(\tau)\) includes the game's dynamics \(P(s_{t+1}, r_t \mid s_t, a_t)\). Why does \(P\) disappear from the gradient, and what does its disappearance let the method do?

    Answer

    The log turns the product in \(p_\theta(\tau)\) into a sum. Only the \(\pi_\theta\) factors depend on \(\theta\), so the gradient of \(\log p(s_0)\) and of every \(\log P\) term is zero. What is left is \(\sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\). The method therefore needs no model of the game and no gradient through one. That is why the same estimator works for a card game, a robot and a language model. (Section 2)

  2. The first form of the gradient, straight out of the derivation, weights every action by the whole run's return \(G_0\). Definition 5.1 weights action \(a_t\) only by \(G_t\). Why is it correct to drop the rewards that arrived before step \(t\)?

    Answer

    Pair the score of \(a_t\) with one earlier reward \(r_k\), \(k \lt t\). Once you condition on everything up to \(s_t\), \(r_k\) is fixed, and the only randomness left is \(a_t \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s_t)\). The score function has mean zero under the policy that defines it: \[\sum_a \pi_\theta \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta = \nabla_\theta \sum_a \pi_\theta = \nabla_\theta 1 = 0.\] So each past reward contributes nothing in expectation. Dropping them leaves the reward to go, \(\gamma^t G_t\). An action cannot cause a reward that came before it. (Section 2)

  3. Explain in your own words why one policy-gradient step is weighted maximum likelihood on the network's own actions. Then say where the analogy with supervised learning breaks.

    Answer

    For a softmax, the gradient of \(\log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\) with respect to the logits is \(\mathbb{1}[a' = a] - \pi_\theta(a' \mid s)\). For the sampled Strike at probabilities \((0.3, 0.2, 0.5)\) that is \((+0.7, -0.2, -0.5)\), the negative gradient of cross-entropy with Strike as the label. The policy gradient makes the same move scaled by the return, or later by \(\hat A_i\). So the sampled action is the label and the return is the example weight. The analogy breaks because in supervised learning the labels do not depend on the model. Here the network chose the actions, so the dataset changes whenever \(\theta\) changes. (Section 2)

  4. Why is REINFORCE so noisy with Spire Mentor's rewards, and why does subtracting a baseline \(b(s)\) leave the gradient unbiased? What did Figure 5.1 show the baseline \(b = V\) buys?

    Answer

    Every run reaches floor 1, so almost every return is positive. REINFORCE then pushes up almost every action it samples, good or bad, and relies on good runs getting a slightly larger push. A baseline that depends on the state but not the action multiplies a score with mean zero, so \(\mathbb{E}[b(s)\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta] = b(s)\cdot 0 = 0\). It changes the variance but not the mean. In the three-option example the variance fell from 0.039 with no baseline to 0.00062 with \(b = V = 0.265\), 63 times smaller, while the mean gradient's norm stayed 0.022. That has the same effect as collecting 63 times more data. (Sections 3 and 4)

  5. In supervised learning, a step that is too large is repaired by the next step. The chapter gives three reasons a bad step does more damage in policy optimisation. What are they?

    Answer

    First, the data moves with the policy. If a bad step stops the policy playing Bash, the next batch has no Bash, and no gradient pushes it back up. Second, data is expensive, so you want many gradient steps per batch, but the gradient holds only at the \(\theta\) that collected the batch. Repeated steps on \(L^{\mathrm{PG}}\) drive every positive-advantage action towards probability 1. Third, distance in weights is not distance in behaviour. Adding 1 to a logit barely moves a softmax at \((5, 0, 0)\) and moves it a lot at \((0, 0, 0)\). (Section 5)

  6. TRPO's Theorem 1 gives a lower bound on \(J(\pi')\) with a penalty \(C\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}^{\max}\). Why can Spire Mentor not use that bound directly, and what does TRPO optimise instead?

    Answer

    The constant is \(C = 4\varepsilon\gamma/(1-\gamma)^2\). With \(\gamma\) near 1 it is huge and the steps would be tiny, and with Spire Mentor's \(\gamma = 1\) it is infinite. TRPO keeps the idea and swaps the penalty for a constraint. It maximises the surrogate \(\mathbb{E}_t[\rho_t(\theta)\hat A_t]\) subject to an average KL from the old policy of at most \(\delta\). Because the constraint is between distributions, it limits the change in behaviour rather than the move in weights. TRPO solves it with the natural gradient \(F^{-1}g\), found by conjugate gradient, and a line search. (Section 6)

  7. Take a row with a positive advantage. What does the clip do to that row's gradient when \(\rho \gt 1.2\), and what does it do when another row's update has pushed \(\rho\) below 1? Given your answer, does PPO bound the KL between the old and new policy?

    Answer

    Above \(1+\epsilon = 1.2\) the min picks the clipped term, which does not depend on \(\theta\), so the row gives no gradient. Below \(\rho = 1\) the two terms are equal and the min picks the unclipped one, so the gradient pushes the action back up. The clip removes the reward for moving further in the advantage's direction but never blocks the correction of a wrong move. It does not bound the KL. It limits only the sampled action's incentive to move. Options that were not sampled can still move far, and many small moves through shared weights can add up. Implementations that need a KL bound add early stopping. (Sections 7 and 13)

  8. What failure does the dual clip fix, and with \(c = 3\), at what ratio does a negative-advantage row stop giving gradient?

    Answer

    For a negative advantage, the min picks the unclipped term \(\rho\hat A\) above \(\rho = 1\), and that term has no lower bound. A row whose action's probability has grown far beyond the old one can then dominate a minibatch's gradient. The dual clip takes \(\max(L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}_t, c\hat A_t)\) for \(\hat A_t \lt 0\), so the term never falls below \(3\hat A_t\). Once the ratio passes 3, the row gives no gradient. (Section 7)

  9. Why does a policy-gradient method need an entropy bonus at all, and why does Spire Mentor divide each state's entropy by \(\log\lvert\mathcal{A}(s)\rvert\)?

    Answer

    A policy gradient method explores only through its own randomness. If the policy becomes certain too early, it stops sampling the other options, never sees their advantages, and gets no gradient to bring them back. The bonus \(\beta\mathcal{H}\) rewards keeping some probability on the other options. The largest possible entropy is \(\log\lvert\mathcal{A}(s)\rvert\), so without the division a 30-option map or shop decision would earn more bonus than a 2-option decision at the same level of indecision. The normalised entropy lies in \([0, 1]\), where 1 means uniform play. (Section 8)

  10. Spire Mentor logs \(k_3 = (\rho - 1) - \log\rho\) as an approximate KL. Why is \(k_3\) unbiased, and why did it read a median of 315,268 nats in the unclipped arm when the exact KL's median was 4.9?

    Answer

    \(k_1 = -\log\rho\) has expectation equal to the KL under \(\pi_{\text{old}}\) by definition. \(k_3\) adds \(\rho - 1\), whose expectation under \(\pi_{\text{old}}\) is \(\sum_a \pi_{\text{new}} - 1 = 0\). That is a control variate, the baseline idea again. \(k_3\) grows linearly in \(\rho\), and in the unclipped arm up to 1.4% of rows had ratios above \(10^6\). Those rows dominate the mean. An approximate KL is a usable diagnostic while the policy moves a little, and when it explodes, the explosion itself tells you the update failed. (Sections 9 and 12)

  11. Why does Spire Mentor use decoupled PPO? In the learner's code, what are ratio and w, and which one does the clip act on?

    Answer

    The workers keep playing with the previous weights while an update runs. A combat row's \(\mu\) lags the update-start policy \(\pi_{\text{old}}\) by about one update, and run rows lag a mean of 3.7 updates. So \(\mu \ne \pi_{\text{old}}\). ratio is \(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{old}}\), and the clip and dual clip act on it alone. w is \(\pi_{\text{old}}/\mu\), clamped above at 2, and it multiplies the clipped term from outside. When the actors and learner are in step, \(w = 1\) and the code is textbook PPO with the dual clip. (Sections 10 and 11)

  12. Describe what the clipped and unclipped arms did over training, and use explained variance and entropy to explain why the unclipped arm collapsed. What question does the clipped-fast arm answer that the other two cannot?

    Answer

    The clipped arm's mean floor rose from 2.04 to 7.78 and passed the learning check. The unclipped arm reached floor 6 within 7 iterations, then collapsed, and its last 1,000 runs averaged 2.39. Its policy moved several nats per update, so the critic's target likely changed almost completely each time. Its explained variance never passed 0.3, its advantages were close to noise, and its entropy fell as low as 0.07. The unclipped arm changed three things at once: no clip, 10 times the learning rate and 4 epochs. The clipped-fast arm keeps the larger step and puts the clip back. Its KL stayed at 0.011 at the median, against 4.9 without the clip, and its clip fraction rose to 12%, so the clip, not the learning rate, limited its step. (Section 12)

Calculations

  1. The update-start policy gave Strike probability 0.30 and sampled it. The advantage is \(\hat A = +1.5\) and \(\epsilon = 0.2\). Compute the row's term \(L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}_t\) and say whether the row gives a gradient in two cases: (a) Strike's probability has risen to 0.40, and (b) another row's update has lowered Strike's ratio to 0.7.

    Answer

    (a) The ratio is \(\rho = 0.40/0.30 = 1.33\). The unclipped term is \(1.33 \times 1.5 = 2.0\). The ratio clips to 1.2, so the clipped term is \(1.2 \times 1.5 = 1.8\). The min is 1.8. It does not depend on \(\theta\), so the row gives no gradient.

    (b) The unclipped term is \(0.7 \times 1.5 = 1.05\). The ratio clips to 0.8, so the clipped term is \(0.8 \times 1.5 = 1.2\). The min is 1.05, the unclipped term, so the row gives a gradient, and the gradient pushes Strike back up. (Section 7)

  2. One decision has three options with \(\pi = (0.5, 0.3, 0.2)\) and expected returns \(G = (0.30, 0.25, 0.20)\). Compute the baseline \(b = V = \sum_a \pi(a) G_a\), then the weight \(G_a - V\) on each option. What changes compared with weighting each option by \(G_a\)?

    Answer

    \[V = 0.5 \times 0.30 + 0.3 \times 0.25 + 0.2 \times 0.20 = 0.150 + 0.075 + 0.040 = 0.265.\]

    The weights are \(0.30 - 0.265 = +0.035\), \(0.25 - 0.265 = -0.015\) and \(0.20 - 0.265 = -0.065\).

    With weights \(G_a\), every sampled option is pushed up, because every return is positive. With \(G_a - V\), only the first option is pushed up and the other two are pushed down. The shared offset of 0.265 is gone, and the variance of the one-sample gradient drops from 0.039 to 0.00062. (Section 4)
