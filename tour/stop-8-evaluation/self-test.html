Evaluating agents: self-test

Questions on Chapter 8, Evaluating agents.

Answer each question from memory, in writing, before you open its answer.

Come back in a few days and answer all of them again.

Recall questions

  1. Why can't the training curve tell you whether a new network wins more often than the old one? What does a proper evaluation do instead?

    Answer

    The training curve averages games of the sampling policy, on seeds the learner has partly fitted, while the weights are still moving. A curve like that tells you training is alive. It says nothing about one fixed policy. An evaluation freezes the network, plays it on fresh games, and counts the wins. Each game costs about 650 decisions and returns one bit, so the evaluation is an experiment with a sample size, and a small one. (Sections 1 and 11)

  2. Some seeds are easy and some are hard. Why does that variation not appear in \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat p) = p(1-p)/n\)? Where does it show up instead?

    Answer

    A single outcome is a Bernoulli draw, whatever mix of easy and hard seeds produced it. The rate \(p\) is already the average over that mix, so the binomial variance includes the seed variation. This holds when each seed is played once and the seeds are drawn independently. The variation between seeds shows up in the paired comparison. A seed that is easy for one network is usually easy for the other, so \(\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) > 0\), and pairing subtracts that covariance. (Sections 2 and 11)

  3. What goes wrong with the Wald interval for a good agent, and what does Wilson do differently to fix it?

    Answer

    The Wald interval puts \(\hat p\) in place of \(p\) in the variance, so it judges a small count with a variance estimated from that same count. Near 1 it breaks. At 19 wins in 20 it gives \(0.95 \pm 0.096\), which runs past 100%, and at 20 in 20 it has zero width. Wilson keeps the unknown \(p\) in the variance and keeps every \(p\) for which \(\hat p\) lies within \(z\) of that value's own standard errors. That is a quadratic inequality in \(p\), and its two roots are the interval. The centre is \((k + z^2/2)/(n + z^2)\), as if about four extra games, two won and two lost, had been played, and the interval never leaves \([0, 1]\). (Section 2)

  4. In the paired design, what does a seed in cell a or cell d tell you? Why does the precision of the paired estimate depend on the discordance \(\delta_d\) and not on the win rates?

    Answer

    A seed in cell a or d gave both networks the same result. It adds the same amount to both win rates and cancels from the difference, so it says the seed was easy or hard and nothing about which network is better. With \(D_i = X_i - Y_i\), \(D\) is nonzero only on cells b and c, which gives \(\operatorname{Var}(D) = \delta_d - \Delta^2\). If the outcomes were independent, \(\delta_d - \Delta^2\) would equal \(p_1(1-p_1) + p_2(1-p_2)\) and pairing would gain nothing. Pairing helps when the networks disagree less often than independent outcomes would. (Section 3)

  5. The chapter compares pairing to CUPED. What plays the role of the covariate, what is \(\theta\), and what does a correlation \(\rho\) between the two networks' outcomes buy you?

    Answer

    Network 2's result on the same seed is the covariate, and pairing is a control variate with \(\theta = 1\). That is not CUPED's variance-minimising \(\theta\). The ratio of paired to unpaired variance is about \(1 - \rho\), so \(n\) paired seeds are worth about \(n/(1-\rho)\) unpaired seeds per network. Both designs play \(2n\) runs. Pairing only decides which seeds the networks share. The CUPED comparison is the chapter's analogy, since Deng and colleagues do not discuss paired seeds. On the chapter's 600 seeds the phi coefficient was 0.22 and the paired variance was 0.78 times the unpaired variance. (Section 3)

  6. Forty-four discordant seeds split 30 to 14. Why is McNemar's p-value the same whether you played 44 seeds or 4,000? What does change between the two cases?

    Answer

    The test conditions on the number of discordant seeds \(m = b + c\). Under \(H_0 : \pi_b = \pi_c\), each discordant seed falls on either side with probability one half, so \(b \mid m \sim \operatorname{Binomial}(m, \tfrac12)\). The concordant counts \(a\) and \(d\) do not appear, so 3,956 extra concordant seeds add nothing to the test. The exact p-value is about 0.023 in both cases. The effect size changes: \(\hat\Delta = 16/n\) is 36 points at \(n = 44\) and 0.4 points at \(n = 4{,}000\). Power grows with \(n\) only because \(m\) grows with \(n\). (Sections 4 and 11)

  7. In Newcombe's method 8, what does the \(-2\hat\phi\) term do? Which method does the project's code compute, and how was the interval checked?

    Answer

    Each side of the interval combines two Wilson half-widths, the same way the paired variance \(\operatorname{Var}(X) + \operatorname{Var}(Y) - 2\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)\) combines two standard errors. The \(-2\hat\phi\) term subtracts the covariance that pairing removes. With \(\hat\phi = 0\) you get Newcombe's interval for independent samples. The code computes method 8 but labels it method 10, which adds a continuity correction to \(\hat\phi\). On the chapter's table the two give the same interval to one decimal. The project cross-checks the interval with a bootstrap that resamples whole seeds, so both networks' results on a seed stay together. (Sections 4 and 9)

  8. The chapter's sample-size formula reduces to \(\text{MDE}_{80} \approx 2.80\sqrt{\delta_d/n}\). Where does 2.80 come from? What does the formula tell you about planning, and what changes at \(\alpha = 0.01\)?

    Answer

    The 2.80 is \(z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{0.80}\), about 1.96 plus 0.84, at \(\alpha = 0.05\). The formula comes from Connor's sample size after dropping \(\Delta^2\), because a few points squared is small against a discordance of a few tenths. The MDE grows with \(\sqrt{\delta_d}\), so comparing two similar networks is cheap. It shrinks as \(1/\sqrt n\), so halving it costs four times the seeds. The unknown is \(\delta_d\), not \(p\), so a plan has to guess how often the candidate will disagree with the baseline. At \(\alpha = 0.01\) the multiplier becomes \(2.576 + 0.842 = 3.42\), and the same MDE needs about 1.5 times as many seeds. (Section 5)

  9. You pick the best of \(K\) checkpoints on one panel. Why does the shared panel luck \(Z_0\) not bias the pick? Why does the same \(\delta_d\) that sets a paired test's power also set the size of the bias?

    Answer

    \(Z_0\) is the same for every candidate, so it cannot change which one is the maximum. Only each candidate's own luck \(Z_k\) varies with \(k\), which gives a bias of \(\sigma\sqrt{1-\rho}\, e_K\). Two equally good candidates have \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat p_j - \hat p_k) = 2\sigma^2(1-\rho)\), and the paired variance says the same quantity is \(\delta_d/n\). Setting them equal gives optimism \(= e_K\sqrt{\delta_d/2n}\). On the two published networks, random half-splits gave 0.90 points against 1.12 from the formula. The formula assumes equal true rates, the worst case, and these two networks are not equally good. The remedy is one panel to select and a different panel to report. (Section 6)

  10. What is a sealed panel, and why does it matter that it is read only once, even when nobody cheats? How did Spire Mentor enforce the seal?

    Answer

    A sealed panel is played and its results go to a place nobody reads. Only a hash of the results file is published, and the pre-registered scorer reads the panel once, after everything that could depend on it is committed. A panel read to pick a checkpoint, then a variant, then to report has become a selection panel three times over, and its number carries the optimism of a \(K\) that nobody counted. Spire Mentor wrote the TEST records outside every working copy, in a directory only the owner can read. The programs printed only row counts, error counts and a SHA-256 hash, and the runner discarded the progress line that would have shown a win rate. The scorer refuses to read TEST before the unseal time in panels.py. (Sections 7 and 9)

  11. Why must both networks run on one engine build? Why is the pairing tightest early in a run?

    Answer

    Common random numbers assume that the same random inputs reach both networks. A change to the simulator, even a bug fix, can change what a seed produces. The comparison is then unpaired at best and confounded at worst, so the scorer marks a contrast invalid when the builds differ. When the engine changed mid-campaign, every baseline was played again on the new engine (Amendment 6). Within a run, each act's map and its lists of fights come from the seed, so both networks see them. Card rewards, shops, shuffles, enemy moves and unknown rooms draw from streams that advance with play. Once one network takes an extra card reward, its later draws differ, so the pairing weakens as the two games diverge. (Section 8)

  12. A pilot scored bit-identical weights at 43.1% on one panel and 47.2% on another, and called the gap 2.9 standard errors. What is wrong with that figure, and what rule did the project adopt?

    Answer

    Each panel was 200 seeds played at 11 ascension levels, and the 2.9 treats the 2,200 runs as independent. They are not, because a hard seed counted 11 times. The true standard error is larger, and the gap is less surprising than 2.9 sigma suggests. Even a real 2.9-sigma gap may be a rare draw among the many panel comparisons a project makes, or a sign that something else differed between the reads. The rule since then is to compare differences within one panel and never rates across panels. Section 10 shows the reason. Network 2's rate moved 4.0 points between the chapter's two 300-seed panels, with p = 0.2. (Sections 9, 10 and 11)

Calculations

  1. An agent wins 20 of 20 games. Compute the 95% Wilson interval with \(z = 1.96\), so \(z^2 \approx 3.84\). What does the Wald interval give for the same count? Which of a 70% agent and an 85% agent does the result rule out?

    Answer

    Here \(\hat p = 1\), so \(\hat p(1 - \hat p) = 0\).

    Denominator: \(1 + z^2/n = 1 + 3.84/20 = 1.192\).

    Centre: \[(\hat p + z^2/2n)/1.192 = (1 + 0.096)/1.192 = 0.919.\] As a check, \[(k + z^2/2)/(n + z^2) = 21.92/23.84 = 0.919.\]

    Half-width: \[1.96\sqrt{0 + 3.84/(4 \cdot 400)}/1.192 = 1.96 \times 0.049/1.192 = 0.081.\]

    Interval: \(0.919 \pm 0.081\), which is \([0.84, 1]\).

    The Wald interval is \(1 \pm 0\), zero width. Twenty straight wins rule out a 70% agent but not an 85% agent. (Section 2)

  2. The chapter's 600 paired seeds gave \(a = 418\), \(b = 64\), \(c = 78\), \(d = 40\). Compute \(\hat\Delta\), \(\hat\delta_d\) and the paired standard error. The unpaired standard error was 2.24 points. What variance ratio does that give?

    Answer

    \[\hat\Delta = (b - c)/n = (64 - 78)/600 = -0.0233,\] or −2.3 points.

    \[\hat\delta_d = (b + c)/n = 142/600 = 0.237.\]

    \[\operatorname{Var}(\hat\Delta) = (\hat\delta_d - \hat\Delta^2)/n = (0.2367 - 0.0005)/600 = 0.000394.\]

    Standard error: \(\sqrt{0.000394} = 0.0198\), or 1.98 points.

    Variance ratio: \((1.98/2.24)^2 = 3.92/5.02 = 0.78\). That matches \(1 - \rho\) with the measured phi coefficient of 0.22. An unpaired design would need about 28% more seeds to match the paired standard error. (Sections 3 and 10)
