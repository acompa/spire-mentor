Recall questions

  1. Q-learning can train on a replay buffer of week-old transitions with no correction, but a policy gradient cannot use rows from an older network as they are. What is the difference between the two targets that causes this? Then define a row's staleness, name the three ways rows in an asynchronous PPO trainer fall behind the parameters, and explain why row A in Figure 6.1c has staleness 1 while row B has staleness 0.

    Answer

    The Q-learning target \(r_t + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s_{t+1}, a')\) takes the best next action, whatever the logging policy did next, so an old transition is still valid input. The policy gradient is an expectation over trajectories \(\tau \sim \pi_\theta\), drawn by the current network with the parameters the update is about to change. If you average over another policy's actions, each action counts as often as that policy chose it, not as often as \(\pi_\theta\) would.

    Staleness is the number of weight updates between the network that played the row and the network being trained. Rows fall behind because PPO takes several gradient steps per batch, because workers keep playing with the old weights while the learner computes an update, and because some rows wait for the rest of their run before their advantage can be computed. Row A was played by v3 early in iteration 4, before update 3 finished, and update 4 trains on it starting from v4. That is one version apart. Row B was played after serving loaded v4, so it matches the network that trains on it. (Section 1)

  2. In the Corpse Slug decision, network A is playing and B is the target. Why does multiplying each row by \(\pi(a \mid s) / \mu(a \mid s)\) make A's samples average like B's? What must the behaviour policy satisfy for this to work, and why does a softmax policy still cause trouble in practice?

    Answer

    The identity \[\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}[f(x)] = \sum_x q(x)\, \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} f(x) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim q}[w(x) f(x)]\] rewrites an average under the target as an average under the proposal. Each sample is scaled up or down by how much more or less often the target would produce it. The condition is support: \(q(x) \gt 0\) wherever \(p(x) f(x) \neq 0\), so the behaviour policy must be able to produce every outcome the target gives weight to. A softmax never assigns exactly zero, so support holds in principle. The trouble is probabilities that are nearly zero, which produce huge weights. (Section 2)

  3. What does the ordinary importance sampling estimator divide by, what does the self-normalised estimator divide by, and what does each one give up? In the evaluation of B's win rate from A's 48 runs, the plain estimator said \(1.5 \times 10^{-68}\) and the self-normalised one said 0.0%, against a true 83.3%. Explain each failure.

    Answer

    The ordinary estimator \(\hat{I}_{\text{IS}}\) divides by \(n\) and is unbiased. The self-normalised estimator \(\hat{I}_{\text{SN}}\) divides by \(\sum_i w_i\), which adds a bias that vanishes as \(n\) grows and keeps the estimate inside the range of \(f\). The plain estimate is near zero because almost every per-run weight is near zero, and the rare huge weight that would correct the mean did not occur in 48 runs. The self-normalised estimate had one effective run, so it reported that run's outcome, and A lost that run. A's own uncorrected win rate, 70.8%, was closer to the truth than either estimate. (Sections 2 and 11.3)

  4. Say in your own words what the effective sample size \(n_{\text{eff}}\) measures. Why does the chi-squared divergence, and not the KL divergence, set the share of the sample that survives? Why does the chapter call this a rule of thumb for importance sampling?

    Answer

    \(n_{\text{eff}} = (\sum_i w_i)^2 / \sum_i w_i^2\) is the number of equally weighted samples that would give the same variance as the weighted mean. Equal weights give \(n\), and one weight that dwarfs the rest gives about 1. The surviving share tends to \(1 / (1 + \chi^2(p \,\|\, q))\), because the variance of one weight is \(\mathbb{E}_q[w^2] - 1 = \chi^2\). The chi-squared divergence squares the probability ratio, so one outcome that \(p\) favours and \(q\) almost never produces can dominate it. Kish's setting assumes fixed weights uncorrelated with the values, but importance weights are random and can correlate with \(f\). (Section 3)

  5. Why does the environment's transition probability drop out of a whole-trajectory weight? Walk through why the surviving share of a product of \(k\) ratios decays like \(e^{-2k\,\mathrm{KL}}\) for a small gap.

    Answer

    \(P(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t)\) appears in both \(\Pr_\pi(\tau)\) and \(\Pr_\mu(\tau)\), so it cancels and leaves \(W(\tau) = \prod_t \rho_t\). The log of the product is a sum of many small, roughly independent terms, so \(\log W\) is close to normal and \(W\) close to log-normal. If \(\log W \sim \mathcal{N}(m, s^2)\), then \(\mathbb{E}[W] = 1\) forces \(m = -s^2/2\), which gives \(\mathbb{E}[W^2] = e^{s^2}\) and a surviving share of \(e^{-k\sigma^2}\) with \(s^2 = k\sigma^2\). A second-order expansion of \(z = \log \rho\) gives \(\sigma^2 \approx 2\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu \,\|\, \pi)\). (Section 4)

  6. Written state by state, the policy gradient contains two distributions that depend on the policy. Which one does the trainer correct, which one does it leave alone, and why? What two biases does the per-decision gradient \(\hat{g}\) carry as a result?

    Answer

    The two distributions are the state visits \(d_{\pi_\theta}(s)\) and the action choice \(\pi_\theta(a \mid s)\). Correcting the action choice costs one ratio per decision and is exact. Correcting the state visits needs \(d_{\pi_\theta}(s) / d_\mu(s)\), an expectation of the product of every ratio before \(s\), which has the variance problem of Section 4. Following Degris et al., the trainer defines the objective over the states \(\mu\) visits. The first bias is the ignored state-distribution shift. The second is that \(\hat{A}_t\) comes from a critic trained on \(\mu\)'s data, so it estimates an advantage closer to \(\mu\)'s than to \(\pi_\theta\)'s. Both shrink as \(\pi_\theta\) approaches \(\mu\). The trainer relies on keeping the gap small, not on a proven bound. (Section 5)

  7. Replacing \(\rho\) with \(\min(\rho, c)\) trades bias for variance. What exactly is lost, and what does the lost probability mass \(\ell_c(s)\) tell you? A colleague says the clamp is the same thing as PPO's clip. What would you tell them?

    Answer

    The cap leaves alone every action that \(\mu\) plays at least \(1/c\) times as often as \(\pi\), and cuts the others down to \(c\,\mu(a \mid s)\). \(\ell_c(s)\) is the probability removed in state \(s\), and it measures the bias. The gradient now points towards a mix of \(\pi\) and \(\mu\) that under-weights the actions \(\pi\) wants more of. In return the variance is at most \(c\). The clip and the clamp limit different things. The clip limits how far the current parameters may move from \(\pi_{\text{prox}}\) within one update. The clamp limits how much one stale row may count. A row can be inside the clip band with its weight clamped, or outside the band with its weight unclamped. (Sections 6 and 12)

  8. In standard PPO, \(\pi_{\text{old}}\) does two jobs. Name them, and say how decoupled PPO splits the ratio \(\pi_\theta / \mu\). What would happen to stale rows if the clip were centred on \(\mu\) instead of \(\pi_{\text{prox}}\)?

    Answer

    \(\pi_{\text{old}}\) is both the behaviour policy that sampled the data and the centre of the trust region. Decoupled PPO writes \(\pi_\theta / \mu = w_t \cdot u_t\), with \(w_t = \pi_{\text{prox}} / \mu\) a constant per row capped at \(c = 2\), and \(u_t = \pi_\theta / \pi_{\text{prox}}\) the only factor that carries a gradient and is clipped to \(1 \pm \epsilon\). With the clip centred on \(\mu\), a stale row starts the update with its ratio already away from one, possibly outside the band. It then contributes no gradient when its advantage pushes further in the direction it drifted, and an unclipped gradient when its advantage pushes back. The plan estimated about 15% of rows would sit outside the band. (Sections 7 and 12)

  9. V-trace truncates with two levels, \(\bar\rho\) and \(\bar c\). What does each one control? IMPALA's ablation included a "1-step importance sampling" variant. Why does the chapter bring it up, and what does it leave untested?

    Answer

    \(\bar\rho\) decides which value function the target converges to: the value of \(\pi_{\bar\rho}\), the normalised \(\min(\bar\rho\, \mu, \pi)\), which is \(\pi\) when \(\bar\rho = \infty\) and approaches \(\mu\) as \(\bar\rho \to 0\). The product of \(c_i\) controls only how far back a TD error reaches, and so the variance and speed of learning, not the fixed point. Spire Mentor's trainer is close to the 1-step variant: weights on the policy term, GAE advantages with no trace correction, and a cap of 2 instead of 1. That variant matched V-trace at the normal small lag and lost on four of five tasks once a replay buffer made the lag much larger. No ablation in this project tests whether the 1-step approach is adequate here. (Section 8)

  10. Off-policy evaluation and a trainer use the same ratio. Why can a trainer accept a capped, single-ratio, biased correction that would be unacceptable in evaluation?

    Answer

    An evaluation produces one estimate, so it must be correct for any gap, and the gap is set by the question, often a different policy entirely. A trainer takes thousands of steps, and a bad weight costs one noisy gradient step that later steps can undo. The trainer also controls the gap to one or a few updates, each held small by PPO's clip, and its propensities are exact. So it caps the weight, drops the state correction, and keeps the gap small by construction. Learning from someone else's logged play puts you back in the evaluation regime. (Section 9)

  11. Spire Mentor's trainer needs three log-probabilities per row. Where does each one come from? Rows played by teacher networks still train the critic. Why do they stay out of the PPO term?

    Answer

    \(\log \mu\) is logp_behav, stored when the learner samples the action from the serving network's softmax, so it is exact. \(\log \pi_{\text{prox}}\) comes from a no-gradient pre-pass with the current weights before each update, and every epoch of that update reuses it. \(\log \pi_\theta\) is recomputed on every minibatch and is the only one differentiated. Teacher rows train the critic and a supervised teacher term, but the mask pi_m admits only rows the student played to the PPO term. The plan gives no reason. The chapter's likely reason is that a teacher's policy is too far from the student's for one capped ratio. (Sections 9 and 10)

Measured results

  1. In the real learner the clamp never acted. In the A against B experiment, only 2.19% of B's per-decision weights exceeded 2, and the networks rank the same action first on 81.7% of A's decisions. Why is B still useless as a target over whole runs?

    Answer

    In the real learner, staleness averaged 0.10 updates, the median update's 1st and 99th percentiles of the weight were 0.94 and 1.07, and the largest weight was 1.59. The correction there is a small reweighting. For B the problem is the shape of the distribution: most weights sit near one, but the left tail reaches below 0.001 and the right tail reaches 290. Single-decision effective sample size is 16.7% for B against 99.0% at \(\varepsilon = 0.1\), ten ratios leave almost nothing, and per-run it is 2.1%, one run of 48. Top-action agreement does not help because the weight depends on the probabilities rather than the ranking, and on every decision rather than the typical one. (Sections 11.1, 11.2 and 12)

Calculations

  1. In the Corpse Slug decision, A plays Strike with probability 0.146 and Unrelenting with 0.854. B gives 0.506 and 0.494. A is the behaviour policy and B the target. Compute the importance weight for each action. Check that the expected weight under A is one. Then apply a cap of \(c = 2\): give the capped weight on Strike and the lost probability mass \(\ell_2\).

    Answer
    1. Unrelenting: \(\rho = 0.494 / 0.854 = 0.58\).
    2. Strike: \(\rho = 0.506 / 0.146 = 3.47\).
    3. Expected weight under A: \[0.146 \times 3.47 + 0.854 \times 0.58 = 0.507 + 0.495 = 1.00.\]
    4. Capped weight on Strike: \(\min(3.47, 2) = 2\). Unrelenting's 0.58 is below the cap and does not change.
    5. \[\ell_2 = 1 - [\min(0.506,\, 2 \times 0.146) + \min(0.494,\, 2 \times 0.854)] = 1 - (0.292 + 0.494) = 0.214,\] which is \(0.506 - 2 \times 0.146 = 0.21\) of B's probability.

    (Sections 2 and 6)

  2. Take a per-decision KL of 0.003 and the log-normal rule \(n_{\text{eff}}/n \approx e^{-k\sigma^2}\) with \(\sigma^2 \approx 2\,\mathrm{KL}\). Compute the surviving share for one decision and for a 700-decision run. Then use the measured \(\sigma^2 = 0.0110\) at \(\varepsilon = 0.1\) to compute the predicted share for a product of 100 ratios.

    Answer
    1. \(\sigma^2 \approx 2 \times 0.003 = 0.006\).
    2. One decision: \(e^{-0.006} \approx 0.994\), so almost nothing is lost.
    3. 700 decisions: \(k\sigma^2 = 700 \times 0.006 = 4.2\), and \(e^{-4.2} \approx 0.015\), about 1.5% of the sample.
    4. 100 ratios at \(\varepsilon = 0.1\): \(100 \times 0.0110 = 1.1\), and \(e^{-1.1} \approx 0.33\). A hundred ratios cost more than half the sample, as the dashed curve in Figure 6.5 shows.

    (Sections 4 and 11.2)
