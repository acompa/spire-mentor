RL tour · Chapter 6 of 8

Off-policy correction

Off-policy correction reweights data collected by one policy so that it averages like data from another. A policy-gradient trainer needs it because the rows it trains on were always played by a slightly older network than the one it is updating. Spire Mentor gives each row one weight, the ratio of the two networks' probabilities for the action that was played. For a small gap and a single decision that weight is accurate, but multiplied across a whole run of about 700 decisions it becomes useless.

1. Why the data is stale

Q-learning needs no correction for old data, because its target, \(r_t + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s_{t+1}, a')\), takes the best next action whatever the logging policy did next. A replay buffer of week-old transitions is still valid input. A policy gradient is different. Chapter 5 derived it as an expectation over trajectories drawn from the policy being improved, and with \(\gamma = 1\), as in this game, it has no \(\gamma^t\) factor:

\begin{equation} \nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}\Big[ \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\, \hat{A}_t \Big]. \label{eq:pg} \end{equation}

Here \(\tau\) is a trajectory, \(\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\) the probability the current network gives action \(a_t\) in state \(s_t\), and \(\hat{A}_t\) the estimated advantage from Chapter 4. The subscript \(\tau \sim \pi_\theta\) says the average is correct only when \(\pi_\theta\) itself sampled the actions, with the parameters the update is about to change. Average over another policy's actions instead, and each action counts as often as that policy chose it, not as often as \(\pi_\theta\) would.

No practical trainer meets that condition exactly. The data falls behind the parameters in three ways.

  1. Several gradient steps per batch. PPO splits a batch into minibatches and takes a step on each, so after the first step every remaining row was played by a network that no longer exists. Chapter 5's clipped ratio is there to limit this drift.
  2. Asynchronous play. Simulation and learning run at the same time. While the learner computes update \(k\), the workers keep playing with the weights from before it, so their rows reach update \(k+1\) one version late.
  3. Rows that wait. A row whose advantage needs the rest of the run cannot train until those later rows arrive, which can be several updates after it was played.

The policy that generated a row is the behaviour policy, written \(\mu(a \mid s)\). In this chapter \(\mu\) always means a policy, never a mean. The policy you want an expectation under is the target policy. A row's staleness is the number of weight updates between the network that played it and the network being trained. Figures 6.1a to 6.1c show where staleness comes from in an asynchronous trainer.

A synchronous trainer would have no stale rows, but it would stop all simulation during every update. An asynchronous trainer keeps the workers busy, accepts the staleness, and uses importance weights to estimate Equation \eqref{eq:pg} from the stale rows anyway.

2. Importance sampling reweights one decision

Importance sampling estimates an average under one distribution from samples drawn from another. You may know it from off-policy evaluation, where it scores a target policy from logged data. Here it is built in a form that carries over to gradients, starting from one decision in a real game.

In Spire Mentor · one decision

Seed 170,000,000, act 1, floor 3, turn 4. The Ironclad holds four Strikes (6 damage, cost 1) and Unrelenting (14 damage, cost 2), and one Corpse Slug is left with 4 HP, so either card kills it. The game merges the four identical Strikes into one action. That leaves two card choices, plus a potion and End Turn, which both networks ignore. The state and the four probabilities below come from a run whose record is not committed, so treat them as unverified. The arithmetic on them is checked.

The published Ironclad network, call it A, plays Strike with probability 0.146 and Unrelenting with 0.854. The all-character network B gives 0.506 and 0.494. A is playing the run, so A is the behaviour policy \(\mu\). Suppose A samples Unrelenting. To ask what B would have done, you weight the row by \(0.494 / 0.854 = 0.58\), so it counts for less, because B picks Unrelenting less often than A does. Had A sampled Strike, a rare choice for A that B makes often, the weight would be \(0.506 / 0.146 = 3.47\) and the row would count for more.

The general identity is one line. Let \(p\) and \(q\) be two distributions over the same discrete set, and \(f\) any function. Whenever \(q(x) \gt 0\) wherever \(p(x) f(x) \neq 0\),

\begin{equation} \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}[f(x)] = \sum_x p(x)\, f(x) = \sum_x q(x)\, \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\, f(x) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim q}\big[w(x)\, f(x)\big], \qquad w(x) = \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}. \label{eq:is} \end{equation}

The importance weight \(w(x)\) makes samples from the proposal \(q\) average like samples from the target \(p\). The condition on \(q\), called support, says the proposal must be able to produce every outcome the target gives weight to. In a policy setting \(p\) is the target policy and \(q\) is \(\mu\), and for one decision the weight is the importance ratio

\begin{equation} \rho_t = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)}{\mu(a_t \mid s_t)}. \label{eq:rho} \end{equation}

Two facts follow. First, the weight has mean one under the behaviour policy: \(\mathbb{E}_{a \sim \mu}[\rho] = \sum_a \mu(a \mid s)\, \pi(a \mid s) / \mu(a \mid s)\), which is \(\sum_a \pi(a \mid s) = 1\). In the example, A's expected weight is \(0.146 \times 3.47 + 0.854 \times 0.58 = 1.00\), and the two ignored actions add nothing because both networks give them near-zero probability. Second, a softmax never assigns exactly zero, so support always holds in principle. The trouble in practice comes from probabilities that are nearly zero, which produce huge weights, as Section 3 shows.

Given \(n\) independent samples from \(q\), the ordinary importance sampling estimator is the sample mean of Equation \eqref{eq:is}:

\begin{equation} \hat{I}_{\text{IS}} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i\, f(x_i). \label{eq:ois} \end{equation}

It is unbiased. The alternative, which Sutton and Barto call weighted importance sampling [1, §5.5] and statistics texts call the self-normalised estimator [11, ch. 9], divides by the sum of the weights:

\begin{equation} \hat{I}_{\text{SN}} = \frac{\sum_{i} w_i\, f(x_i)}{\sum_{i} w_i}. \label{eq:snis} \end{equation}

Dividing by \(\sum_i w_i\) instead of \(n\) adds a bias that vanishes as \(n\) grows, and it keeps the estimate inside the range of \(f\). If you read Sutton and Barto alongside this chapter, they write \(b\) for the behaviour policy and \(R_{t+1}\) for the reward that this chapter calls \(r_t\), and their \(\rho_{t:T-1}\) is a product of the one-step ratios written \(\rho_t\) here.

3. The weights cost samples

Importance sampling pays for being unbiased with variance. To see how much, take \(f \equiv 1\), so that every estimate should equal one. The variance of a single weight is then

\begin{equation} \operatorname{Var}_{q}[w] = \mathbb{E}_{q}[w^2] - 1 = \sum_x \frac{p(x)^2}{q(x)} - 1 = \chi^2(p \,\|\, q). \label{eq:chi2} \end{equation}

The right-hand side is the chi-squared divergence of \(p\) from \(q\). Unlike the KL divergence, it squares the probability ratio, so one outcome that \(p\) favours and \(q\) almost never produces can dominate it. In the example that outcome is Strike, which A chooses 15% of the time, each time with weight 3.47.

A single number summarises what a set of weights costs. Suppose we average uncorrelated values \(y_i\) with common variance \(\sigma_y^2\) using fixed weights \(w_i\). The variance of the weighted mean is

\begin{equation} \operatorname{Var}\Big[\frac{\sum_i w_i y_i}{\sum_i w_i}\Big] = \sigma_y^2 \frac{\sum_i w_i^2}{\big(\sum_i w_i\big)^2} = \frac{\sigma_y^2}{n_{\text{eff}}}, \qquad n_{\text{eff}} = \frac{\big(\sum_i w_i\big)^2}{\sum_i w_i^2}. \label{eq:ess} \end{equation}

The effective sample size \(n_{\text{eff}}\), from Kish's correction for unequal survey weights [2], is the number of equally weighted samples that would give the same variance. Equal weights give \(n_{\text{eff}} = n\), and one weight that dwarfs the rest gives \(n_{\text{eff}} \approx 1\) however large \(n\) is. Dividing numerator and denominator by \(n^2\) and letting \(n\) grow, the share of the sample that survives is

\begin{equation} \frac{n_{\text{eff}}}{n} \;\to\; \frac{\mathbb{E}_q[w]^2}{\mathbb{E}_q[w^2]} = \frac{1}{1 + \chi^2(p \,\|\, q)}. \label{eq:essshare} \end{equation}

So the chi-squared divergence between the two policies sets the effective sample size. For importance sampling this is a rule of thumb rather than an exact variance, because the weights are random and can correlate with \(f\), and Kish's setting excludes both [11, ch. 9].

4. Products of ratios lose almost every sample

To evaluate a whole run off-policy you need the probability of the whole trajectory. The environment's transitions appear in both the numerator and the denominator and cancel [1, §5.5], leaving a product of one-step ratios:

\begin{equation} W(\tau) = \frac{\Pr_{\pi}(\tau)}{\Pr_{\mu}(\tau)} = \frac{\prod_{t} \pi(a_t \mid s_t)\, P(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t)}{\prod_{t} \mu(a_t \mid s_t)\, P(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t)} = \prod_{t=0}^{T-1} \rho_t. \label{eq:traj} \end{equation}

The variance of this product grows exponentially with its length. To see why, suppose every state had the same divergence \(\chi^2\). Conditioning on the history and applying Equation \eqref{eq:chi2} one step at a time gives

\begin{equation} \mathbb{E}_{\mu}\Big[\prod_{t=0}^{k-1} \rho_t^2\Big] = \big(1 + \chi^2\big)^{k}, \qquad \frac{n_{\text{eff}}}{n} \to \big(1 + \chi^2\big)^{-k}. \label{eq:prodvar} \end{equation}

Real states differ, and a second derivation fits real data better. The log of the product, \(\log W = \sum_t \log \rho_t\), is a sum of many small, roughly independent terms, so it is close to normal and \(W\) is close to log-normal. If \(\log W \sim \mathcal{N}(m, s^2)\), the requirement \(\mathbb{E}[W] = 1\) forces \(m = -s^2/2\), and then \(\mathbb{E}[W^2] = e^{s^2}\). With \(s^2 = k\, \sigma^2\), where \(\sigma^2 = \operatorname{Var}_\mu[\log \rho]\) is the variance of one decision's log ratio,

\begin{equation} \frac{n_{\text{eff}}}{n} \approx e^{-k \sigma^2}, \qquad \sigma^2 \approx 2\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu \,\|\, \pi) \text{ for a small gap.} \label{eq:lognormal} \end{equation}

The second relation comes from a second-order expansion. Write \(z = \log \rho\). The constraint \(\mathbb{E}_\mu[e^{z}] = 1\) gives \(\mathbb{E}[z] \approx -\tfrac12 \mathbb{E}[z^2]\), and \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu \,\|\, \pi) = -\mathbb{E}_\mu[z] \approx \tfrac12 \operatorname{Var}[z]\). By Equation \eqref{eq:lognormal}, then, the surviving share of a product of \(k\) ratios decays like \(e^{-2k\,\mathrm{KL}}\). A per-decision KL of 0.003 costs almost nothing for one decision, but a run of this game is about 700 decisions, and after 700 it leaves \(e^{-4.2} \approx 1.5\%\) of the sample.

Section 11 measures this on real play, with network A playing 48 runs and network B as the target. The per-run weights \(\prod_t \rho_t\) span 185.2 orders of magnitude, from 10−243.0 to 10−57.7. The effective sample size is 2.1% of 48 runs, which is one run. The largest weight holds 100% of the total, and the other 47 runs contribute nothing to the estimate.

5. A gradient can afford only the action correction

Off-policy evaluation estimates a number, \(J(\pi)\), while a trainer needs a direction, \(\nabla_\theta J\). The weight is the same ratio in both cases, but in a gradient the policy enters in two places, and the trainer can afford to correct only one of them.

To find those two places, write Equation \eqref{eq:pg} state by state. With \(\gamma = 1\) and episodes that end, let \(d_\pi(s) = \sum_t \Pr_\pi(s_t = s)\) be the expected number of visits to \(s\) in one episode. Collecting the terms of the sum by state gives

\begin{equation} \nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \sum_s d_{\pi_\theta}(s) \sum_{a} \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\, A^{\pi_\theta}(s, a). \label{eq:pgstate} \end{equation}

Sutton and Barto give the policy gradient theorem in this form [1, §13.2], here with an equality because we did not normalise \(d\). Two distributions appear in it, the state visits \(d_{\pi_\theta}\) and the action choice \(\pi_\theta(a \mid s)\). Correcting the inner sum with an importance weight is exact and cheap:

\begin{equation} \sum_{a} \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\, g(s,a) = \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \mu(\cdot \mid s)}\big[\rho(s,a)\, g(s,a)\big], \qquad g(s,a) = \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\, A^{\pi_\theta}(s,a). \label{eq:actionis} \end{equation}

It costs one ratio per decision, with the variance of Equation \eqref{eq:chi2}. Correcting the outer sum would need \(d_{\pi_\theta}(s) / d_\mu(s)\), which is an expectation, over the paths that reach \(s\), of the product of every ratio before \(s\), as in Equation \eqref{eq:traj}. Practical off-policy actor-critic methods do not estimate that factor. Degris et al. instead define the objective over the states that the behaviour policy visits, and then use those states as sampled [3]:

\begin{equation} \hat{g} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{(s_t, a_t) \sim \mu} \rho_t\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\, \hat{A}_t. \label{eq:offpg} \end{equation}

Equation \eqref{eq:offpg} is the per-decision correction that a trainer can afford, and it has two biases. It ignores the shift in the state distribution, and its \(\hat{A}_t\) comes from a critic trained on \(\mu\)'s data, so it estimates an advantage closer to \(\mu\)'s than to \(\pi_\theta\)'s. Both biases shrink as \(\pi_\theta\) approaches \(\mu\), which is why the correction works for data a few updates old and fails for data from a different policy. Chapter 5's trust-region bound does not cover the state shift. That bound grows with the horizon, and Chapter 5 showed it is infinite at \(\gamma = 1\), so even a small per-decision gap can shift the state distribution over a 700-decision run. The trainer relies on keeping the gap small, not on a proven bound. That this bias stays tolerable is our inference from the trainer's results, not a guarantee.

The weight also multiplies something different. In evaluation it multiplies a return, a number fixed by what happened. In a gradient it multiplies a score function times an advantage, and the advantage already has a baseline subtracted, as Chapter 4 showed. A large weight on a row with \(\hat{A}_t \approx 0\) does little harm, while a large weight on a row with a big advantage moves the parameters a long way on the evidence of one decision.

6. A cap on the weight trades bias for variance

The standard remedy for heavy-tailed weights is to cap them, replacing \(\rho\) by \(\min(\rho, c)\) for a constant \(c \geq 1\). The expectation of any function under the capped weight is again a sum over actions:

\begin{equation} \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \mu}\big[\min(\rho, c)\, g(s,a)\big] = \sum_a \min\big(\pi(a \mid s),\, c\, \mu(a \mid s)\big)\, g(s,a). \label{eq:clampexp} \end{equation}

The cap leaves alone every action that \(\mu\) plays at least \(1/c\) times as often as \(\pi\), and cuts the others down to \(c\,\mu(a \mid s)\). The probability it removes in state \(s\), the lost probability mass, is

\begin{equation} \ell_c(s) = 1 - \sum_a \min\big(\pi(a \mid s),\, c\, \mu(a \mid s)\big), \label{eq:lost} \end{equation}

and it measures the bias. The gradient now points towards a mix of \(\pi\) and \(\mu\) that under-weights exactly the actions \(\pi\) wants more of. In return the variance is bounded, because \(\mathbb{E}_\mu[\min(\rho,c)^2] \le c\, \mathbb{E}_\mu[\min(\rho, c)] \le c\). A smaller \(c\) means more bias and less variance. In the worked example, a cap of 2 cuts B's weight on Strike from 3.47 to 2 and loses \(0.506 - 2 \times 0.146 = 0.21\) of B's probability in that state.

The cap can sit in two places. On the weight in front of a gradient term, it changes which policy the gradient serves. On the weights inside a product of ratios, it changes how far back credit flows, which the IMPALA paper likens to the "trace cutting" of Retrace [6, 8]. Section 8 returns to that second kind.

Neither cap is PPO's clip. PPO clips the ratio \(\pi_\theta / \pi_{\text{old}}\) between the network being trained and a reference network, and Chapter 5 showed that the clip stops the gradient once a step has gone far enough. The importance-weight cap has a different job, which is to limit the variance of a correction for data from another policy.

7. Decoupled PPO separates correction from the trust region

Standard PPO uses one old policy, \(\pi_{\text{old}}\), for two jobs. It is the behaviour policy that sampled the data, and it is the centre of the trust region. Hilton, Cobbe and Schulman showed that the two jobs can go to different policies [4]. The trust region can be centred on any recent policy, which they call the proximal policy \(\pi_{\text{prox}}\), while the behaviour policy is whatever played. Where Chapter 5's PPO ratio was \(\pi_\theta / \pi_{\text{old}}\), the decoupled objective splits the full ratio into two factors:

\begin{equation} \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)}{\mu(a_t \mid s_t)} = \underbrace{\frac{\pi_{\text{prox}}(a_t \mid s_t)}{\mu(a_t \mid s_t)}}_{w_t:\ \text{off-policy correction}} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{\text{prox}}(a_t \mid s_t)}}_{u_t:\ \text{trust region}}. \label{eq:split} \end{equation}

Only the second factor depends on \(\theta\), and only it is clipped. The first is a constant per row, computed once before the update. With this trainer's cap \(c\) on the first factor, the objective is

\begin{equation} L(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(s_t,a_t) \sim \mu}\Big[ \min(w_t, c)\; \min\big( u_t \hat{A}_t,\ \operatorname{clip}(u_t, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\, \hat{A}_t \big) \Big]. \label{eq:decoupled} \end{equation}

Here \(\epsilon\) is PPO's clip range [10], 0.2 in this trainer. At \(\theta = \theta_{\text{prox}}\), \(u_t = 1\) and the gradient of \(u_t\) is \(\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta\), so the first step of Equation \eqref{eq:decoupled} is the off-policy gradient of Equation \eqref{eq:offpg}, with the action correction \(\min(w_t, c)\) and target \(\pi_{\text{prox}}\). Later minibatch steps move \(\theta\) away from \(\pi_{\text{prox}}\), and the clip stops them at \(\pm\epsilon\) in ratio. Hilton et al. write \(r_t(\theta)\) for the ratio that this chapter calls \(u_t\), since this tour keeps \(r_t\) for the reward. Their objective has no cap on the weight, and in their staleness experiments they bounded the ratio only at 100, for numerical stability [4, App. B]. The cap at \(c = 2\) is this trainer's choice. Its plan tags the value with a literature source that is not in the public repository, so the reason cannot be checked. The trainer also applies Ye et al.'s dual clip, which bounds the surrogate from below at \(3\hat{A}_t\) when the advantage is negative [5], as Chapter 5 covered.

Decoupling keeps stale rows in the update. If the clip were centred on \(\mu\), a stale row would start the update with its ratio already away from one, and possibly outside the clip band before any learning happened. It would then contribute no gradient when its advantage pushes further in the direction it has already drifted, and an unclipped gradient when its advantage pushes back. The plan for this trainer estimates that about 15% of rows would sit outside the band under its staleness. Centring the clip on \(\pi_{\text{prox}}\) gives every row a full trust region and moves the stale part of the gap into \(w_t\), where the weight corrects it.

8. V-trace also corrects the critic

IMPALA separates acting from learning. Hundreds of actors play with whatever weights they last fetched and send trajectory segments of a fixed length to a learner [6], so its authors faced the staleness of Figure 6.1c with lags of several updates. They corrected both the policy gradient and the critic's target. The critic target, V-trace, is an n-step target in which each TD error is weighted by truncated ratios. In this chapter's notation, with truncated weights \(\tilde\rho_t = \min(\bar\rho, \rho_t)\) and \(c_i = \min(\bar c, \rho_i)\),

\begin{equation} v_j = V_\phi(s_j) + \sum_{t=j}^{j+n-1} \gamma^{t-j} \Big( \prod_{i=j}^{t-1} c_i \Big)\, \tilde\rho_t\, \delta_t, \qquad \delta_t = r_t + \gamma V_\phi(s_{t+1}) - V_\phi(s_t). \label{eq:vtrace} \end{equation}

Here \(V_\phi\) is the learned critic, \(\delta_t\) is the TD residual from Chapter 4, and the empty product at \(t = j\) is one. The target can be computed backwards in one pass:

\begin{equation} v_j = V_\phi(s_j) + \tilde\rho_j\, \delta_j + \gamma\, c_j \big( v_{j+1} - V_\phi(s_{j+1}) \big). \label{eq:vtracerec} \end{equation}

When \(\mu = \pi\) and both truncation levels are at least one, every weight is one and \(v_j\) is the ordinary n-step return. The two truncation levels do different jobs, and IMPALA's analysis separates them exactly. \(\bar\rho\) decides which value function the target converges to. In the tabular case the fixed point is the value of

\begin{equation} \pi_{\bar\rho}(a \mid s) = \frac{\min\big(\bar\rho\, \mu(a \mid s),\, \pi(a \mid s)\big)}{\sum_b \min\big(\bar\rho\, \mu(b \mid s),\, \pi(b \mid s)\big)}, \label{eq:vtracefix} \end{equation}

the same capped mixture as Equation \eqref{eq:clampexp}, normalised. It is \(\pi\) when \(\bar\rho = \infty\) and approaches \(\mu\) as \(\bar\rho \to 0\). The product of \(c_i\) controls only how far back a TD error reaches, and so the variance and the speed of learning, not the fixed point. The policy is updated along

\begin{equation} \tilde\rho_j\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_j \mid s_j)\, \big( r_j + \gamma v_{j+1} - V_\phi(s_j) \big). \label{eq:vtracepg} \end{equation}

Equation \eqref{eq:vtracepg} is Equation \eqref{eq:offpg} with a capped weight and an advantage built from V-trace targets. IMPALA's analysis assumes \(\bar\rho \ge \bar c\). The paper used \(\bar\rho = \bar c = 1\), which did best among \(\bar\rho \in \{1, 10, 100\}\).

IMPALA's ablation includes the method closest to this trainer, a "1-step importance sampling" variant that weights each policy-gradient term by its ratio and leaves the critic uncorrected, with no traces. With the normal small lag it matched V-trace. When the authors added a replay buffer, which made the lag much larger, V-trace won on four of five tasks [6, Table 2]. Spire Mentor's trainer is close to the 1-step variant. It puts weights on the policy term, uses GAE advantages from Chapter 4 with no trace correction, and caps the weight at 2 instead of 1. IMPALA's table suggests that this variant is adequate while the lag stays small, and Section 10 shows the trainer's lag is small, but no ablation in this project tests that inference.

Traces need truncation because \(\prod c_i\) is a product of ratios, and Section 4 showed how products behave. Capping each factor at one makes the product shrink instead of explode. It still decays, and how fast it decays measures how far apart the policies are. Figure 6.7 shows the decay for real policies.

9. Evaluation and training use the weight differently

In off-policy evaluation, inverse-propensity scoring is importance sampling with the logged propensities as \(\mu\), and a doubly robust estimator is importance sampling with a model as control variate [7]. Both ideas reappear in policy optimisation under other names. The critic's baseline is the control variate, and the clamp is weight truncation. The table compares the two uses.

Off-policy evaluation against off-policy correction inside a trainer.

Gap between policiesSet by the question. Often a different policy entirely.Controlled by the trainer: one or a few updates, each held small by PPO's clip.

The last two rows are the practical difference. An evaluation produces one estimate, so it must be correct for any gap. A trainer takes thousands of steps and can afford a biased one, provided the bias points roughly the right way, so it caps the weight, drops the state correction, and keeps the gap small by construction. Learning from someone else's logged play, such as a human's games or a teacher network's, puts you back in the evaluation regime, and Section 11 measures how far one ratio per decision falls short there.

10. How Spire Mentor's trainer applies the correction

Spire Mentor's trainer uses decoupled PPO with the weight capped at 2, as in Equation \eqref{eq:decoupled}. It needs three log-probabilities for each row, and it gets them at three different times.

In Spire Mentor · where \(\mu\) is recorded

The learner process holds the serving network. Each round it takes one pending decision from every environment slot, runs the network on all of them in one batch, samples one action per row from the softmax, and stores that action's log-probability with the row as logp_behav. That value is \(\log \mu(a_t \mid s_t)\) exactly, not an estimate. Each round also records version, the number of weight loads so far, and a row's staleness at training time is the current version minus the row's version.

Before each update, a pre-pass runs the current weights once with no gradient. It computes values for every row in the update's table except rows of dormant runs. For the rows that train in this update it also saves \(\log \pi_{\text{prox}}(a_t \mid s_t)\), which every epoch of the update reuses. The loss then builds the two branches of Figure 6.2c:

In the code, lp is \(\log \pi_\theta\), lpp is \(\log \pi_{\text{prox}}\), and lpb is \(\log \mu\). ratio is \(u_t\), w is \(\min(w_t, 2)\), and a is \(\hat{A}_t\). The mask pi_m selects the rows that the student itself played. The learner's own configuration calls the cap is_cap and passes it to the loss as is_clamp.

In Spire Mentor · the design choices

Where staleness comes from. Combat rows train in the iteration that stored them, so their only lag is the serving lag of Figure 6.1c. Rows for decisions outside combat, such as card rewards and map choices, wait until the run ends or until the run has sent rows for four more iterations. A run that sends no rows does not count towards those four, so a row can wait longer. The plan estimates the staleness of these rows at a mean of 3.7 updates and a maximum of 5. For fresh rows, the campaign log reports that a change which sped up the iteration cut raised mean staleness from 0.85 to 1.0, with a maximum of 1, "as expected".

Choosing workers for low staleness. The pre-registration chose the number of workers by "the best end-to-end rate at staleness ≤ 1", and the campaign log applied that rule to fresh rows. In the test that applied it, 32 and 40 workers both kept fresh staleness at most 1, and 32 was slightly faster, so the run launched with 32. About 1 billion decisions in, it switched to 40. The record does not say why the bar is 1 and not 2.

A rejected alternative. The plan considered plain PPO without the pre-pass, which centres the clip on \(\mu\) and saves one forward pass per update. It dropped that option, estimating that about 15% of rows would start outside the clip band, for about 8% more speed. The plan marks the 15% as an estimate and gives the 8% as approximate, and neither derivation is in the public repository.

Data from a different policy is not corrected. Early in training, teacher networks play some fights and some run decisions, and their log-probabilities become \(\mu\) for those rows. Those rows train the critic and a supervised teacher term, but only rows that the student played enter Equation \eqref{eq:decoupled}, so the PPO term excludes them. The plan states this split without giving a reason. The likely reason is the one from Section 9, that a teacher's policy is too far from the student's for one capped ratio.

How big the weights are in practice. A comment in the learner's configuration compares the spread of the weight from real policy change with the spread from rounding alone, by their 1st and 99th percentiles. Real policy change, measured at a third of the base learning rate, gives 0.87 and 1.10. Rounding alone, measured at learning rate 0, gives 0.986 and 1.014, widening to 0.975 and 1.025 when the serving forward pass is compiled. On a GPU, serving and the update both run in bf16, but the compiled serving pass rounds differently from the update's uncompiled pass.

11. Measurements on real play

The three measurements below start with the real trainer, where the gap between policies is small. They then use two published networks to make the gap large on purpose and find where correction breaks, and end with an off-policy evaluation whose true answer is known.

11.1 In the real trainer the weights stay near one

Measured · the real learner, learning-check mode, CPU · M2 Max

The learner from Chapter 5 trained from scratch with 4 workers of 32 environments each, iterations of 8,192 decisions, two epochs per update, and a small 2-layer, 128-wide network. It stands in for the production run, which launched with 32 workers and ran most of its decisions with 40, used iterations of 131,072 to 1,048,576 decisions, and trained a larger network on GPUs. Over 41 updates, staleness averaged 0.10 updates with a maximum of 3, and fresh rows never lagged by more than 1. In the median update, the 1st and 99th percentiles of the weight were 0.94 and 1.07. The largest weight in any update was 1.59, and the share of weights above the clamp was 0% in every update.

In normal operation, then, the correction is a small reweighting and the clamp never acts. A clamp matters only for a gap larger than this training run produces, and Section 11.2 creates one.

11.2 A large gap breaks the correction

The published Ironclad network A plays 48 seeds, sampling every decision from its softmax, so A is the behaviour policy. That gives 32,233 decisions, about 672 per run, with 6.33 legal action classes on average. At every state the all-character network B is also asked for its probabilities, and the two networks rank the same action first on 81.7% of A's decisions.

The target policies are geometric mixtures,

\[ \log \pi_\varepsilon = \operatorname{log\,softmax}\big((1-\varepsilon) \log \mu + \varepsilon \log \pi_B\big), \]

where \(\varepsilon\) is the step from A towards B, and \(\varepsilon = 1\) is B itself. Here \(\varepsilon\) is a mixing weight, not PPO's clip range. The trainer's intermediate checkpoints are not published, so the mixtures stand in for "A a few updates later", calibrated by KL divergence. At \(\varepsilon = 0.1\), the mean per-state \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu \,\|\, \pi_\varepsilon)\) is 0.0053. The campaign log's per-update KL medians for the production run were about 0.0014 to 0.0036, but they estimate \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\text{prox}} \,\|\, \pi_\theta)\) at the sampled action, a different quantity. Our inference is that \(\varepsilon = 0.1\) corresponds to roughly one to a few updates of staleness. At \(\varepsilon = 1\) the mean KL is 0.51.

At the small gap, \(\varepsilon = 0.1\), the share of per-decision weights above the clamp of 2 is 0.10%, about one decision in a thousand. For B, a very different policy, it is still only 2.19%, about one weight in fifty. The problem shows in the shape of the distribution rather than in the share above 2. Most of B's weights are near one, and a few are tiny or huge, as Figure 6.4 shows.

For single decisions, the effective sample size is 99.0% of decisions at \(\varepsilon = 0.1\) and 16.7% for B. Products of ratios lose far more. Figure 6.5 groups each run's decisions into consecutive windows of \(k\) and computes the effective sample size of the window products.

Whole-run weights are the extreme case. At \(\varepsilon = 0.1\) their effective sample size is 7.4% of the runs. The small gap costs almost nothing per decision, but over about 700 decisions it discards most of the runs. For B it is 2.1%.

11.3 Off-policy evaluation misses a known answer

The last measurement is a standard off-policy evaluation, which estimates B's win rate from A's runs alone. The true answer is known, because B also played the same seeds, sampling from its own softmax.

A won 34 of 48 (70.8%) and B won 40 of 48 (83.3%), which is the truth. The plain estimator, Equation \eqref{eq:ois} with per-run weights, says 1.5 × 10−68. It is unbiased, but almost every sample of it is near zero, and the rare huge sample that would correct the mean did not occur in 48 runs. The self-normalised estimator, Equation \eqref{eq:snis}, says 0.0%, because with one effective run it reports that run's outcome, and that run was a loss. A's own win rate, with no correction, is closer to the truth than either estimate.

Measured · 48 seeds of PANEL_A · M2 Max · what was substituted

A is ironclad_e2e_d9000_ema0999 and B is allchar_e2e_d4500_ema0999, run through the simulator's Rust inference rather than the PyTorch module the trainer serves. Both play through the simulator's peek, decide and apply loop. With greedy choices, that loop reproduced the native self-play move for move on the one seed checked. No record of that check is committed, so treat it as unverified. The \(\varepsilon\)-mixtures are not real checkpoints. They stand in for a network a few updates newer than A, calibrated by KL as described above. The 48 truth runs give B's win rate with a standard error of about 5 percentage points. The real-trainer numbers in 11.1 come from a short learning check with a small network, not from the production run.

12. Common confusions

"The importance weight is what makes PPO off-policy." In PPO without decoupling, one ratio \(\pi_\theta / \pi_{\text{old}}\) does two jobs. It is the importance weight from the policy that sampled the data, and it is the variable that the surrogate optimises and clips. With fresh data the weight is one at the first step, so the clip does the visible work, and it is easy to mistake the clip for the correction. The decoupled version splits off the stale part as a separate correction factor, \(\pi_{\text{prox}} / \mu\), which carries no gradient.

"The weight clamp is the same as PPO's clip." They limit different things. The clip limits how far the current parameters may move from \(\pi_{\text{prox}}\) within one update, and the clamp limits how much one stale row may count. A row can be inside the clip band with its weight clamped, or outside the band with its weight unclamped.

"Per-decision weights are an unbiased version of per-run weights." In evaluation, the per-decision estimator of Precup et al. [9] is unbiased because a reward's expectation depends only on the ratios before it [1, §5.9]. The per-decision gradient of Equation \eqref{eq:offpg} is a different object. It drops the state-distribution correction entirely, so it is biased, and the bias is small only because the trainer keeps the gap small.

"High agreement on the top action means the policies are close." A and B choose the same top action on about four decisions in five, yet their per-run weights span hundreds of orders of magnitude. The weight depends on the probabilities rather than the ranking, and on every decision rather than the typical one. Coming from Q-learning you would check greedy agreement, because that is what matters for a greedy policy, but importance sampling uses the whole distribution.

"With enough samples, plain importance sampling always works." It is unbiased, and its variance can be finite and still useless. With \(\sigma^2\) per decision and \(T\) decisions, Equation \eqref{eq:lognormal} says you need about \(e^{T \sigma^2}\) runs before the largest weight stops deciding the estimate. For B over 700 decisions, that number has more than a thousand digits.

13. Suggested experiments

Run every command from the repository root of the tour branch. If you work in a git worktree, put TOUR_VENV=~/git/spire-mentor/sts2-pycli TOUR_WEIGHTS=~/git/spire-mentor/weights before each command.

  1. Reproduce the measurements. Play the 48 runs, rebuild the data, and redraw the figures and numbers in this chapter. The first command took about 11 minutes on an M2 Max with 3 workers.
  2. Test the per-run estimator with more runs. Repeat step 1 with --runs 96. Equation \eqref{eq:lognormal} predicts that the per-run effective sample size for B stays at about one run. Compare the self-normalised estimate of B's win rate with the 48-run value.
  3. Compare your own estimates with the measured values. Write your estimates of seven of this chapter's quantities in predictions.toml. Then run check.py, which measures the seven quantities and scores your estimates. The second command runs the arithmetic tests.
