Off-policy correction

Off-policy correction reweights data that one policy collected so that it averages like data from another policy. A policy-gradient trainer needs it because its rows were always played by a slightly older network than the one it trains. In Spire Mentor, each row carries one weight, the ratio of the two networks' probabilities for the action that was played. That weight is accurate for a small gap and one decision. Over a whole run of about 700 decisions, the same weights become useless.

1. Why the data is stale

Q-learning needs no correction for old data. Its target, \(r_t + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s_{t+1}, a')\), takes the best next action, whatever action the logging policy took next. So a replay buffer of week-old transitions is still valid input. A policy gradient is different. Chapter 5 derived it as an expectation over trajectories drawn from the policy being improved:

\begin{equation} \nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}\Big[ \sum_{t=0}^{T} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\, \hat{A}_t \Big]. \label{eq:pg} \end{equation}

Here \(\tau\) is a trajectory, \(\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\) the probability the current network gives action \(a_t\) in state \(s_t\), and \(\hat{A}_t\) the estimated advantage from Chapter 4. The subscript \(\tau \sim \pi_\theta\) is the condition that matters. The average is correct only when \(\pi_\theta\) itself sampled the actions, with the parameters that the update is about to change. An average over another policy's actions counts each action as often as that policy chose it, not as often as \(\pi_\theta\) would.

No practical trainer meets that condition exactly. Three mechanisms make the data older than the current parameters.

  1. Several gradient steps per batch. PPO splits one batch into minibatches and takes a step on each. After the first step, every remaining row was played by a network that no longer exists. Chapter 5's clipped ratio exists to limit this drift.
  2. Asynchronous play. Simulation and learning run at the same time. While the learner computes update \(k\), the workers keep playing with the weights from before it. Their rows reach update \(k+1\) one version late.
  3. Rows that wait. Some rows cannot train until later rows arrive, because their advantage needs the rest of the run. They train several updates after they were played.

The policy that generated a row is the behaviour policy, written \(\mu(a \mid s)\). In this chapter \(\mu\) is always a policy, never a mean. The policy that you want an expectation under is the target policy. The staleness of a row is the number of weight updates between the network that played it and the network being trained. Figures 6.1a to 6.1c build up where staleness comes from in an asynchronous trainer.

A synchronous trainer would have no stale rows, but it would stop all simulation during every update. An asynchronous trainer keeps the workers busy and accepts the staleness. The importance weight lets it still estimate Equation \eqref{eq:pg} from those rows.

2. Importance sampling reweights one decision

Importance sampling estimates an average under one distribution from samples drawn from another. You may know it from off-policy evaluation, where it scores a target policy from logged data. This section builds it in a form that carries over to gradients, starting with one decision from a real game.

In Spire Mentor · one decision

Seed 170,000,000, act 1, floor 3, turn 4. The Ironclad holds four Strikes (6 damage, cost 1) and Unrelenting (14 damage, cost 2). One Corpse Slug is left, with 4 HP. Either card kills it. The game merges the four identical Strikes into one action, so there are two card choices, plus a potion and End Turn, which both networks ignore.

The published Ironclad network, call it A, plays Strike with probability 0.146 and Unrelenting with 0.854. The all-character network B says 0.506 and 0.494. A plays the run, so A is the behaviour policy \(\mu\). Suppose A samples Unrelenting. To ask what B would have done, you weight the row by \(0.494 / 0.854 = 0.58\). B takes this action less often than A, so the row counts for less. If A had sampled Strike, the weight would be \(0.506 / 0.146 = 3.47\). Strike is a rare choice for A that B makes often, so that row would count for more.

The general identity takes one line. Let \(p\) and \(q\) be two distributions over the same discrete set, and \(f\) any function. Whenever \(q(x) \gt 0\) wherever \(p(x) f(x) \neq 0\),

\begin{equation} \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}[f(x)] = \sum_x p(x)\, f(x) = \sum_x q(x)\, \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\, f(x) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim q}\big[w(x)\, f(x)\big], \qquad w(x) = \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}. \label{eq:is} \end{equation}

The importance weight \(w(x)\) makes samples from the proposal \(q\) average like samples from the target \(p\). The condition on \(q\) is called support. The proposal must be able to produce every outcome that the target gives weight to. In a policy setting, \(p\) is the target policy and \(q\) is \(\mu\). For one decision, the weight is the importance ratio

\begin{equation} \rho_t = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)}{\mu(a_t \mid s_t)}. \label{eq:rho} \end{equation}

Two facts follow. First, the weight has mean one under the behaviour policy: \(\mathbb{E}_{a \sim \mu}[\rho] = \sum_a \mu(a \mid s)\, \pi(a \mid s) / \mu(a \mid s)\), which is \(\sum_a \pi(a \mid s) = 1\). In the example, A's expected weight is \(0.146 \times 3.47 + 0.854 \times 0.58 = 1.00\). The two ignored actions have near-zero probability under both networks, so they add nothing. Second, a softmax never assigns exactly zero, so support always holds in principle. In practice, near-zero probabilities cause the trouble. They produce huge weights, as Section 3 shows.

Given \(n\) independent samples from \(q\), the ordinary importance sampling estimator is the sample mean of Equation \eqref{eq:is}:

\begin{equation} \hat{I}_{\text{IS}} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i\, f(x_i). \label{eq:ois} \end{equation}

It is unbiased. Sutton and Barto call the alternative weighted importance sampling. Statistics texts call it the self-normalised estimator [1, §5.5]:

\begin{equation} \hat{I}_{\text{SN}} = \frac{\sum_{i} w_i\, f(x_i)}{\sum_{i} w_i}. \label{eq:snis} \end{equation}

Division by \(\sum_i w_i\) instead of \(n\) adds a bias that vanishes as \(n\) grows. It also keeps the estimate inside the range of \(f\). Sutton and Barto write \(b\) for the behaviour policy and \(R_{t+1}\) for the reward that this chapter calls \(r_t\). Their \(\rho_{t:T-1}\) is a product of the one-step ratios that this chapter writes \(\rho_t\).

3. The weights cost samples

Importance sampling is unbiased, and it pays for that in variance. To see how much, take \(f \equiv 1\), so that every estimate should equal one. The variance of a single weight is

\begin{equation} \operatorname{Var}_{q}[w] = \mathbb{E}_{q}[w^2] - 1 = \sum_x \frac{p(x)^2}{q(x)} - 1 = \chi^2(p \,\|\, q). \label{eq:chi2} \end{equation}

The right-hand side is the chi-squared divergence of \(p\) from \(q\). Unlike the KL divergence, it squares the probability ratio, so one outcome that \(p\) favours and \(q\) almost never produces can dominate it. In the example, A chooses Strike 15% of the time, and each time the row carries weight 3.47.

One number measures how much a set of weights costs. Suppose we average values \(y_i\) with common variance \(\sigma^2\) using fixed weights \(w_i\). The variance of the weighted mean is

\begin{equation} \operatorname{Var}\Big[\frac{\sum_i w_i y_i}{\sum_i w_i}\Big] = \sigma^2 \frac{\sum_i w_i^2}{\big(\sum_i w_i\big)^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{n_{\text{eff}}}, \qquad n_{\text{eff}} = \frac{\big(\sum_i w_i\big)^2}{\sum_i w_i^2}. \label{eq:ess} \end{equation}

The effective sample size \(n_{\text{eff}}\) is the number of equally weighted samples that would give the same variance. It comes from Kish's correction for unequal survey weights [2]. Equal weights give \(n_{\text{eff}} = n\). One weight that dwarfs the rest gives \(n_{\text{eff}} \approx 1\), however large \(n\) is. Dividing numerator and denominator by \(n^2\) and letting \(n\) grow, the share of the sample that survives is

\begin{equation} \frac{n_{\text{eff}}}{n} \;\to\; \frac{\mathbb{E}_q[w]^2}{\mathbb{E}_q[w^2]} = \frac{1}{1 + \chi^2(p \,\|\, q)}. \label{eq:essshare} \end{equation}

So the chi-squared divergence between the two policies sets exactly how many samples importance sampling loses.

4. Products of ratios lose almost every sample

Off-policy evaluation of a whole run needs the probability of the whole trajectory. The environment's transitions appear in the numerator and the denominator and cancel [1, eq. 5.3], leaving a product of one-step ratios:

\begin{equation} W(\tau) = \frac{\Pr_{\pi}(\tau)}{\Pr_{\mu}(\tau)} = \frac{\prod_{t} \pi(a_t \mid s_t)\, P(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t)}{\prod_{t} \mu(a_t \mid s_t)\, P(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t)} = \prod_{t=0}^{T-1} \rho_t. \label{eq:traj} \end{equation}

The variance of this product grows exponentially with its length. To see why, suppose every state had the same divergence \(\chi^2\). Conditioning on the history and applying Equation \eqref{eq:chi2} one step at a time gives

\begin{equation} \mathbb{E}_{\mu}\Big[\prod_{t=0}^{k-1} \rho_t^2\Big] = \big(1 + \chi^2\big)^{k}, \qquad \frac{n_{\text{eff}}}{n} \to \big(1 + \chi^2\big)^{-k}. \label{eq:prodvar} \end{equation}

Real states differ, so a second derivation fits real data better. The log of the product is a sum, \(\log W = \sum_t \log \rho_t\). A sum of many small, roughly independent terms is close to normal, so \(W\) is close to log-normal. For a log-normal weight with \(\log W \sim \mathcal{N}(m, s^2)\), the requirement \(\mathbb{E}[W] = 1\) forces \(m = -s^2/2\), and \(\mathbb{E}[W^2] = e^{s^2}\). With \(s^2 = k\, \sigma^2\), where \(\sigma^2 = \operatorname{Var}_\mu[\log \rho]\) is the variance of one decision's log ratio,

\begin{equation} \frac{n_{\text{eff}}}{n} \approx e^{-k \sigma^2}, \qquad \sigma^2 \approx 2\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu \,\|\, \pi) \text{ for a small gap.} \label{eq:lognormal} \end{equation}

The second relation comes from a second-order expansion. Write \(\delta = \log \rho\). The constraint \(\mathbb{E}_\mu[e^{\delta}] = 1\) gives \(\mathbb{E}[\delta] \approx -\tfrac12 \mathbb{E}[\delta^2]\), and \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu \,\|\, \pi) = -\mathbb{E}_\mu[\delta] \approx \tfrac12 \operatorname{Var}[\delta]\). By Equation \eqref{eq:lognormal}, the surviving share of a product of \(k\) ratios decays like \(e^{-2k\,\mathrm{KL}}\). A per-decision KL of 0.003 costs almost nothing for one decision. After 700 decisions it leaves \(e^{-4.2} \approx 1.5\%\) of the sample, and a run of this game is about 700 decisions.

Section 11 measures this on real play. Network A plays 48 runs, and the target is network B. The per-run weights \(\prod_t \rho_t\) span 185.2 orders of magnitude, from 10−243.0 to 10−57.7. The effective sample size is 2.1% of 48 runs, which is one run. The largest weight holds 100% of the total. The other 47 runs contribute nothing to the estimate.

5. A gradient can afford only the action correction

Off-policy evaluation estimates a number, \(J(\pi)\). A trainer needs a direction, \(\nabla_\theta J\). The weight is the same ratio in both cases. In a gradient, the trainer can afford to correct only one of the two places where the policy enters.

To find those two places, write Equation \eqref{eq:pg} state by state. With \(\gamma = 1\) and episodes that end, let \(d_\pi(s) = \sum_t \Pr_\pi(s_t = s)\) be the expected number of visits to \(s\) in one episode. Collecting the terms of the sum by state gives

\begin{equation} \nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \sum_s d_{\pi_\theta}(s) \sum_{a} \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\, A^{\pi_\theta}(s, a). \label{eq:pgstate} \end{equation}

This is the policy gradient theorem in Sutton and Barto's form [1, §13.2], with an equality because we did not normalise \(d\). Two distributions appear. One is the state visits \(d_{\pi_\theta}\), and the other is the action choice \(\pi_\theta(a \mid s)\). An importance weight on the inner sum is exact and cheap:

\begin{equation} \sum_{a} \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\, g(s,a) = \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \mu(\cdot \mid s)}\big[\rho(s,a)\, g(s,a)\big], \qquad g(s,a) = \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\, A^{\pi_\theta}(s,a). \label{eq:actionis} \end{equation}

It costs one ratio, with the variance of Equation \eqref{eq:chi2} for one decision. Correcting the outer sum would need \(d_{\pi_\theta}(s) / d_\mu(s)\), which in the worst case is the product of every ratio before \(s\), as in Equation \eqref{eq:traj}. Practical off-policy actor-critic methods skip that factor and use states as the behaviour policy visited them [3]:

\begin{equation} \hat{g} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{(s_t, a_t) \sim \mu} \rho_t\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\, \hat{A}_t. \label{eq:offpg} \end{equation}

Equation \eqref{eq:offpg} is the per-decision correction that a trainer can afford. It has two biases. It ignores the shift in the state distribution. Also, \(\hat{A}_t\) comes from a critic trained on \(\mu\)'s data, so it estimates an advantage closer to \(\mu\)'s than to \(\pi_\theta\)'s. Both biases shrink as \(\pi_\theta\) approaches \(\mu\). The trust-region results of Chapter 5 bound the first: when the per-state divergence is small, \(d_{\pi_\theta}\) and \(d_\mu\) are close. That is why the correction works for data a few updates old and fails for data from a different policy.

The weight also multiplies a different quantity than in evaluation. In evaluation it multiplies a return, which is a number fixed by what happened. Here it multiplies a score function times an advantage, and the advantage already has a baseline subtracted, as Chapter 4 showed. A large weight on a row with \(\hat{A}_t \approx 0\) does little harm. A large weight on a row with a big advantage moves the parameters a long way on the evidence of one decision.

6. A cap on the weight trades bias for variance

The standard remedy for heavy-tailed weights is to cap them. Replace \(\rho\) by \(\min(\rho, c)\) for a constant \(c \geq 1\). The expectation of any function under the capped weight is again a sum over actions:

\begin{equation} \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \mu}\big[\min(\rho, c)\, g(s,a)\big] = \sum_a \min\big(\pi(a \mid s),\, c\, \mu(a \mid s)\big)\, g(s,a). \label{eq:clampexp} \end{equation}

The cap leaves alone every action that \(\mu\) plays at least \(1/c\) times as often as \(\pi\). It cuts the others to \(c\,\mu(a \mid s)\). The lost probability mass in state \(s\) is

\begin{equation} \ell_c(s) = 1 - \sum_a \min\big(\pi(a \mid s),\, c\, \mu(a \mid s)\big), \label{eq:lost} \end{equation}

and it measures the bias. The gradient now points towards a mix of \(\pi\) and \(\mu\) that under-weights exactly the actions that \(\pi\) wants more of. In return the variance is bounded, because \(\mathbb{E}_\mu[\min(\rho,c)^2] \le c\, \mathbb{E}_\mu[\min(\rho, c)] \le c\). A smaller \(c\) gives more bias and less variance. In the worked example, a cap of 2 turns B's weight on the Strike from 3.47 into 2, and loses \(0.506 - 2 \times 0.146 = 0.21\) of B's probability for this state.

The cap can sit in two places. A clamp on the weight in front of a gradient term changes which policy the gradient serves. A clamp on the weights inside a product of ratios changes how far back credit flows. V-trace calls the second kind trace cutting. Section 8 separates the two.

Neither cap is PPO's clip. PPO clips the ratio \(\pi_\theta / \pi_{\text{old}}\) between the network being trained and a reference network. Chapter 5 showed that this clip stops the gradient once a step has gone far enough. The importance-weight cap has a different job. It limits the variance of a correction for data from another policy.

7. Decoupled PPO separates correction from the trust region

Standard PPO uses one old policy, \(\pi_{\text{old}}\), for two jobs. It is the behaviour policy that sampled the data, and it is the centre of the trust region. Hilton, Cobbe and Schulman showed that the two jobs can use different policies [4]. The trust region can be centred on any recent policy, which they call the proximal policy \(\pi_{\text{prox}}\). The behaviour policy is whatever played. In Chapter 5's notation, PPO's ratio was \(\pi_\theta / \pi_{\text{old}}\). The decoupled objective splits the full ratio into two factors:

\begin{equation} \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)}{\mu(a_t \mid s_t)} = \underbrace{\frac{\pi_{\text{prox}}(a_t \mid s_t)}{\mu(a_t \mid s_t)}}_{w_t:\ \text{off-policy correction}} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{\text{prox}}(a_t \mid s_t)}}_{r_t:\ \text{trust region}}. \label{eq:split} \end{equation}

Only the second factor is clipped, and only the second factor depends on \(\theta\). The first is a constant per row, computed once before the update. The objective, with this trainer's cap \(c\) on the first factor, is

\begin{equation} L(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(s_t,a_t) \sim \mu}\Big[ \min(w_t, c)\; \min\big( r_t \hat{A}_t,\ \operatorname{clip}(r_t, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\, \hat{A}_t \big) \Big]. \label{eq:decoupled} \end{equation}

Here \(\epsilon\) is PPO's clip range (0.2 in this trainer). Compare with Equation \eqref{eq:offpg}. At \(\theta = \theta_{\text{prox}}\), \(r_t = 1\) and the gradient of \(r_t\) is \(\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta\), so the first step of Equation \eqref{eq:decoupled} is the off-policy gradient with the action correction \(\min(w_t, c)\) and target \(\pi_{\text{prox}}\). Later minibatch steps move \(\theta\) away from \(\pi_{\text{prox}}\), and the clip stops them at \(\pm\epsilon\) in ratio. Hilton et al. state the weight without a cap. The cap at \(c = 2\) is this trainer's choice. Its plan states the value but gives no reason for it. The trainer also applies Ye et al.'s dual clip, which bounds the surrogate from below at \(3\hat{A}_t\) when the advantage is negative [5]. Chapter 5 covers that.

Decoupling keeps stale rows in the update. Suppose instead that the clip were centred on \(\mu\). A stale row would then start the update with \(r_t\) already away from one. The row might sit outside the clip band before any learning happened, and then it would contribute no gradient. The plan for this trainer estimates that about 15% of rows would sit outside the band under its staleness. A clip centred on \(\pi_{\text{prox}}\) gives every row a full trust region. The stale part of the gap goes into \(w_t\), where the weight corrects it.

8. V-trace also corrects the critic

IMPALA separates acting from learning. Hundreds of actors play with whatever weights they last fetched, and they send whole trajectories to a learner [6]. Its authors had the staleness of Figure 6.1c with lags of several updates. They corrected both the policy gradient and the critic's target. Their critic target, V-trace, is an n-step target in which each TD error is weighted by truncated ratios. In this chapter's notation, with truncated weights \(\tilde\rho_t = \min(\bar\rho, \rho_t)\) and \(c_i = \min(\bar c, \rho_i)\),

\begin{equation} v_s = V_\phi(s_s) + \sum_{t=s}^{s+n-1} \gamma^{t-s} \Big( \prod_{i=s}^{t-1} c_i \Big)\, \tilde\rho_t\, \delta_t, \qquad \delta_t = r_t + \gamma V_\phi(s_{t+1}) - V_\phi(s_t). \label{eq:vtrace} \end{equation}

Here \(V_\phi\) is the learned critic and \(\delta_t\) is the TD residual from Chapter 4. The empty product, at \(t = s\), is one. The target can be computed backwards in one pass:

\begin{equation} v_s = V_\phi(s_s) + \tilde\rho_s\, \delta_s + \gamma\, c_s \big( v_{s+1} - V_\phi(s_{s+1}) \big). \label{eq:vtracerec} \end{equation}

When \(\mu = \pi\) and the truncation levels are at least one, every weight is one, and \(v_s\) is the ordinary n-step return. The two truncation levels do different jobs, and IMPALA's analysis makes the split exact. \(\bar\rho\) decides which value function the target converges to. In the tabular case the fixed point is the value of

\begin{equation} \pi_{\bar\rho}(a \mid s) = \frac{\min\big(\bar\rho\, \mu(a \mid s),\, \pi(a \mid s)\big)}{\sum_b \min\big(\bar\rho\, \mu(b \mid s),\, \pi(b \mid s)\big)}, \label{eq:vtracefix} \end{equation}

the same capped mixture as Equation \eqref{eq:clampexp}, normalised. With \(\bar\rho = \infty\) it is \(\pi\). As \(\bar\rho \to 0\) it approaches \(\mu\). The product of \(c_i\) controls only how far back a TD error reaches, and so the variance and the speed of learning, not the fixed point. The policy is updated along

\begin{equation} \tilde\rho_s\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_s \mid s_s)\, \big( r_s + \gamma v_{s+1} - V_\phi(s_s) \big). \label{eq:vtracepg} \end{equation}

This is Equation \eqref{eq:offpg} with a capped weight and an advantage built from V-trace targets. IMPALA used \(\bar\rho = \bar c = 1\) and found it best among \(\bar\rho \in \{1, 10, 100\}\).

IMPALA's ablation includes the method closest to this trainer. They tried a "1-step importance sampling" variant. It weights each policy-gradient term by its ratio and leaves the critic uncorrected, with no traces. With the normal small lag, it matched V-trace. When they added a replay buffer, which made the lag much larger, V-trace won on four of five tasks [6, Table 2]. The trainer in this project is close to the 1-step variant: weights on the policy term, GAE advantages from Chapter 4 with no trace correction, and a cap of 2 instead of 1. It works because its lag is small.

Traces need truncation because \(\prod c_i\) is a product of ratios, and Section 4 showed how products behave. A cap of one on each factor makes the product shrink instead of explode. The trace still decays, and how fast it decays measures how far apart the policies are. Figure 6.7 in the measured section shows this decay for real policies.

9. Evaluation and training use the weight differently

In off-policy evaluation, inverse-propensity scoring is importance sampling with the logged propensities as \(\mu\). A doubly robust estimator is importance sampling with a model as control variate [7]. Both ideas reappear in policy optimisation under other names. The critic's baseline is the control variate. The clamp is weight truncation. The table puts the two uses side by side.

Off-policy evaluation against off-policy correction inside a trainer.

What is ignoredNothing, if the estimator is unbiased.The state-distribution shift, and the critic's lag.

The last two rows hold the practical difference. An evaluation produces one estimate, so it must be correct for any gap. A trainer takes thousands of steps and can afford a biased step, provided the bias points roughly the right way. So the trainer caps the weight, drops the state correction, and keeps the gap small by construction. Learning from someone else's logged play, such as a human's games or a teacher network's, is the off-policy evaluation regime. One ratio per decision cannot cover that gap, as Section 11 measures.

10. How Spire Mentor's trainer applies the correction

Spire Mentor's trainer uses decoupled PPO with the weight capped at 2, as in Equation \eqref{eq:decoupled}. It needs three log-probabilities for each row, and it gets them at three different times.

In Spire Mentor · where \(\mu\) is recorded

The learner process holds the serving network. Each round, it takes one pending decision from every environment slot and runs the network on all of them in one batch. It samples one action per row from the softmax. It then stores that action's log-probability with the row as logp_behav. This value is \(\log \mu(a_t \mid s_t)\) exactly, not an estimate. Each round also records version, the number of weight loads so far. The staleness of a row at training time is the current version minus the row's version.

Before each update, a pre-pass runs the current weights once over the rows that train in this update. It saves \(\log \pi_{\text{prox}}(a_t \mid s_t)\) for each of them, and every epoch of the update reuses those values. The loss then builds the two branches of Figure 6.2c:

The code's names map to this chapter's symbols as follows. lp is \(\log \pi_\theta\), lpp is \(\log \pi_{\text{prox}}\), and lpb is \(\log \mu\). ratio is \(r_t\), w is \(\min(w_t, 2)\), and a is \(\hat{A}_t\). The mask pi_m selects the rows that the student itself played. The learner's own configuration calls the cap is_cap and passes it to the loss as is_clamp.

In Spire Mentor · the design choices

Where staleness comes from. Combat rows train in the iteration that stored them, so their only lag is the serving lag of Figure 6.1c. Rows for decisions outside combat, such as card rewards and map choices, wait until the run ends or until the run has sent rows for four more iterations. A run that sends no rows does not count towards those four, so a row can wait longer. The plan estimates the staleness of these rows at a mean of 3.7 updates and a maximum of 5. The campaign log reports fresh-row staleness of mean 0.85 to 1.0, maximum 1, after a serving change, "as expected".

Choosing workers for low staleness. The pre-registration chose the number of workers by "the best end-to-end rate at staleness ≤ 1", counting fresh rows only. In the run that applied the rule, 32 and 40 workers both kept fresh staleness at most 1. The 32-worker configuration was slightly faster and was chosen. The record does not say why the bar is 1 and not 2.

A rejected alternative. The plan considered plain PPO without the pre-pass. That version centres the clip on \(\mu\) and saves one forward pass per update. The plan dropped it, estimating that about 15% of rows would start outside the clip band, for about 8% more speed. Both numbers are marked as estimates, and their derivation is not in the public repository.

Data from a different policy is not corrected. Early in training, teacher networks play some fights and some run decisions, and their log-probabilities become \(\mu\) for those rows. Those rows train the critic and a supervised teacher term. The PPO term excludes them, because only rows that the student played enter Equation \eqref{eq:decoupled}. The plan states this split but gives no reason for it. The likely reason is Section 9's: a teacher's policy is too far from the student's for one capped ratio.

How big the weights are in practice. A comment in the learner's configuration compares two spreads of the weight, by their 1st and 99th percentiles. Real policy change, measured at a third of the base learning rate, gives 0.87 and 1.10. Rounding alone, measured at learning rate 0, gives 0.986 and 1.014. With the serving forward pass compiled, the rounding spread widens to 0.975 and 1.025. On a GPU, serving and the update both run in bf16, but the compiled serving pass rounds differently from the update's uncompiled pass.

11. Measurements on real play

This section reports three measurements. The first reads the trainer's own diagnostics during a short real training run. The second uses two published networks to make the gap between policies large on purpose, and finds where correction breaks. The third estimates one network's win rate from the other network's runs, where the true answer is known.

11.1 In the real trainer the weights stay near one

Measured · the real learner, learning-check mode, CPU · M2 Max

The learner from Chapter 5 trained from scratch: 4 workers with 32 environments each, iterations of 8,192 decisions, two epochs per update, and a small 2-layer, 128-wide network. This run stands in for the production run, which used 32 workers, iterations of 131,072 to 1,048,576 decisions and a larger network on GPUs. Over 41 updates, staleness averaged 0.10 updates. Its maximum was 3, and fresh rows never lagged by more than 1. The median update had weights between 0.94 and 1.07 at the 1st and 99th percentiles. The largest weight in any update was 1.59, and the share of weights above the clamp was 0% in every update.

So in normal operation the correction is a small reweighting, and the clamp never acts. A clamp matters only for a gap larger than this training run produces. Section 11.2 creates such a gap.

11.2 A large gap breaks the correction

The published Ironclad network A plays 48 seeds, sampling every decision from its softmax, which makes A the behaviour policy. That is 32,233 decisions, about 672 per run, with 6.33 legal action classes on average. At every state, the all-character network B is also asked for its probabilities. The two networks rank the same action first on 81.7% of A's decisions.

The target policies are geometric mixtures,

\[ \log \pi_\varepsilon = \operatorname{log\,softmax}\big((1-\varepsilon) \log \mu + \varepsilon \log \pi_B\big), \]

where \(\varepsilon\) is the step from A towards B. Here \(\varepsilon\) is a mixing weight, not PPO's clip range. \(\varepsilon = 1\) is B itself. The trainer's intermediate checkpoints are not published, so the mixtures stand in for "A a few updates later". They are calibrated by KL divergence. At \(\varepsilon = 0.1\), the mean per-state \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu \,\|\, \pi_\varepsilon)\) is 0.0053. The production run's per-update KL was about 0.0015 to 0.004, so \(\varepsilon = 0.1\) is a little more than one update of staleness. At \(\varepsilon = 1\) the mean KL is 0.51.

At one update of staleness, \(\varepsilon = 0.1\), the share of per-decision weights above the clamp of 2 is 0.10%, about one decision in a thousand. For B it is 2.19%. B is a very different policy, yet only about one weight in fifty exceeds the clamp. The share above 2 does not show the problem. The shape of the distribution does. Most of B's weights are near one, and a few are tiny or huge, as Figure 6.4 shows.

For single decisions, the effective sample size is 99.0% of decisions at \(\varepsilon = 0.1\) and 16.7% for B. Products of ratios lose far more. Figure 6.5 groups each run's decisions into consecutive windows of \(k\) and computes the effective sample size of the window products.

The whole-run weights are the extreme case. At \(\varepsilon = 0.1\) the effective sample size of the per-run weights is 7.4% of the runs. A gap of one update costs almost nothing per decision, but over about 700 decisions it discards most of the runs. For B it is 2.1%.

11.3 Off-policy evaluation misses a known answer

The last measurement is a standard off-policy evaluation. It estimates B's win rate from A's runs alone. The true answer is known, because B also played the same seeds, sampling from its own softmax.

A won 34 of 48 (70.8%). B won 40 of 48 (83.3%), the truth. The plain estimator, Equation \eqref{eq:ois} with per-run weights, says 1.5 × 10−68. It is unbiased, but almost every sample of it is near zero, and the rare huge sample that would correct the mean did not occur in 48 runs. The self-normalised estimator, Equation \eqref{eq:snis}, says 0.0%. With one effective run, it reports that run's outcome, and that run was a loss. A's own win rate, with no correction, is closer to the truth than either estimate.

Measured · 48 seeds of PANEL_A · M2 Max · what was substituted

A is ironclad_e2e_d9000_ema0999 and B is allchar_e2e_d4500_ema0999, run through the simulator's Rust inference. They are not the PyTorch module the trainer serves. Both play through the simulator's peek, decide and apply loop. With greedy choices, that loop reproduces the native self-play move for move on the one seed checked. The \(\varepsilon\)-mixtures stand in for a network a few updates newer than A, calibrated by KL as described above. They are not real checkpoints. The 48 truth runs give B's win rate with a standard error of about 5 percentage points. The real-trainer numbers in 11.1 come from a short learning check with a small network, not from the production run.

12. Common confusions

"The importance weight is what makes PPO off-policy." PPO without decoupling has no weight on the data at all. Its ratio \(\pi_\theta / \pi_{\text{old}}\) is the variable that the surrogate optimises and clips. It is not a correction applied to a fixed estimate. The decoupled version adds a true correction factor, \(\pi_{\text{prox}} / \mu\), which carries no gradient.

"The weight clamp is the same as PPO's clip." They limit different things. The clip limits how far the current parameters may move from \(\pi_{\text{prox}}\) within one update. The clamp limits how much one stale row may count. A row can be inside the clip band with its weight clamped. A row can also be outside the band with its weight unclamped.

"Per-decision weights are an unbiased version of per-run weights." In evaluation, the per-decision estimator of Sutton and Barto §5.9 is unbiased because a reward's expectation depends only on the ratios before it [1, eq. 5.14]. The per-decision gradient of Equation \eqref{eq:offpg} is a different object. It drops the state-distribution correction entirely, and it is biased. Its bias is small only because the trainer keeps the gap small.

"High agreement on the top action means the policies are close." A and B choose the same top action on about four decisions in five. Their per-run weights still span hundreds of orders of magnitude. The weight depends on the probabilities, not the ranking, and on every decision, not the typical one. If you come from Q-learning, greedy agreement is what you check, because it is what matters for a greedy policy. Importance sampling uses the whole distribution.

"With enough samples, plain importance sampling always works." It is unbiased, and its variance can be finite and still useless. With \(\sigma^2\) per decision and \(T\) decisions, Equation \eqref{eq:lognormal} says you need about \(e^{T \sigma^2}\) runs before the largest weight stops deciding the estimate. For B over 700 decisions, that number has more than a thousand digits.

13. Suggested experiments

Run every command from the repository root of the tour branch. If you work in a git worktree, put TOUR_VENV=~/git/spire-mentor/sts2-pycli TOUR_WEIGHTS=~/git/spire-mentor/weights before each command.

  1. Reproduce the measurements. Play the 48 runs, rebuild the data, and redraw the figures and numbers in this chapter. The first command took about 11 minutes on an M2 Max with 3 workers.
  2. Test the per-run estimator with more runs. Repeat step 1 with --runs 96. Equation \eqref{eq:lognormal} predicts that the per-run effective sample size for B stays at about one run. Compare the self-normalised estimate of B's win rate with the 48-run value.
  3. Compare your own estimates with the measured values. Write your estimates of seven of this chapter's quantities in predictions.toml. Then run check.py, which measures the seven quantities and scores your estimates. The second command runs the arithmetic tests.
