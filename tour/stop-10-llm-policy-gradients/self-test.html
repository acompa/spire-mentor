Questions on Chapter 10, Policy gradients on language models.

Recall

  1. Describe a model answering one prompt as a decision process: the state, the action, the transition and the reward. Which feature of the transition lets Ahmadian et al. treat the whole completion as one action, and what kind of problem does that make it?

    Answer

    The first state is the prompt, \(s_0 = x\), and after \(t\) tokens the state is the prompt plus everything written so far, \((x, y_{\lt t})\). An action is one token, picked from the whole vocabulary, which is the same at every state. The transition appends the token, so nothing random happens. The reward is zero after every token and \(v(x, y)\) once the completion ends, with \(\gamma = 1\). Because the transition is deterministic and known, the state is exactly the history of actions. You can therefore treat the whole completion as one action and the problem as a contextual bandit, with the prompt as the context. (Section 2)

  2. An agent writes a tool call, the environment runs the tool and appends its output, and the agent writes again. Why does the loss mask out the tool's output tokens, and what two things change compared with a single completion?

    Answer

    The tool's output becomes part of the state, and later tokens condition on it, but the policy did not sample it. It is not an action, so the trajectory's log-probability sums only over the tokens the model wrote. Two things change. The transition is no longer deterministic, because a tool can return different results. The episode can also be long, with many model turns, which brings back the credit-assignment problem that Spire Mentor's critic exists to solve. (Section 2)

  3. In chapter 5, step \(t\) was weighted by its return \(G_t\). What is \(G_t\) for every token of a completion, and what does that mean for a decisive token next to a filler token? With a reward of 0 or 1 and no baseline, what does the estimate of the gradient reduce to?

    Answer

    All the reward sits at the end and \(\gamma = 1\), so \(G_t = v(x, y)\) for every \(t\), and every token in a completion gets the same weight. Within one completion the estimator cannot tell a decisive token from filler. It learns which tokens matter only by averaging over many completions, in which a token that tends to appear in correct answers collects more positive weight. Without a baseline, wrong answers have \(v = 0\) and drop out. What remains is the log-likelihood gradient of the correct samples:

    \[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i:\,R_i = 1} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y^{(i)} \mid x).\]

    That is supervised fine-tuning on the model's own correct answers, which DeepSeekMath calls Online RFT. If no sample in the group is correct, the estimate is zero. (Section 3)

  4. What is a verifier, and why is a learned reward model not one? Name the two things RLVR changes for the gradient.

    Answer

    A verifier is a function of the prompt and the completion that a program computes without learned parameters. Tülu 3 extracts the final answer of a GSM8K or MATH problem and checks it against the reference, and it checks instruction-following constraints such as a word count. A reward model is trained on human preferences, and the DeepSeek-R1 authors say they avoided neural reward models for reasoning because the policy can learn to exploit them. For the gradient, the reward takes only a few values, usually two, so one number \(p_x\) summarises a prompt's whole reward distribution. The reward also has no measurement noise, so all the variance comes from sampling the completions. (Section 4)

  5. RLOO gives completion \(i\) the mean reward of the other \(n - 1\) completions as its baseline \(b_i\). That baseline is random and changes from sample to sample. Why does it still leave the gradient unbiased?

    Answer

    The baseline \(b_i\) depends only on the other samples, and the samples are drawn independently given \(x\). So \(b_i\) is independent of \(y^{(i)}\), and the expectation of the product splits:

    \[\mathbb{E}\big[ b_i\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y^{(i)} \mid x) \big] = \mathbb{E}[b_i] \cdot \mathbb{E}\big[ \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y^{(i)} \mid x) \big] = \mathbb{E}[b_i] \cdot 0 = 0.\]

    The score has mean zero because \(\sum_y \pi_\theta(y \mid x) = 1\). Only the \(R_i\) term of each weight is left, and each sample contributes \(\nabla_\theta\, p_x\). So RLOO's expectation is exactly \(\nabla_\theta\, p_x\) for any \(n \ge 2\). (Section 5)

  6. Suppose you subtract the group mean \(\bar R\) from each reward and divide by \(n\) instead of using RLOO. What happens to the direction and the scale of the expected gradient? At \(n = 2\), what is the relation between GRPO and RLOO, and how sure is the chapter of it?

    Answer

    RLOO's weight can be rewritten with the group mean:

    \[R_i - b_i = \frac{n}{n-1}\big(R_i - \bar R\big).\]

    The group-mean estimator is therefore \(\frac{n-1}{n}\) times RLOO. Its direction is right, and its scale is short by a factor that depends only on \(n\). The factor is one half at \(n = 2\) and 0.94 at \(n = 16\). At \(n = 2\), with the population standard deviation, GRPO equals RLOO. The only informative group is one correct and one wrong, with \(\bar R = 0.5\), \(s_R = 0.5\) and advantages \(\pm 1\), which are RLOO's weights. The cited papers do not say which standard deviation they use, so the chapter calls this identity unverified for any specific implementation. With the sample standard deviation the advantages are \(\pm 0.71\) instead. (Section 5)

  7. GRPO divides each group's advantages by \(s_R\). Under the chapter's idealisation, what objective does GRPO ascend instead of \(\sum_x p_x\)? Which prompts get the largest weight, what name do Liu et al. give that effect, and what is the status of the derivation?

    Answer

    Replace \(s_R\) by its oracle value \(\sigma_x = \sqrt{p_x(1 - p_x)}\) and take \(n\) large. The expected contribution of prompt \(x\) becomes

    \[\frac{\nabla_\theta\, p_x}{\sqrt{p_x(1-p_x)}} = \nabla_\theta \big( 2 \arcsin \sqrt{p_x} \big),\]

    so GRPO ascends \(\sum_x 2\arcsin\sqrt{p_x}\). A prompt at 50% success gets a weight of 2, one at 3% or 97% gets 5.9, and the weight has no upper limit as \(p_x\) approaches 0 or 1. Liu et al. call this the question-level difficulty bias. The derivation is the chapter's own, not a result stated in the cited papers, and it is an approximation, because the real \(s_R\) is estimated from the same samples it divides. (Section 5)

  8. Read against chapter 5, what three changes turn PPO's clipped surrogate into GRPO? Why did DeepSeekMath's clip have no effect? The KL penalty and the clip both keep the policy close to something. To what, in each case?

    Answer

    The advantage comes from the group statistic instead of GAE. The loss averages over tokens within each completion and then over completions. A KL penalty to a frozen reference replaces the value loss. DeepSeekMath took a single gradient step per batch of samples, so \(\pi_{\mathrm{old}} = \pi_\theta\) when the gradient is taken, every ratio is 1, and the clip does nothing. GRPO is then REINFORCE with a normalised group baseline. The clip keeps \(\pi_\theta\) close to \(\pi_{\mathrm{old}}\), the policy that generated this batch. The KL penalty keeps it close to \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\), a frozen copy from before training. DAPO removes the KL penalty and leaves the clip in place, and DeepSeek-R1-Zero keeps a weight of 0.001 but replaces the reference every 400 steps. (Sections 5, 7 and 10)

  9. Two wrong completions have the same advantage, \(-0.5\). One is 100 tokens long and one is 1,000. Under GRPO's per-completion average, what weight does each token get? What did Liu et al. report this does during training, and how do DAPO and Dr. GRPO aggregate the loss instead?

    Answer

    Each completion's token sum is divided by its own length. Each token of the short answer gets \(-0.005\) and each token of the long answer \(-0.0005\), so the long wrong answer's tokens are pushed down ten times less. Among correct answers, each token of a short answer gets the larger push up. Liu et al. call this the response-level length bias and report that under GRPO the length of incorrect responses keeps growing. DAPO divides by the number of tokens in the batch, which gives every token the same weight. Dr. GRPO divides by a constant \(L\), such as the generation budget, and also drops the division by \(s_R\). (Section 6)

  10. What did Cui et al. find about entropy during GRPO training, and what do they say causes the drop? Why does DAPO blame the upper clip, and what is Clip-Higher?

    Answer

    In Cui et al.'s runs, 73% of the entropy drop and 76% of the performance gain happened in the first 200 of 2,400 steps. Their fit of performance against entropy stops improving once entropy is used up. They trace the drop to the update itself. A step lowers entropy when the tokens the policy already likes also get positive advantages. An entropy bonus was sensitive to its weight, and a KL penalty hurt performance. DAPO points at the clip. With \(\epsilon = 0.2\), a token at probability 0.01 can rise only to 0.012 in one update, while a token at 0.9 can rise to 1.08, which is no limit. Clip-Higher separates the two ends, with \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{low}} = 0.2\) and \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{high}} = 0.28\), so rare tokens with positive advantages can gain more. (Section 6)

  11. The papers dropped the critic for two reasons. Why does neither reason hold for Spire Mentor? Name one mechanism in Spire Mentor that has a counterpart in this chapter.

    Answer

    DeepSeekMath's first reason is cost. PPO's value model is usually a second model of the policy's size. Spire Mentor's critic is one value block with two heads on the shared trunk. The second reason is that the reward arrives only at the last token, which makes a per-token value hard to train. Spire Mentor's floors and bosses pay out during the run, so its value function has intermediate rewards to learn from. A run is also about 667 decisions long, and a group baseline would give all 667 decisions one number, while the critic and GAE give each decision its own baseline. As a counterpart, Spire Mentor normalises advantages per decision type, which reweights the types as GRPO's \(s_R\) reweights prompts. Its rollouts also run ahead of the learner, so its samples are stale, as in asynchronous RL for language models. (Section 8)

  12. Section 9 measured four estimators on a toy problem. What did it show about the baseline's effect on noise, about the group mean, and about GRPO's direction and scale as \(n\) grows?

    Answer

    This was a toy problem, not a language model: eight prompts, each a softmax over eight whole completions. RLOO's inverse SNR was 1.4 times lower than no baseline at \(n = 2\) and 2.9 times lower at \(n = 64\), because the leave-one-out baseline goes from one noisy sample to the mean of 63. The group mean had the same inverse SNR as RLOO at every \(n\), and its scale stayed within 0.002 of \(\frac{n-1}{n}\). GRPO matched RLOO at \(n = 2\). As \(n\) grew its cosine with \(\nabla J\) fell to 0.982 at \(n = 64\), on the way to the oracle value of 0.978, about 12°, and its scale rose to 2.24. How far GRPO turns depends on the success rates, and the chapter measured only these eight. (Section 9)

Calculations

  1. A group of 16 completions has one correct answer, reward 1, and fifteen wrong ones, reward 0. Compute the RLOO weight of the correct completion and of a wrong one. Then compute \(\bar R\), \(s_R\) with the population standard deviation, and GRPO's two advantages. By what factor does GRPO scale this group relative to RLOO?

    Answer

    RLOO: the correct completion's baseline is the mean of fifteen zeros, so its weight is \(1 - 0 = 1\). A wrong completion's baseline is \(\frac{1}{15}\), so its weight is about \(-0.067\).

    GRPO:

    \[\bar R = \tfrac{1}{16} = 0.0625, \qquad s_R = \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{16} \cdot \tfrac{15}{16}} = \tfrac{\sqrt{15}}{16} \approx 0.242.\]

    \[\hat A_{\text{correct}} = \frac{0.9375}{0.242} \approx +3.87, \qquad \hat A_{\text{wrong}} = \frac{-0.0625}{0.242} \approx -0.258.\]

    Both GRPO advantages are 3.87 times the RLOO weights. For a group of 16 with eight correct, GRPO gives \(\pm 1\) and RLOO about \(\pm 0.53\), a factor of 1.88. Measured against that group, the rare success counts about twice as much under GRPO as under RLOO. (Section 5)

  2. A prompt has success rate \(p_x = 0.9\). What share of groups carries no signal at \(n = 4\) and at \(n = 16\)? What does DAPO's dynamic sampling do about it, and what does it cost?

    Answer

    A group carries no signal when all its rewards are equal, with probability \(p_x^{\,n} + (1 - p_x)^n\).

    \[n = 4: \quad 0.9^4 + 0.1^4 = 0.6561 + 0.0001 \approx 0.66.\]

    \[n = 16: \quad 0.9^{16} + 0.1^{16} \approx 0.185 + 0 \approx 0.19.\]

    So 66% of groups are wasted at \(n = 4\) and 19% at \(n = 16\). As training succeeds and prompts move toward \(p_x = 1\), the wasted share grows. Dynamic sampling keeps sampling and drops every group whose accuracy is 0 or 1 until the batch is full of groups with nonzero advantages. The cost is the generation compute spent on the dropped groups. (Section 6)
