RL tour · Chapter 7 of 8

Imitation and warm starts

Imitation learning trains a policy to copy a player that already plays well. Its simplest form, behaviour cloning, is supervised classification. When the copy plays for itself, each mistake moves it to states that the training data never covered. This chapter shows why that costs more than the error rate suggests and how DAgger fixes it. It also shows how a reinforcement learner starts from a copy and then stops copying.

1. Imitation learning copies a teacher

Imitation learning trains a policy from a teacher's actions instead of from reward. The teacher, \(\pi^*\), is anything that already plays well: an older network, a search procedure, or a human. At every state it visits, it names an action. Learning from those actions is supervised learning. The input is the state, the label is the teacher's action, and the loss is cross-entropy. No reward or advantage appears in the loss, and no run has to end before the student learns something.

Spire Mentor's training began this way. The end-to-end Ironclad network first copied two older networks. Then PPO took over, and the weight on the copying term fell to zero. Section 8 describes those phases.

The teacher's signal is much stronger than the signal from reward. Chapter 5's policy gradient raises an action's probability in proportion to its advantage, and the advantage depends on what happened later in the run. At Ascension 10, a network with random weights dies within the first few floors of almost every run. Most of its decisions get credit or blame for a death many decisions later. Reinforcement learning from random weights spends its first millions of decisions learning to survive Act 1.

Copying has one weakness. Suppose a student disagrees with the teacher on a fraction \(\epsilon\) of decisions. Over a run of \(T\) decisions it can pay a cost of order \(\epsilon T^2\), because each mistake moves it to states the teacher never visited. DAgger reduces the cost to order \(u \epsilon T\), where \(u\) bounds the cost of one mistake (Section 4) and \(\epsilon\) is measured on the student's own states. It asks the teacher to label the states that the student visits. Distillation trains the student on the teacher's full probability distribution instead of the teacher's single choice.

Section 9 measures the weakness in this game. The teacher is the published Ironclad network, which wins about four runs in five. A fresh network with the same architecture learns from 100 of the teacher's runs. It picks the teacher's move on nearly three decisions in four, and it wins 1 run in 150.

If you know offline RL, the setting is familiar. A behaviour policy \(\mu\) logged the data, and here \(\mu = \pi^*\). The goal is to copy the logged policy, not to beat it. In this tour, \(\mu\) always means the behaviour policy, never a mean. As in off-policy evaluation, the trouble starts when the deployed policy visits states outside the support of the logged data.

2. Behaviour cloning is classification

Behaviour cloning trains a classifier on the teacher's decisions [1, 2]. Take one decision from a run that the student never trained on. It is floor 1. The Ironclad has 62 HP and the starter deck: five Strikes, four Defends, Bash, and the Ascender's Bane curse. The first fight is won, and the card reward offers Tremble, True Grit, Setup Strike, or skip. The game merges identical options into classes (Chapter 1), so the policy chooses among four classes. The teacher picks Setup Strike, class 2, with probability 0.9998.

The training example is the tokenized state \(s\) with the label \(a^* = 2\). Suppose the student's softmax gives class 2 a probability of 0.25. The loss on this example is \(-\log 0.25 = 1.39\), and a gradient step raises that probability. Behaviour cloning repeats this step over every decision the teacher made. The student in Section 9 trained on 100 teacher runs. At this state it gives Setup Strike 0.80 and Tremble 0.19, so it picks the same card as the teacher. The probabilities in this example and in Section 5's example come from a local probe of these networks. They are not in the committed data, so treat them as unverified until a rerun records them.

In general, let \(d^{\pi}_t(s)\) be the probability that a policy \(\pi\) is in state \(s\) at decision \(t\). Let \(d^{\pi} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1} d^{\pi}_t\) be the state distribution averaged over a run of \(T\) decisions, \(t = 0, \dots, T-1\). The teacher's runs give samples \(s \sim d^{\pi^*}\), each with the teacher's action \(a^*(s)\).

Definition 7.1 · Behaviour cloning

Behaviour cloning fits a policy \(\pi_\theta\) by maximum likelihood on the teacher's actions, at the teacher's states:

\begin{equation} \theta_{\text{BC}} = \arg\min_\theta \; \mathbb{E}_{s \sim d^{\pi^*}} \big[ -\log \pi_\theta\big(a^*(s) \mid s\big) \big] \label{eq:bc} \end{equation}

Here \(\pi_\theta(a \mid s)\) is the softmax of the logits \(z_\theta(s, a)\) over the legal classes \(\mathcal{A}(s)\). The label \(a^*(s)\) is the teacher's greedy choice. Equation \eqref{eq:bc} is the cross-entropy loss of a classifier whose inputs are states from the teacher's runs. Reward does not appear in it.

A teacher network also gives probabilities. When you train on the teacher's distribution instead of its choice, the loss is the cross-entropy between two distributions. It splits into two parts:

\begin{equation} \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi^*(\cdot \mid s)}\big[-\log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\big] = D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\pi^*(\cdot \mid s) \,\|\, \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s)\big) + \mathcal{H}\big[\pi^*(\cdot \mid s)\big] \label{eq:ce-kl} \end{equation}

The left side is the expected negative log-likelihood of the teacher's action. On the right, \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi^* \,\|\, \pi_\theta) = \sum_a \pi^*(a \mid s) \log \frac{\pi^*(a \mid s)}{\pi_\theta(a \mid s)}\) is the forward KL divergence. The second term, \(\mathcal{H}[\pi^*] = -\sum_a \pi^*(a \mid s)\log \pi^*(a \mid s)\), is the teacher's entropy. The entropy does not depend on \(\theta\). So minimising cross-entropy and minimising forward KL give the same student. Section 5 and Spire Mentor's trainer both rely on this identity.

A supervised learner reports its test error. For imitation, the test error is the probability that the student disagrees with the teacher at the teacher's own states:

\begin{equation} \epsilon = \mathbb{E}_{s \sim d^{\pi^*}} \Big[ \Pr_{a \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s)}\big(a \neq a^*(s)\big) \Big] \label{eq:eps} \end{equation}

Here \(\epsilon\) is the per-decision error on held-out teacher states. For a greedy student, \(\epsilon\) is one minus the agreement, the share of decisions on which the student and the teacher pick the same class. The held-out set must contain whole runs that the student never saw, not random decisions. Decisions within a run share the deck, the fight and the HP, so they are strongly correlated. The rule is the same as sampling sessions instead of turns.

A classifier's job ends at its test error. The student's job is to play. Define a cost \(c(s, a) \in [0, 1]\) for each decision. The simplest choice is \(c(s, a) = 1\) when \(a \neq a^*(s)\), so the cost counts mistakes. The expected total cost of a run is

\begin{equation} J(\pi) = \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \mathbb{E}_{s \sim d^{\pi}_t}\big[ C_\pi(s) \big], \qquad C_\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi(\cdot \mid s)}\big[c(s, a)\big] \label{eq:cost} \end{equation}

\(C_\pi(s)\) is the expected one-step cost of following \(\pi\) at \(s\). This chapter follows Ross and Bagnell [3] and writes costs to minimise instead of the tour's rewards. A cost is a reward with its sign flipped and its scale changed. Nothing else changes. Three symbols from the tour's notation table also take new meanings in this chapter. \(\epsilon\) is a per-decision error rate, not PPO's clip range. \(\tau\) is a softmax temperature, not a trajectory. A bare \(\lambda\) in Equation \eqref{eq:tilt} is a teacher weight, not the GAE parameter.

Compare Equation \eqref{eq:cost} with Equation \eqref{eq:eps}. The error is an expectation under the teacher's state distribution \(d^{\pi^*}\). The cost is an expectation under the student's own distribution \(d^{\pi_\theta}_t\). Supervised learning controls the error. The cost is what the student pays when it plays.

3. Errors compound with the square of the run length

Follow the student through a fight that the teacher won. Early on, the student's states look like the teacher's states, and the student mostly agrees with the teacher.

Suppose that at one decision the student plays Bash where the teacher would play Defend, and it takes 12 damage that it did not need to take. This mistake is an illustration, not one recorded in Section 9. From then on, every state differs from the teacher's states in at least one way: the HP is lower. The teacher never visited these states, so the training data contain nothing about them.

A classifier is less reliable away from its training distribution. Its error rate on these states is higher, and each new error moves it further from the data. Pomerleau met the same problem when training a network to steer a car from a person's driving [1]. The person kept the car centred, so the network never saw how to recover after the car drifted.

Levine's CS285 lecture on imitation gives a short derivation of the cost [5]. It simplifies Ross and Bagnell's proof [3]. Assume that the student errs with probability at most \(\epsilon\) at any state the teacher visits. Split the run prefixes into two groups: the prefixes in which the student has made no mistake yet, and all the others.

Step 1: the state distribution is a mixture. The student makes no mistake in its first \(t\) decisions with probability at least \((1-\epsilon)^t\). Along those prefixes it has done exactly what the teacher would do, so its state at \(t\) has approximately the teacher's distribution. Along the other prefixes it is somewhere else, with a distribution \(p^{\text{mistake}}_t\) about which we know nothing:

\begin{equation} d^{\pi_\theta}_t = (1-\epsilon)^t \, d^{\pi^*}_t + \big(1 - (1-\epsilon)^t\big)\, p^{\text{mistake}}_t \label{eq:mixture} \end{equation}

The first weight is the probability of a clean prefix. The second weight covers every other prefix. This step is a simplification. Its per-state error assumption is stronger than Ross and Bagnell need, and conditioning on a clean prefix shifts the state distribution slightly, so the mixture is a sketch, not a proof. Their proof assumes the error rate only on average over the teacher's states. In their proof, the probability of at least one mistake in the first \(t\) decisions is at most the sum of the per-decision error rates. After a mistake, the cost is at most 1 per decision. That argument gives the same \(\epsilon T^2\).

Step 2: the distance between the two distributions grows linearly. Subtract \(d^{\pi^*}_t\) from both sides and take the L1 distance, the sum of absolute differences over states. It is twice the total variation distance:

\begin{equation} \sum_s \big| d^{\pi_\theta}_t(s) - d^{\pi^*}_t(s) \big| = \big(1 - (1-\epsilon)^t\big) \sum_s \big| p^{\text{mistake}}_t(s) - d^{\pi^*}_t(s) \big| \le 2\big(1 - (1-\epsilon)^t\big) \le 2\epsilon t \label{eq:tv} \end{equation}

The middle inequality holds because two probability distributions differ by at most 2 in this norm. The last inequality uses \((1-\epsilon)^t \ge 1 - \epsilon t\), which is Bernoulli's inequality. So the distance between the states the student visits and the states it trained on grows linearly in \(t\).

Step 3: bound the cost at decision \(t\). At teacher states, the student's expected cost is at most \(\epsilon\). At other states, it is at most 1, the largest possible cost. Split the expectation over the two distributions:

\begin{equation} \mathbb{E}_{s \sim d^{\pi_\theta}_t}\big[C_{\pi_\theta}(s)\big] \le \mathbb{E}_{s \sim d^{\pi^*}_t}\big[C_{\pi_\theta}(s)\big] + \sum_s \big| d^{\pi_\theta}_t(s) - d^{\pi^*}_t(s) \big| \cdot \max_s C_{\pi_\theta}(s) \le \epsilon + 2\epsilon t \label{eq:stepcost} \end{equation}

The first term is the error that the classifier makes on its training distribution. The second term is the penalty for being somewhere else. It equals the distance from Equation \eqref{eq:tv} times the worst cost, 1.

Step 4: sum over the run.

\begin{equation} J(\pi_\theta) \le \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} (\epsilon + 2\epsilon t) = \epsilon T + \epsilon T (T-1) = \epsilon T^2 \label{eq:quadratic} \end{equation}

The expected cost is of order \(\epsilon T^2\), not \(\epsilon T\). The extra factor of \(T\) appears because a mistake at decision \(t\) can spoil every decision after it. Ross and Bagnell prove the matching statement for general costs [3]. If the student's expected 0-1 loss under the teacher's distribution is \(\epsilon\), then \(J(\pi_\theta) \le J(\pi^*) + T^2 \epsilon\). They also build a small example, with \(\epsilon \le 1/T\), in which behaviour cloning's cost exceeds the teacher's by order \(T^2\epsilon\). So the bound is tight. No better guarantee holds without more assumptions.

A single unrecoverable mistake reaches the order of the bound. Suppose that from the first mistake on, every decision costs 1. In this game, a card pick that ruins the deck for the rest of the run is close to this case. The expected cost is then the expected number of decisions from the first mistake on:

\begin{equation} \mathbb{E}[\text{cost}] = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \Big( 1 - (1-\epsilon)^t \Big) \;\approx\; \sum_{t=1}^{T} \epsilon t = \frac{\epsilon T (T+1)}{2} \quad \text{when } \epsilon T \ll 1 \label{eq:tightrope} \end{equation}

Each term is the probability that the first mistake has happened by decision \(t\). For small \(\epsilon T\), that probability is about \(\epsilon t\), and the sum is quadratic in \(T\). When \(\epsilon T\) is much larger than 1, almost every run has gone wrong, and the cost levels off at about \(T\).

A survival model states the same result in terms of wins. Slay the Spire scores a run once, at the end, so a cost for each decision is an abstraction. Suppose that a fraction \(\epsilon_f\) of the decisions on the teacher's path are fatal mistakes. A fatal mistake loses the run, with no recovery. A run of \(T\) decisions then survives with probability

\begin{equation} \Pr(\text{no fatal mistake}) = (1 - \epsilon_f)^{T} \approx e^{-\epsilon_f T} \label{eq:survival} \end{equation}

The teacher's runs here last about 660 decisions. With \(\epsilon_f = 0.5\%\), \(e^{-3.3} \approx 4\%\) of runs survive. A classifier that avoids a fatal mistake on 99.5% of its decisions still loses almost every run. Agreement averages a few fatal errors with many harmless ones, and the fatal errors decide the win rate.

Section 9 measures this gap. A fresh network trained on 100 of the teacher's runs agrees with the teacher on 72.8% of held-out decisions. It wins 0.7% of 150 runs, and the teacher wins 82.7%. Its mean floor is 17.0, against the teacher's 45.1.

4. DAgger labels the learner's own states

The quadratic term comes from one mismatch. The student trains under \(d^{\pi^*}\) and plays under \(d^{\pi_\theta}\). The direct fix is to train under the distribution that the student visits. That distribution depends on the student, so it changes as the student learns. DAgger (Dataset Aggregation), by Ross, Gordon and Bagnell, handles the changing distribution with a loop [4]:

  1. Start from any policy \(\hat\pi_1\) and an empty dataset \(\mathcal{D}\).
  2. For \(i = 1, 2, \dots, N\), run the mixture policy \(\pi_i\) below and record the states it visits.
  3. Ask the teacher what it would do in each of those states, and add the labelled states to \(\mathcal{D}\).
  4. Train \(\hat\pi_{i+1}\) on all of \(\mathcal{D}\).

Behaviour cloning is the first round alone, with \(\beta_1 = 1\). The teacher plays, and its states and actions become the dataset.

DAgger then lets the trained student play. The student's runs reach states that the teacher's runs did not.

The teacher labels those states, and the labelled rows join the dataset. The next student trains on the larger dataset.

The policy that collects data in round \(i\) mixes the teacher and the student:

\begin{equation} \pi_i = \beta_i \, \pi^* + (1 - \beta_i)\, \hat\pi_i \label{eq:mix} \end{equation}

With probability \(\beta_i\) the teacher acts. Otherwise the current student acts. Ross and colleagues require only that the average of \(\beta_i\) goes to zero. They report that the simplest choice, \(\beta_1 = 1\) and \(\beta_i = 0\) afterwards, often works best. In both cases, the action taken decides only which states are visited. The labels always come from the teacher:

\begin{equation} \mathcal{D} \leftarrow \mathcal{D} \,\cup\, \big\{ \big(s, a^*(s)\big) \;:\; s \sim d^{\pi_i} \big\} \label{eq:aggregate} \end{equation}

The dataset grows with every round. Each round's states come from a policy close to the current student. After enough rounds, the training distribution is close to the distribution of the policy being trained. Equation \eqref{eq:tv} measured the distance between those two distributions, and DAgger drives that distance down.

The bound becomes linear. The proof uses the performance difference lemma, in the finite-horizon cost form that Ross, Gordon and Bagnell use to prove their Theorem 2.2 [4]. The lemma swaps the student in for the teacher one decision at a time and adds up the changes. Let \(Q^{\pi^*}_t(s, a)\) be the expected cost-to-go when you take \(a\) at \(s\) at decision \(t\) and then follow the teacher. Let \(V^{\pi^*}_t(s) = Q^{\pi^*}_t(s, a^*(s))\). Then, for a deterministic student,

\begin{equation} J(\hat\pi) - J(\pi^*) = \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \mathbb{E}_{s \sim d^{\hat\pi}_t} \Big[ Q^{\pi^*}_t\big(s, \hat\pi(s)\big) - V^{\pi^*}_t(s) \Big] \label{eq:pdl} \end{equation}

Read the sum one decision at a time. At each state that the student reaches, the bracket asks how much worse it is to take the student's action once and then give control to the teacher. The sum telescopes. Each step's "then follow the teacher" term cancels the next step's baseline, so the terms add up exactly. The expectation is under the student's own distribution, \(d^{\hat\pi}_t\). The bracket is the teacher's advantage of the student's action, written for costs. It is zero wherever the student and the teacher agree.

Now assume that a wrong action raises the teacher's cost-to-go by at most \(u\), so that \(Q^{\pi^*}_t(s, a) - V^{\pi^*}_t(s) \le u\) for all \(s\), \(a\) and \(t\). Then

\begin{equation} J(\hat\pi) - J(\pi^*) \le u \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \Pr_{s \sim d^{\hat\pi}_t}\big(\hat\pi(s) \neq a^*(s)\big) = u \, T \, \epsilon_{\text{own}} \label{eq:linear} \end{equation}

Here \(\epsilon_{\text{own}}\) is the disagreement rate at the student's own states. DAgger trains on exactly these states.

Ross, Gordon and Bagnell prove the DAgger version as their Theorem 3.2 [4]. The theorem has four conditions. The training loss is strongly convex and bounded, and it is an upper bound on the 0-1 loss. The mixing weight \(\beta_i\) decays at least geometrically. Each round has unlimited samples. The number of rounds is of order \(uT\). Under these conditions, at least one of the trained policies \(\hat\pi_1, \dots, \hat\pi_N\) satisfies \(J(\hat\pi) \le J(\pi^*) + u T \epsilon_N + O(1)\). Here \(\epsilon_N\) is the error of the best single policy in hindsight on the states that the learners visited. The guarantee holds for some policy in the sequence, not necessarily the last one. In practice you keep the policy that does best on validation runs. Their finite-sample versions have the same form.

Two quantities set the bound: the error on the learner's own states, and \(u\), the cost of a single mistake. In a game where one bad card choice can lose a run, \(u\) is large, and even the linear bound is loose.

DAgger needs the teacher during training. The teacher must label arbitrary states, not only play its own runs. A human teacher makes that expensive. A teacher network labels a state at the cost of one forward pass. So when the teacher is a model, DAgger-style relabelling is the default.

5. Distillation trains on the teacher's whole distribution

Distillation trains the student on the teacher's full probability distribution instead of the teacher's single choice [6]. The loss is the cross-entropy of Equation \eqref{eq:ce-kl}, which equals the forward KL up to a constant.

Three floors after the Setup Strike decision, the same run reaches another card reward: Shockwave, Restlessness, Ultimate Strike, or skip. The teacher gives Shockwave 0.86 and skip 0.14. It gives each of the two other cards less than one in ten million. A hard label keeps only "Shockwave". The label discards two facts. Skipping was a reasonable second choice, and both other cards were firmly ruled out. The 100-run student spreads its probability almost evenly. It gives Shockwave 0.25, Restlessness 0.25, Ultimate Strike 0.32 and skip 0.18, so it picks Ultimate Strike, a card that the teacher rules out.

The gradient of the loss shows what soft labels change. Take one state, the softmax over legal classes, and any target distribution \(q\):

\begin{equation} \frac{\partial}{\partial z_\theta(s, a)} \Big( -\sum_{a' \in \mathcal{A}(s)} q(a') \log \pi_\theta(a' \mid s) \Big) = \pi_\theta(a \mid s) - q(a) \label{eq:grad} \end{equation}

For hard labels, \(q\) is one-hot on \(a^*\). The gradient pushes the chosen class's logit up until its probability reaches 1. It pushes every other class down in proportion to that class's current probability. For soft labels, \(q = \pi^*(\cdot \mid s)\). The gradient is zero exactly when the student matches the teacher. It keeps the runner-up above the clear blunder, and a confident teacher pushes harder than an unsure one. Every example carries information about all the classes, not about one class.

Hinton, Vinyals and Dean also soften the teacher with a temperature \(\tau\) [6]:

\begin{equation} q_\tau(a \mid s) = \frac{\exp\big(z^*(s, a)/\tau\big)}{\sum_{a' \in \mathcal{A}(s)} \exp\big(z^*(s, a')/\tau\big)} \label{eq:temp} \end{equation}

Here \(z^*\) are the teacher's logits. With \(\tau > 1\) the small probabilities grow, so the student also learns the ranking among the unlikely classes. With \(\tau = 1\) you recover the teacher's own policy. Hinton and colleagues apply the same temperature to the student's softmax during training, and they multiply the soft-target gradient by \(\tau^2\) to keep its scale. After training, the student plays at \(\tau = 1\).

Rusu and colleagues compared three distillation losses for Atari policies, all trained on the teacher's own play [7]. The losses were a hard-label negative log-likelihood, a squared error on Q-values, and a KL divergence with a temperature. The KL loss gave the best students. Their teacher was a Q-network, and the best temperature was low, \(\tau = 0.01\). A low temperature sharpens Q-values into a peaked distribution. So the temperature adjusts for the scale of the teacher's outputs, and it has no single correct value. Language-model post-training uses the same loss. Distilling a large model into a small one with token-level KL is Equation \eqref{eq:ce-kl} with tokens as actions.

Soft labels change what the student learns at each state. They do not change which states the student sees. A student distilled on the teacher's runs still meets distribution shift when it plays. The two fixes combine. Run the student, and score its states with the teacher's full distribution. Agarwal and colleagues call this on-policy distillation for language models, and they connect it to DAgger explicitly [8]. Spire Mentor's trainer does the same during reinforcement learning (Section 8).

6. A warm start begins reinforcement learning from a clone

A warm start uses a cloned policy as the initial policy for reinforcement learning. Imitation alone can at best match the teacher. To beat the teacher, the student needs reward again. A warm start gives PPO a player that already survives into later floors, so PPO trains on informative states from its first update.

AlphaGo is the best-known example [9]. A policy network trained by supervised learning on expert moves predicted the expert's move 57% of the time. Policy-gradient self-play started from that network. The resulting policy won more than 80% of games against the supervised one. The full system still used the supervised policy to guide its search, because the supervised policy worked better there.

A plain warm start copies the weights and then runs PPO. In practice the clone can get worse at the start of RL. Its critic has learned nothing yet, so its first advantages are noise. No term in the loss keeps the policy near the clone. The usual remedy keeps the teacher in the loss for a while. Kickstarting adds a distillation term to the RL loss [10]. The term is computed on the student's own states, and its weight falls to zero. In the tour's notation, the policy update minimises

\begin{equation} L(\theta) = L^{\text{PPO}}(\theta) + \lambda_{\text{KS}}(n) \; \mathbb{E}_{s \sim d^{\pi_\theta}} \Big[ D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\pi^*(\cdot \mid s) \,\|\, \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s)\big) \Big] \label{eq:kickstart} \end{equation}

Here \(L^{\text{PPO}}\) is Chapter 5's clipped surrogate loss, with its value and entropy terms. The count \(n\) is the number of decisions of training so far. The weight on the teacher term is \(\lambda_{\text{KS}}(n) \ge 0\). The expectation is over the student's own states. So the teacher term is on-policy distillation: DAgger's labels, given as a soft target inside the RL update. Schmitt and colleagues use the cross-entropy instead of the KL. By Equation \eqref{eq:ce-kl}, the two losses differ only by the teacher's entropy, which has zero gradient.

The teacher weight falls to zero because a fixed weight caps the student near the teacher. With a fixed weight \(\lambda\), the objective does not reward the best policy. It rewards a compromise between reward and closeness to the teacher. The compromise is easiest to see for a single state with the reverse KL. Choose the distribution \(\pi\) over actions that maximises \(\sum_a \pi(a) Q(a) - \lambda D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi \,\|\, \pi^*)\), subject to \(\sum_a \pi(a) = 1\). Set the derivative of the Lagrangian to zero:

\begin{equation} \pi_\lambda(a) = \frac{\pi^*(a) \, \exp\big(Q(a)/\lambda\big)}{\sum_{a'} \pi^*(a') \exp\big(Q(a')/\lambda\big)} \label{eq:tilt} \end{equation}

The regularised optimum is the teacher's policy reweighted by \(\exp(Q/\lambda)\), where \(Q(a)\) is the action's value. If the teacher gives an action almost no probability, a fixed \(\lambda\) keeps that action unlikely, however good the action is. Only as \(\lambda \to 0\) does \(\pi_\lambda\) become the greedy policy on \(Q\).

The project's term is the forward KL of Equation \eqref{eq:kickstart}, which has no closed form like Equation \eqref{eq:tilt}. It has the same effect. It penalises the student for giving low probability to any action that the teacher prefers. Schmitt and colleagues measured the effect [10]. With a constant weight, their agents stopped improving near the teacher's score. Weights that fell linearly to zero did better, but only when they fell quickly. Slow ramps ended near the score of training from scratch. The paper's main runs tuned the weight with population-based training. Spire Mentor's teachers win 12% of runs. Staying close to them helps at the start of training and limits the student later. So the weight falls to zero, and after that the student optimises reward alone.

Four failure modes recur in warm-started RL. Spire Mentor's plan has a mechanism for each one. The plan states the purpose of only some of them, so part of this matching is this chapter's reading:

  • Value collapse. The critic starts untrained, so early advantages are noise, and PPO's first updates can undo the clone. The guard trains the critic first, while the teacher acts and the policy stays frozen.
  • Off-policy rows. Rows where the teacher acted were not generated by \(\pi_\theta\). PPO's ratio \(\rho_t\) assumes that \(\mu \approx \pi_\theta\) (Chapter 6). Teacher-acted rows can train the imitation term and the critic. They should not train the policy gradient.
  • Low entropy. A clone trained on hard labels is overconfident. Most of its probability sits on one class, so PPO samples few alternatives and explores little. An entropy bonus pushes the policy towards uniform, against the teacher term, so the two weights need one schedule.
  • Dependence on the teacher. A warm start can hide a broken RL algorithm, because the clone plays well for a while whether or not PPO works. The guard shows separately that the algorithm learns from random weights.

7. Real systems drop four textbook assumptions

The teacher is not the goal. In Sections 2 to 4 the teacher defines success, and the cost counts disagreements. In practice the student is judged by reward, and the teacher is only a starting point. Agreement then becomes a diagnostic. Early in training, high agreement shows that the student learned from the teacher. Late in training, a student that beats the teacher should disagree with it, so agreement misleads.

The teacher cannot always label every state. DAgger assumes a labeller for arbitrary states. A human cannot label millions of them. A network teacher can, but only if it can read the student's states. A teacher built for an older state representation may not.

Labels are soft, not hard. The bound in Section 3 counts 0-1 mistakes. Production imitation uses the KL to the teacher's distribution. The KL also penalises a student that picks the right action with too much confidence, or that ranks the other actions wrongly. Section 9's main students use hard labels, the classic form of behaviour cloning. Two soft-label students are measured separately.

Imitation and RL run at once, not in sequence. The textbook version clones first and fine-tunes second. Kickstarting runs both losses together and moves the weight from one to the other. The student acts from early in training, so the imitation term is computed on the student's own states. The result is DAgger without a separate relabelling loop. The cost is a guarantee. Czarnecki and colleagues show that distillation on the student's own trajectories is not the gradient of any objective [11]. Once reward is added, the student can oscillate instead of converging. Fading the teacher out is one solution. AlphaStar used another [12]. It started RL from its supervised policy and also kept a KL term towards that policy for the whole of training, as a regulariser for exploration.

8. Spire Mentor copied two older networks, then ran PPO

In Spire Mentor · the teachers

The end-to-end Ironclad network, the transformer of Chapter 2, did not start from random weights. Training began from two older networks that played without search. A combat network chose moves in fights, and a run network chose everything outside fights. Together they won about 12% of runs at Ascension 10 on the project's development seeds. They were the teachers.

The training plan gives the reason for a warm start. Play from random weights at Ascension 10 dies at about floor 2.7. The plan also rules out three alternatives: cloning human play, teachers that see hidden information, and running search while the actors sample.

Both teachers are frozen. The learner process runs them on its GPU next to the student. It scores each training row with the teacher of the row's kind: the combat teacher for rows inside a fight, and the run teacher for all other rows. The teacher's class probabilities are stored on the row. One loss term uses them, \(\lambda_{\text{KS}} \cdot D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi^* \,\|\, \pi_\theta)\) from Equation \eqref{eq:kickstart}.

As the decision count \(n\) grows, the schedule changes two numbers. The teacher acting probability \(\beta_{\text{act}}\) is the probability that a teacher, not the student, chooses the moves. The weight \(\lambda_{\text{KS}}\) scales the teacher term. In the first phase only \(\beta_{\text{act}}\) changes, and \(\lambda_{\text{KS}}\) stays at 1.

In U0, \(\beta_{\text{act}}\) falls linearly from 1 to 0 over the first 80 million decisions. The actor is drawn once per fight for combat rows, and once per act for all other rows. A teacher therefore plays a whole fight or a whole act, and no fight mixes two players. The draw is a hash of the seed, the run, and the index of the fight or act, so a replay gives the same draw.

Each row records who acted: the student, the combat teacher or the run teacher. For a teacher-acted row, the stored behaviour log-probabilities are the teacher's. The teacher term uses all rows, at weight \(\lambda_{\text{KS}} = 1\). By Equation \eqref{eq:ce-kl}, that term has the same gradient as cross-entropy to the teacher. The critic also uses all rows. The PPO loss, the entropy bonus and the advantage statistics use student-acted rows only.

U0 resembles DAgger's mixture policy, Equation \eqref{eq:mix}, with \(\beta\) on a schedule. The plan does not mention DAgger, so the comparison is this chapter's. U0 also differs from DAgger in two ways. The actor is drawn per fight or act, not per decision, and the student trains online instead of retraining on an aggregated dataset. At the start the teachers play everything, and the student learns by behaviour cloning. As \(\beta_{\text{act}}\) falls, the student plays more fights and acts, and the teachers still score every row. The project's record does not say why the draw is per fight and not per decision. A consequence is that each fight is one player's coherent play. The plan gives no reason for restricting PPO to student rows. Chapter 6 suggests one. A teacher-acted row was not sampled from \(\pi_\theta\), so its importance ratio can be far from 1.

In U1, from 80 million to 500 million decisions, the student plays everything. The teacher term is now a forward KL on the student's own states, and \(\lambda_{\text{KS}}\) falls linearly from 1 to 0. U1 is kickstarting, Equation \eqref{eq:kickstart}. The plan states the schedule without a reason for its linear shape or its end point. Section 6's argument applies: a fixed weight would keep the student near a 12% player.

Two more phases complete the plan. At the start of U0, the first 3 updates train only the critic, and the policy stays frozen. This phase is V-warm. Its decisions count towards U0's 80 million. After U1, phase U2 runs plain PPO from 500 million decisions to the end, at about 9 billion. The table lists all four phases.

The plan states V-warm without a reason. Section 6's first failure mode explains it. An untrained critic gives noisy advantages, so the critic first learns the returns of the teachers' play before PPO moves the policy.

The two schedules are piecewise linear in the decision count:

\begin{equation} \beta_{\text{act}}(n) = 1 - \min\!\Big(1, \frac{n}{n_{U0}}\Big), \qquad \lambda_{\text{KS}}(n) = 1 - \mathrm{clip}\Big(\frac{n - n_{U0}}{n_{\text{KS}} - n_{U0}},\, 0,\, 1\Big) \label{eq:schedules} \end{equation}

If the weight was held and a later check passes, offset starts the ramp at that decision count. A check that passes before 80 million decisions leaves the ramp where the plan put it. Each variable matches a symbol in Equations \eqref{eq:ce-kl} and \eqref{eq:kickstart}:

  • p_t is \(\pi^*(\cdot \mid s)\), restricted to the row's legal classes by cmask.
  • lp_all is \(\log \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s)\).
  • ce_rows is the cross-entropy, and kl_rows is the KL. Their difference, the sum of xlogy(p_t, p_t), is minus the teacher's entropy, as Equation \eqref{eq:ce-kl} says.
  • ks_w is \(\lambda_{\text{KS}}\) for the row's decision type. A type with no teacher, the potion swap, has a weight of zero.

A row whose teacher probabilities are missing or not finite is left out of the term and counted. A NaN therefore cannot enter the loss.

In Spire Mentor · two guards, and one gate that was waived

The learning check tests PPO without the teacher. Before launch, again at K2, and at every restart, a network with random weights trains for 15 minutes at Ascension 10 on the training machine. It has no teacher, and \(\lambda_{\text{KS}} = 0\). It passes if the mean floor of its last 10,000 finished runs is at least one floor above the mean of its first 10,000. It must also show no non-finite values and an explained variance of the critic on combat rows above 0.1, taken as the median over the second half of the check. On the training machine, the check went from floor 1.96 to floor 14.58 in 7.1 million decisions. The plan states the purpose: the check shows that PPO learns on its own before the teachers fade. Its list of risks names the failure that the check guards against, a teacher that hides a broken PPO path. That failure is Section 6's fourth failure mode. A re-check that fails after an earlier check passed raises an alarm for the operator and leaves the ramp in place.

The entropy bonus waits for the teacher term. A decision type's entropy bonus is zero while its \(\lambda_{\text{KS}}\) is above 0.3. The weight reaches 0.3 at 374 million decisions. After that, the bonus decays geometrically until 4 billion decisions. The plan gives no reason for the gate. Section 6's third failure mode suggests one. While the teacher term holds the student near the teacher's distribution, an entropy bonus would push the student the other way.

The K2 gate checked the warm start, and one of its bars was waived. At about 100 million decisions, the plan checked that the warm start had worked. The student had to come within 8 percentage points of the teachers' win rate. It also had to agree with the teachers on at least 85% of combat decisions and 75% of run decisions. At the read, the student won 16.9% of 1,000 development seeds, against the teachers' 11.5% on the same seeds. The paired gain was 5.4 points. A paired test on those seeds, McNemar's test (Chapter 8), gives \(p = 0.00016\). Run agreement was 0.879. Combat agreement was 0.805, below the 0.85 bar.

The project's owner waived the combat bar and recorded the deviation in the pre-registration. The recorded reason is that the bar stood for "the student learned from the teacher", and the student already outplayed the teacher. A later control ran the teachers through the same evaluation code and measured 12.1%, so the evaluation code did not explain the student's lead. Two differences remained untested. The student's evaluation applies a class's first member, and it turns on an interactive potion swap that the teachers' runs did not use. Agreement had stopped measuring what it was meant to measure, as Section 7 describes.

The rest of the campaign is consistent with the reason for dropping the teacher term, though it does not test it. The student won 27.8% of runs at 500 million decisions, when \(\lambda_{\text{KS}}\) reached zero. The campaign log notes that the jump at 500 million coincides with the end of the teacher term. At 750 million decisions the student won 43.6%. That gain also follows the switch to one PPO epoch per update at about 551 million decisions, so the log cannot separate the two causes. The published network in this chapter is that student after about 9 billion decisions.

The project's first generation of agents also started from behaviour cloning. Its Ironclad clone matched its teacher's choice on 81% of decisions. It won 33.2% of runs, against the teacher's 38.7%. PPO then trained the clone further.

9. Measured: high agreement, almost no wins

The theory makes three predictions that this game can test. A behaviour-cloned student agrees with the teacher on most decisions and still loses almost every run. Its agreement is lower on its own states than on the teacher's states at the same point in a run. Labelling the student's own states helps more than the student's agreement suggests.

Agreement does not predict winning. The 100-run student agrees with the teacher on 72.8% of held-out decisions. It won 1 of 150 runs, and the teacher won 124. Each doubling of the data raised agreement by about 4 points. From 50 to 100 runs, the mean floor rose by 3.8, with a standard error of 0.9, paired by seed. The number of wins did not change.

The disagreements are clear errors, not close calls. The teacher is confident. On held-out states its top class has a mean probability of 0.93, and on 80% of decisions that probability is above 0.9. Where the 100-run student disagrees, the teacher gives the student's choice 0.08 on average. On 19.1% of its own decisions, the student picks a class that the teacher rates below 5%. The Ultimate Strike pick of Section 5 is the same kind of mistake, made on a held-out teacher state, where the rate is 18.5%.

Equation \eqref{eq:survival} turns the win rates into a rate of fatal mistakes. Solve \(e^{-\epsilon_f T} = \text{win}_{\text{student}} / \text{win}_{\text{teacher}}\) with \(T\) = 663 decisions, the teacher's run length. The answer is \(\epsilon_f \approx\) 0.73%, one fatal mistake per 138 decisions. The student makes a clear mistake about 26 times as often. So most clear mistakes are survivable. A small share of them decides the run, and agreement cannot identify which ones. This calculation is a model, not a measurement of which mistakes were fatal. It assumes that fatal mistakes are independent and spread evenly along the run.

Overall averages hide the distribution shift. Overall, the 100-run student agrees with the teacher more on its own states, 73.8%, than on held-out teacher runs, 72.8%. The two averages cover different parts of a run. The student's runs end after about 198 decisions, so its own-state average comes mostly from the opening floors, where agreement is easiest. The teacher's runs last about 663 decisions and reach later floors, where agreement is lower. Restricted to the first 198 decisions of each held-out run, agreement is 81.0%. That value is higher than the 73.8% on the student's own states. Figure 7.5 compares the two at each point in the run.

DAgger improved play more than its agreement shows. The DAgger student trained on the same 100 teacher runs plus one round of 8101 rows from the student's own play, labelled by the teacher. Its held-out agreement is 72.8%, the same as the 100-run student's. On the same 150 seeds, its mean floor is 20.9 against 17.0. The paired gain is 3.9 floors, with a standard error of 0.9. It reaches floor 17 in 41% of runs, against 24%. Its agreement on its own states rose to 75.3%, and its clear-mistake rate fell to 17.5%. Held-out agreement cannot show this gain, because the new rows cover states that the teacher never visits. The DAgger student won 3 runs against 1, too few to compare.

The DAgger comparison has a confound. The round added 8101 rows to 64253, 13% more data. Each doubling of the teacher's data added about 4 floors, and from 50 to 100 runs the gain was 3.8. Suppose floors grow with the logarithm of the data, as these three students suggest. Then 13% more teacher rows is 0.17 of a doubling, worth about 0.6 floors. The DAgger rows added 3.9. The comparison suggests that the location of the new rows, not their number, caused most of the gain, as Section 4 predicts. It is not a controlled test. The control would add the same number of rows from new teacher runs.

Soft labels barely help with this teacher. At 25 runs, the soft-label student agrees on 64.1%, against 64.4% for hard labels. At 50 runs, it agrees on 68.3%, against 67.9%. The mean floor rose slightly. The gain was 0.8 floors at 25 runs, with a standard error of 0.3. At 50 runs it was 1.0, with a standard error of 0.6.

Equation \eqref{eq:grad} explains the small effect. Soft labels add information only where the teacher spreads its probability. This teacher's mean entropy is 0.17 nats per decision. On most decisions its distribution is nearly one-hot, so the soft target is nearly the hard one. The Shockwave decision, with 0.14 on skip, is an exception. A teacher with more spread, such as the visit counts of a search procedure, would give soft labels more information to carry.

The measurements leave the quadratic law untested. They show three things. Agreement and winning diverge. Agreement is lower on the student's own states at the same point in a run. One round of labels on the student's states gives a large floor gain. Testing the quadratic law itself needs a family of students with controlled \(\epsilon\) and runs of controlled length. Each student here comes from one training seed. A different seed would move agreement by an amount not measured here. Win counts below five are too small to compare.

10. Four common confusions

"73% agreement means the student plays 73% as well." Agreement is an average over decisions, and the outcome of a run depends on the few decisions that end it. Equation \eqref{eq:survival} shows how a small rate of fatal mistakes compounds over hundreds of decisions. Which decisions the student gets wrong matters more than how many. One fatal error ends a run, and many disagreements on close calls cost little.

"A held-out test set measures distribution shift." It does not. Held-out runs are still the teacher's runs, so their states are drawn from \(d^{\pi^*}\). They measure how well the classifier generalises across seeds. They do not measure how it performs on the states that it reaches when it plays. Only the student's own runs, labelled by the teacher, measure \(\epsilon\) under \(d^{\pi_\theta}\).

"DAgger is just more data." One DAgger round adds rows, so part of any gain can come from the larger dataset. DAgger's claim is narrower. The new rows come from the learner's distribution, which is where the cost is incurred. Separating the two effects needs a control that adds the same number of teacher rows. Section 9's single round has no such control.

"Agreement should keep rising during reinforcement learning." Agreement should rise only while the teacher is better. Once the student outplays the teacher, the student should disagree where the teacher is wrong. Spire Mentor's K2 read is an example. Combat agreement missed its bar, and the win rate beat the teachers' rate by 5.4 points.

11. Reproduce and extend the measurements

Before you run these commands, set two variables. TOUR_VENV points at a checkout that has the simulator's Python environment. TOUR_WEIGHTS points at the published weights. Run every command from the repository root. Each command uses at most three game processes.

Reproduce the measurements.

  1. Build the datasets and train the students:

    TOUR_VENV=~/git/spire-mentor/sts2-pycli TOUR_WEIGHTS=~/git/spire-mentor/weights tour/run stop-7-imitation/prepare.py --workers 3

    The students are written to out/full/.

  2. Play and score every policy:

    TOUR_VENV=~/git/spire-mentor/sts2-pycli TOUR_WEIGHTS=~/git/spire-mentor/weights tour/run stop-7-imitation/figures.py measure

    This command writes figures.json.

  3. Redraw the figures and fill the numbers into the chapter:

    TOUR_VENV=~/git/spire-mentor/sts2-pycli TOUR_WEIGHTS=~/git/spire-mentor/weights tour/run stop-7-imitation/figures.py render

    All play is greedy and seeded. Training on the GPU may not reproduce bit for bit, so expect close numbers, not identical ones. The tour has not tested how close.

Measure the effect of the training seed. Section 9 used one training seed per student. To measure how much a seed moves the results, change the default of seed in train, in imitation.py. prepare.py skips files that already exist, so move the student files (bc*, soft* and dagger*) out of out/full/. Then run the three steps above again, and compare the new held-out agreement and win rates with Table 7.2.

Test soft labels with more data. In figures.py, set SOFT = (25, 50, 100). Then run the three steps above again. Compare the soft-label student on 100 runs with the hard-label student on 100 runs, for both agreement and mean floor.

Check the pipeline end to end. check.py measures the four hard-label students and compares them with the values in predictions.toml. It refuses to start until every entry in that file is filled in. predictions.sample.toml holds an example, and with TOUR_DEV=1 the script reads that file instead. For a quick check without preparation, run:

TOUR_VENV=~/git/spire-mentor/sts2-pycli TOUR_WEIGHTS=~/git/spire-mentor/weights tour/run stop-7-imitation/check.py --quick --workers 3

The quick plan's numbers show only that the pipeline works.
